Herbert Henry Asquith (1852–1928)

Chancellor of the Exchequer (1905–1908) · Prime Minister (1908–1916) · Leader of the Liberal Party

Chancellor of the Exchequer & Liberal Leadership

Asquith served as Chancellor of the Exchequer in Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman’s Liberal government from 1905 to 1908. During this period he established himself as one of the leading figures of the Liberal ministry, known for his formidable intellect, mastery of parliamentary procedure, and steady temperament. He was closely associated with the “Liberal Imperialist” wing of the party and with Sir Edward Grey’s foreign policy.

As Chancellor, Asquith managed the financial aspects of the Liberal programme of social reform. The Annual Register records that in February 1908 he introduced the Licensing Bill, declaring that “effective control of the licensing laws was long overdue” and arguing for the gradual recovery for the State of its dominion over the liquor monopoly. The Bill encountered fierce opposition and was ultimately rejected by the House of Lords — a confrontation that foreshadowed the great constitutional crisis of 1909–1911.

When Campbell-Bannerman’s health failed in April 1908, Asquith succeeded him. The succession had been anticipated for some time: the King summoned Asquith to Biarritz where the royal party was in residence, a departure from constitutional precedent that The Times criticised as “an inconvenient and dangerous departure from precedent.” Asquith resigned the Chancellorship on 8 April 1908 and was appointed Prime Minister and First Lord of the Treasury. He promoted David Lloyd-George to the Exchequer and Winston Churchill to the Board of Trade, forming what became one of the most formidable Cabinets of the twentieth century.

Premiership (1908–1914): Social Reform & Constitutional Crisis

Social Reform

Asquith’s first premiership was marked by an ambitious programme of social legislation. The Old Age Pensions Bill, inherited from his predecessor’s government, passed into law in 1908, establishing non-contributory pensions for the aged poor. The Coal Mines (Eight Hours) Bill and the Labour Exchanges scheme followed. Asquith himself described the pension scheme as a “great experiment in social organisation.”

At the annual National Liberal Federation meeting in Birmingham in 1908, Asquith gave a major speech defending the Government’s record and outlining the progressive programme ahead. He was increasingly seen as the unifying force holding together the diverse wings of the Liberal Party — from the radical land reformers to the Whiggish aristocratic element. The Annual Register noted that “Mr. Asquith was not popular among the extreme Labour party and the Irish Nationalists” but that he would, as The Times said, “pull the party into line.”

The People’s Budget and the Parliament Act 1909–1911

The great domestic crisis of Asquith’s early premiership centred on the 1909 “People’s Budget” introduced by Lloyd-George. The Budget’s radical tax proposals — including a super-tax on high incomes, land value duties, and increased death duties — provoked a furious reaction from the Unionist-dominated House of Lords. The Lords’ rejection of the Budget in November 1909 was a breach of centuries-old constitutional convention that the Upper House did not veto money bills.

Asquith’s response was decisive. He denounced the peers’ action and secured two general elections in 1910 (January and December) on the constitutional issue of the Lords’ veto power. The Liberals lost their overall majority but retained power with Irish Nationalist and Labour support. The price of that support was a commitment to Irish Home Rule.

The Parliament Act 1911, passed after immense struggle, removed the Lords’ power to veto legislation, reducing it to a two-year delaying power. Asquith had been forced to threaten the creation of sufficient Liberal peers to force the Bill through the Lords — a weapon he used with reluctance but effectiveness. The Act fundamentally altered the British constitution and marked the culmination of the pre-war Liberal ascendancy.

During the protracted budget campaign, Asquith personally took the fight to the country. The Annual Register records him at a great Sheffield meeting in October 1909 where Suffragist attempts to force an entry and “excite a riot” were repelled. He also made a notable speech at the Reform Club in early 1908 defending the Government’s franchise reform plans.

Foreign Policy and Defence

In foreign and defence policy, Asquith continued the Edwardian policy of the entente with France and the naval race with Germany. The Annual Register records that in March 1908 Asquith “refused to let the House of Commons expressly affirm” the two-power naval standard though he “had given a pledge that Germany should not be allowed to pass us.”

Asquith presided over the Committee of Imperial Defence (CID), but his approach to strategic coordination was notably passive. The Politics of Grand Strategy observes that “the C.I.D.’s failure as a coordinating institution mainly reflected Asquith’s personal inclination to postpone troublesome problems until the last possible moment.” He chaired a crucial subcommittee on continental military intervention in 1908–1909, which included Haldane, McKenna, Fisher, and French. The subcommittee’s final report tilted toward a pro-continental policy, though Asquith himself was sceptical about large British military commitments to the Continent.

When the French Premier Clemenceau pressed for a larger British military force in August 1908, Asquith expressed irritation, telling Grey that Clemenceau “completely ignores the existence — from a military point of view — of his Russian ally,” and that he could not conceive of Britain “keeping here a standing army of half or three quarters of a million men, ready to meet the Germans in Belgium.” This statement, The Politics of Grand Strategy notes, “indicates that Asquith viewed the continental problem as a Belgian one” and that he felt six divisions were a sufficient British force.

The secret Anglo-French staff conversations of 1906–1911 were known to Asquith only imperfectly. When Grey finally briefed him fully on their extent in April 1911, it was the first time in three years that the Foreign Secretary had disclosed the full scope of military commitments. Grey’s confession that “what they settled I never knew” suggests a remarkable looseness at the highest levels of government — a looseness Asquith tolerated. When the radical MP William Byles asked in March 1910 whether any secret treaty or convention existed with France, Asquith assured the Commons that none existed — technically correct but carefully evasive regarding the military conversations.

During the Agadir crisis of 1911, Asquith convened a secret defence meeting attended only by Grey, McKenna, Lloyd-George, and Churchill — a decision that caused “a good deal of resentment” among other Cabinet members. By November 1911, Asquith, Grey, and Haldane had resolved to aid France in the event of a German attack. Haldane told Asquith that “if we failed France, he would not give ten years’ purchase for the British Empire.”

The 1909 invasion scare led Asquith to set up a CID subcommittee on the subject. Its report vindicated the Admiralty view that naval supremacy made large-scale invasion impossible — a conclusion that aligned with Hankey’s analysis. In 1911, at Haldane’s instigation, Asquith established a standing CID subcommittee for “the co-ordination of Departmental Action on the outbreak of war.”

Irish Home Rule Crisis

Asquith’s commitment to Irish Home Rule — the price of Nationalist support after the 1910 elections — dominated domestic politics from 1912 to 1914. The Government of Ireland Bill passed the Commons three times under the Parliament Act procedure, but Unionist opposition and Ulster’s armed resistance pushed the country toward civil war. Asquith’s response was characteristically legalistic and conciliatory: he insisted on “self-government for Ireland in regard to purely local affairs” while asserting the supremacy of the Imperial Parliament.

The Annual Register notes that Asquith assured the Commons that “he still believed in self-government for Ireland” and that he could not vote for John Redmond’s resolution as it stood “because it did not explicitly recognise” the need for an amendment protecting the supremacy of Parliament. The compromise offered exclusion of Ulster counties for a period of years, but negotiations collapsed in July 1914. The Home Rule Bill was placed on the statute book simultaneously with the Suspensory Act postponing its operation until after the war.

The Great War (1914–1916)

The Outbreak of War

When the July Crisis erupted in 1914, Asquith’s government moved with reluctance toward intervention. In his historic speech on the outbreak of war, Asquith declared that the German Chancellor’s appeal for British neutrality amounted to “free licence to Germany to annex, if successful, the whole of the French possessions” and treated with contempt “the position of the small States” — Belgium’s neutrality being, to Asquith personally, “a crucial and almost the governing consideration.”

The war united a deeply divided Cabinet. Asquith’s leadership at this juncture — steady, patient, and imperturbable — was widely praised. The Liberals, the Unionist Opposition under Bonar Law, and the Irish Nationalists under Redmond all rallied behind the government.

The War Council and Strategic Decision-Making

Asquith’s constitutional methods proved ill-suited to the demands of total war. The War Council was formed in November 1914 as a substitute for the CID, followed by the Dardanelles Committee (June 1915) and the War Committee (November 1915). Hankey served as Secretary to all these bodies. As Hankey’s biographer notes, the business of the War Committee was “not to be achieved in Asquith’s time as Prime Minister” because the core problem — reducing the size of the Cabinet itself — was never addressed.

The Dardanelles campaign of 1915 was the great strategic failure of Asquith’s wartime premiership. The decision-making process was chaotic: the War Council took decisions without proper staff work, and Asquith’s relaxed chairmanship allowed ministers to pursue contradictory policies simultaneously. Hankey later noted that “the lack of executive authority” in the CID and War Council was “a source of serious weakness,” and that Asquith’s refusal to impose a coherent strategic direction reflected his personal style.

When Fisher resigned as First Sea Lord in May 1915, Asquith played peacemaker — Fisher’s first resignation attempt was “withdrawn when Asquith played the peace-maker” — but could not prevent the final rupture. Asquith failed to keep the King properly informed; George V’s diary records that he knew nothing of Fisher’s resignation until Asquith showed him the Admiral’s ultimatum days later.

The First Coalition (May 1915 – December 1916)

The Fisher-Churchill crisis forced Asquith to reconstruct his government as a Coalition with the Unionists in May 1915. Asquith issued a circular asking Cabinet members to place their resignations in his hands, announcing that “he thought it necessary in the national interest to introduce members of other political parties into the Cabinet.” The new Coalition government, with Bonar Law as Colonial Secretary, Balfour at the Admiralty, and a reconstructed Liberal team, was greeted with relief but proved no more effective in prosecuting the war.

As Prime Minister of the Coalition, Asquith retained his essential style: chairing large, discursive War Committee meetings rather than delegating executive authority. Hankey’s diary records that by early 1916 the War Committee’s unwieldy size continued to be the central problem: “the cure lay, however, not in revising or re-defining the duties of the War Council but in reducing the size of the Cabinet itself; and that was not to be achieved in Asquith’s time as Prime Minister.”

Asquith’s relationship with Lloyd-George, the Minister of Munitions and later Secretary for War, became increasingly strained. Lloyd-George pressed for a smaller, more efficient War Council and for a more energetic direction of the war. Asquith resisted, viewing the existing machinery as constitutionally proper.

The Somme and Mounting Pressure

The Somme offensive of 1916 precipitated a crisis of confidence. Hankey had warned Asquith in writing about the dangers of the planned offensive, but Asquith — despite describing Hankey as “the most useful man in Europe—he has never been wrong” — did not overrule the generals. The explanation, as Hankey told the King, was that Asquith “had a difficult team to drive, and if he forced a showdown with the soldiers he knew that the team would fall to pieces.”

In April 1916, after the fall of Kut-el-Amara and the Easter Rising in Ireland, Asquith temporarily took on the Chief Secretaryship for Ireland himself while continuing as Prime Minister. Though this “showed that he did not lack physical courage,” as Hankey noted, “one may doubt whether it was wise for the Prime Minister to add to his heavy burdens.”

Fall from Power (December 1916)

Asquith’s fall was precipitated by the political crisis of November–December 1916. Lloyd-George, supported by the Unionist leadership and the press barons (particularly Lord Northcliffe of The Times), demanded radical reform of the war direction machinery. Hankey attempted to mediate, proposing that Asquith remain Prime Minister while Lloyd-George chaired a smaller War Committee — “retaining the running of the Cabinet and of Parliament, with, of course, an overall right to oversee and keep in close touch with the War Committee.”

This compromise was “on the point of acceptance” when The Times published a leading article on 4 December 1916 that reopened the rift. The article, written by editor Geoffrey Dawson, was widely attributed (falsely, as later emerged) to Lloyd-George’s influence. Asquith’s wife Margot and his ministerial allies refused to believe Lloyd-George’s denials of prior knowledge.

Asquith demurred at serving under Bonar Law, and after a Buckingham Palace conference on 6 December, the King turned to Lloyd-George. Within 24 hours Lloyd-George had formed a new government. Asquith declined to serve in a subordinate post. The new administration was “principally, though not exclusively, Unionist, and no Liberal member of the late government (except Lloyd George himself) joined it.”

Asquith did not bear his fall philosophically. He entertained “bitter feelings about the Press campaign which had been conducted against him with such malevolence,” as his biographer records. Hankey wrote Asquith a deeply affectionate letter of farewell, praising his “courage, nerve, tact, unswerving straightness, incredible patience, and indomitable perseverance.” Asquith replied that “no Prime Minister has ever been so loyally and efficiently served as I have been by you.”

Front Bench and the Late Election (Belloc and Chesterton)

Hilaire Belloc and Cecil Chesterton, writing The Party System in the dust of the December 1910 general election, treat Asquith as a Front-Bench figure in an arranged contest, not as a subject of private motive. Liberals, they record, sang “One with us is He who leads us, / Asquith, God and right!” — lines that would stir those who pictured him leaving “his plough or his smithy” to lead a stormy democracy against privilege. Conservatives professed fear that John Redmond might buy the Liberal Front Bench with £40,000. On the other poster Lloyd George and Winston Churchill appeared as “patriots confronting a haughty aristocracy.”

The authors’ key is institutional. After the Lords threw out Lloyd George’s Budget, Asquith at the Albert Hall pledged he would neither assume nor retain office without “guarantees” that the veto would be limited. On that pledge he won the January 1910 election, assumed office, retained office, and produced no guarantees. Instead he entered a “conference” with his alleged opponents; both sides then announced that the Conference had failed, and another election was declared necessary, “though, as matter of fact, there was absolutely nothing to vote about.” “The Conference did not fail. It did exactly what it was intended to do. It saved for a moment the life of the moribund Party System.” The so-called Opposition’s refusal to attack the breach of the Albert Hall pledge is offered as evidence that “practically every move in the silly and dangerous game is arranged beforehand by the confederates on the two Front Benches.”

Under “The Governing Group,” ministries fill vacancies by co-option. When Asquith entered the Gladstone Government of 1892 he was “unconnected by any direct tie”; “Now he and his are connected by a dozen such ties.” He and Balfour “belong to the same set”; they married sisters of the same family as Alfred Lyttelton. Enthusiasts still look on Asquith and Churchill as “tribunes of a people rightly struggling to be free,” or blame him for being “too Whiggish,” missing that the net is kinship and Front-Bench alliance, not personal temperament. Drink policy is framed as Balfour’s Licensing Bill versus “Mr Asquith’s.” The book does not analyse Asquith’s mind; it places him inside one close oligarchical corporation of the two Front Benches.

Character and Assessment

Contemporary assessments of Asquith’s character are strikingly consistent. Hankey repeatedly emphasised his “admiration for the Prime Minister’s sturdy imperturbability, as well as for his intellectual brilliance.” Curzon, no political ally, commented on Asquith’s “astonishing physique and equanimity, which he considered to be his greatest asset and an indispensable one.”

The Politics of Grand Strategy describes Asquith as a man who “had perfected the art of indecision and evasion as a positive political asset.” This judgement captures a central paradox: Asquith’s ability to hold together a fractious Liberal coalition through prevarication and patience was a political asset in peacetime but a fatal weakness in war.

Hankey’s final assessment was the most penetrating. Asquith possessed “many of the qualities of greatness,” but his character “was surely flawed by lack of the moral courage to adopt the course in military affairs in which he believed, because of the likely political consequences. And in the end that weakness destroyed both him and the Liberal Party.”

The Dardanelles Commission later proved a bitter postscript. The War Cabinet under Lloyd-George, “obviously delighted because it hits Asquith hard, decided to publish immediately” the report’s criticism of the War Council’s methods. Hankey recorded that Lloyd-George and Curzon were “most vindictive and unpleasant” in their observations about Asquith — “who is a bigger man than any of them.” Hankey himself had “both orally and in writing warned Asquith of the trouble he was laying up for himself by his rather easy and slipshod, or perhaps I should say unsystematic methods.”
