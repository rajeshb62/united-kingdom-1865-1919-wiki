William Ewart Gladstone

William Ewart Gladstone (29 December 1809 – 19 May 1898) was the dominant figure of late Victorian British politics, serving four times as Prime Minister over a span of more than a quarter of a century. No statesman of his time performed a greater part in history, nor won so high a place in the admiration and affection of the living world. A titan of the Liberal Party, Gladstone was at once a great orator, a profound student of history and literature, a philanthropist of the purest order, and a parliamentary combatant of unsurpassed energy and eloquence. His career was the story of a man whose one impassioned desire was “to see the right, and to follow it whithersoever it might lead.”

Early Career & First Ministry (1868–1874)

Gladstone’s rise to the leadership of the Liberal Party came amidst a shifting of the political guard. With the death of Lord Palmerston, the Queen invited Lord Russell to form a government, and Mr. Gladstone became leader of the House of Commons. He and Benjamin Disraeli were now brought directly face to face — one led the House, the other led the Opposition. Gladstone had what Condorcet happily called “an impatient spirit,” and it was to many a problem of deep interest whether his genius would prove equal to the trying task of leadership under circumstances of peculiar difficulty. As McCarthy records, Gladstone’s “admirers were the most extreme of the Liberals,” while he was “distrusted and dreaded by all of Conservative instincts.”

The Great Ministry

When the general election of 1868 returned the Liberals to power with a commanding majority, the Queen sent for Mr. Gladstone. What followed was one of the most remarkable reforming administrations in British history. McCarthy captures the astonishing legislative energy of these years:

“The organising energy which had accomplished such marvels during three or four resplendent years had spent itself and was out of breath.”

The first great measure was the Irish Church Disestablishment Act (1869), which severed the connection between the Anglican Church and the state in Ireland — a long-standing grievance of the Catholic majority. This was followed by the Irish Land Act of 1870, which began the process of reforming the landlord-tenant system that had for centuries been a source of Irish misery.

In the same burst of reform, Mr. Forster’s Education Bill (1870) established a national system of elementary education in England. As McCarthy notes, “The Government undertook a great and a much-needed work when it set about establishing a national system of elementary education” — a system that had been scandalously neglected compared to other European nations.

The Ballot Act of 1872 introduced the secret ballot, a reform of immense consequence. McCarthy observes that the Ballot Act “put an end to an enormous amount of corruption, and it may be said to have almost altogether extinguished the illegitimate influence of the landlord, the employer, and the patron.”

Cardwell’s Army reforms and the Licensing Act of Mr. Bruce were further planks in this extraordinary legislative programme. The Treaty of Washington (1871) and the subsequent Geneva arbitration on the Alabama claims marked a triumph for Gladstone’s commitment to international arbitration over the angry demands of the jingo press.

The Fall

The great ministry met its nemesis in the Irish University Bill of 1873. Gladstone’s gallant effort to reconcile the conflicting claims of Irish education was a measure that satisfied none of the contending parties — Protestants, Catholics, and Nonconformists alike condemned it. As McCarthy movingly records:

“Mr. Gladstone was the first English Prime Minister who had ever really sacrificed place and popularity to serve the interests of Ireland; it seemed a cruel stroke of fate which made his fall from power mainly the result of the Irish vote in the House of Commons.”

The bill was defeated by a majority of three — 287 against 284. Though Disraeli declined to form a government, and Gladstone returned reluctantly to office, the administration’s authority was shattered. In January 1874, Gladstone suddenly dissolved Parliament. The country was taken utterly by surprise, and the result was a Conservative majority of about fifty. Mr. Gladstone resigned at once.

“So sudden a fall from power had not up to that time been known in the modern political history of the country. The great Liberal Administration had fallen as suddenly as the French Empire; had disappeared like Aladdin’s palace.”

In March 1875, Gladstone announced his intention of retiring from the leadership of the Liberal Party. The party reeled under the blow, seeming for a moment not only without a leader, but even without a policy or a motive for existence. McCarthy records the bitter murmurs among Liberals who contrasted his abandonment of them with Disraeli’s unwearying perseverance through long years of opposition.

The Midlothian Campaign & Second Ministry (1880–1885)

The Challenge to Beaconsfield

Gladstone did not remain in retirement. During the Eastern Crisis of 1876, he emerged to denounce the “Bulgarian horrors” committed by the Turks, publishing a pamphlet that sold in tens of thousands and electrified the nation. But it was the Midlothian Campaign of 1879–1880 that transformed British politics. In a series of epic speeches delivered from railway carriages, in great halls, and to vast open-air crowds, Gladstone held the Disraeli government — then led by Lord Beaconsfield — to account for its “imperialist” adventures in Afghanistan, South Africa, and the Near East.

As McCarthy describes, the 1880 general election was a cataclysm for the Conservatives:

“With the very first day of the elections it was evident that the Conservative majority was already gone. Each succeeding day showed more and more the change that had taken place in public feeling. Defeat was turned into disaster. Disaster became utter rout and confusion. When the elections were over it was found that the Conservative party were nowhere. A majority of some hundred and twenty sent the Liberals back into power.”

The Queen sent for Lord Hartington, then for Lord Granville — but everyone knew in advance who was to come into power at last:

“Mr. Gladstone had stood up against Lord Beaconsfield for some years and fought him alone. He had dragged his party after him into many a danger. Now, at last, the battle had been given to his hands, and it was a matter of necessity that the triumph should bring back to power the man whose energy and eloquence had inspired the struggle.”

The Second Gladstone Ministry

Gladstone’s second administration (1880–1885) inherited a host of foreign entanglements from its Conservative predecessors — troubles in Western Asia, in Egypt, and in South Africa. There were wars as yet unfinished, and as McCarthy noted, “some of the men who were most necessary to a strong Liberal Administration were in their hearts utterly opposed to any policy which aimed at the extension of territory.”

The early years were plagued by the Bradlaugh Affair — the atheist MP for Northampton being denied the right to take his seat — which consumed vast amounts of parliamentary time. In Ireland, the agricultural depression and the rise of the Land League under Charles Stewart Parnell created a crisis that demanded action. The Land Act of 1881 granted the “three Fs” (fair rent, fixity of tenure, free sale), but coercion and the imprisonment of Parnell at Kilmainham were followed by the Kilmainham Treaty, a compromise that alarmed the Whig wing of the Liberal Party.

Egypt and the Sudan

The most fateful foreign entanglement was Egypt. In 1882, the Gladstone government bombarded Alexandria and sent Sir Garnet Wolseley to defeat the nationalist forces of Arabi Pasha at Tel el-Kebir, leading to the British occupation of Egypt. As the Langer volume notes, “There can be little or no doubt that Gladstone intended to withdraw again from Egypt as soon as possible” — yet the occupation would last for decades.

The Sudan crisis proved even more disastrous. The government sent General Charles George Gordon to Khartoum to evacuate Egyptian forces from the Sudan, where the Mahdi’s rebellion was sweeping all before it. Gordon instead became besieged at Khartoum. McCarthy vividly describes the agonising delay:

“There was a strange, and to this day an unaccountable, delay about the measures to be taken for Gordon’s rescue. People said that the Government had no hope of being able to do anything to save him; and a Government is in sore straits when people believe that there is something that it ought to do and cannot even try to do.”

The relief expedition under Wolseley arrived two days too late. Khartoum had fallen; Gordon was dead. The news struck England like a thunderbolt. Though the ministry survived a vote of censure, the moral authority of the government was deeply wounded.

The Third Reform Act

Despite these disasters, Gladstone’s government achieved one great constitutional reform. The Franchise Bill of 1884 extended the household suffrage enjoyed by borough voters to the counties, enfranchising millions of agricultural labourers. The Redistribution Act of 1885 redrew constituency boundaries to reflect population. As Gladstone declared when introducing the Franchise Bill:

“I take my stand upon the broad principle that the enfranchisement of capable citizens, be they few or be they many — and if they be many, so much the better — is an addition to the strength of the State.”

The government fell in June 1885 on a budget amendment, narrowly defeated. Lord Salisbury formed a minority Conservative administration.

Home Rule & the Liberal Split (1886)

The general election of 1885 returned a hung parliament, with Parnell’s Irish Nationalists holding the balance of power. Gladstone, by now convinced that justice for Ireland demanded a measure of self-government, became converted to the cause of Home Rule.

His conversion was not sudden. As early as the 1870s, when the Home Rule movement first emerged under Isaac Butt, Gladstone had shown an anxious desire to understand the new agitation. McCarthy records that while many English voices cried out that Home Rule must be “simply brushed away as a nuisance,” Gladstone “was not a man to deal with political questions in that sort of way. He showed an anxiety to understand the new agitation and its objects.”

Gladstone returned to office in February 1886 at the head of his third administration. On 8 April 1886, he introduced the First Home Rule Bill, a measure to create a separate Irish parliament for domestic affairs while reserving imperial questions to Westminster. The Bill split the Liberal Party. Joseph Chamberlain and Lord Hartington — Gladstone’s most formidable colleagues — led the secession of the Liberal Unionists, who allied themselves with the Conservatives in opposing the measure.

The Bill was defeated in the House of Commons on 8 June 1886, with 93 Liberals voting against their own government. McCarthy writes:

“Gladstone’s first Home Rule Bill was rejected by the House of Commons. His second Home Rule Bill passed safely through the House of Commons and was rejected only when it came for the first time into the House of Lords.”

The general election that followed returned a massive Conservative and Liberal Unionist majority. The Liberal Party was shattered. Gladstone resigned, and Lord Salisbury formed his second government. For a decade, the Liberal Party would remain a broken force, divided between Gladstonian Home Rulers and Liberal Unionists.

Final Ministry & Legacy (1892–1894)

Despite his advancing years — he was now in his eighties — Gladstone led the Liberal Party to a narrow victory at the general election of 1892. His fourth and final administration was formed with the support of the Irish Nationalists. The great object was to pass Home Rule once more.

The Second Home Rule Bill was introduced in 1893. It passed the House of Commons after a titanic struggle, but was overwhelmingly rejected by the House of Lords. McCarthy records the significance of this moment:

“Gladstone, in the last speech which he ever delivered to the House of Commons, had dwelt upon this important fact, and had impressed upon the House that the hereditary chamber had done with the Home Rule Bill just what it had done at first with every important measure brought forward in the cause of political advancement. If Gladstone had been but a few years younger, or could have retained his health and strength for a few years more, he would in all human probability have compelled the House of Lords to accept his measure.”

But age and infirmity had finally caught up with the Grand Old Man. He resigned the premiership in March 1894, succeeded by Lord Rosebery. In his retirement, he was not idle. As McCarthy notes, “Again and again some great public question, such as that which was created by the sufferings of the Armenians under Turkish rule, had aroused him into active appeals on behalf of the side for which the claims of justice and mercy were combined.”

Death and Apotheosis

William Ewart Gladstone died at his beloved home, Hawarden Castle, on Ascension Day, 19 May 1898. McCarthy’s narrative of his final hours is poignant:

“On that day William Ewart Gladstone breathed his last. He died in his Hawarden home — passed away from the ever-loving and ever-devoted ministrations of the wife to whom he had been so tenderly attached and who had always been his most trusted and most affectionate companion.”

“When the inevitable came at last and was made known, a profound shock passed through all civilised nations. No statesman of the time had performed a greater part in history than Gladstone had done, or had won so high a place in the admiration and affection of the living world. Every one felt that he had left no equal behind him.”

Both Houses of Parliament gave generous expression to the national grief. In the House of Lords, the Prime Minister, Lord Salisbury — his greatest political opponent — delivered a speech at once eloquent, sincere, and heartfelt, noting “the universal agreement of all persons, classes, and schools of thought in doing honour to the memory of a man who had been engaged more continually in political conflict than probably any other man of his generation.”

Gladstone’s wish for simplicity was respected. He was buried in Westminster Abbey, near the graves of Sir Robert Peel and Lord Beaconsfield. His grave lies close to that of his old rival — the rivals whom the poet might have said were joined in death as “drop upon Fox’s grave a tear, ‘twill trickle to his rival’s bier.”

“Those whose political faith made them followers of Gladstone might have remembered with satisfaction that some of Disraeli’s most memorable successes in legislation were but the carrying out of the policy originated by Gladstone and opposed and for the time even overthrown by his most powerful political opponent.”

Character and Oratory

McCarthy’s voluminous tribute to Gladstone’s personal qualities is worth quoting at length:

“As a Parliamentary orator and debater Gladstone never had a superior in the splendid record of British Parliamentary eloquence. He had a magnificent voice, a splendid delivery, a fluency which never faltered yet never failed to find the most appropriate words, and he showed a rare and happy gift of humour heightened with touches of sarcasm which were none the less successful because they never carried the sting of malignity with them.”

“He was a most delightful talker, and there was hardly a subject in the range of human reading on which he could not converse with readiness and with full appreciation. He was filled, too, with an intense desire to increase his stock of knowledge from every available source.”

The ways and artifices common to statesmanship had never been moving influences with him. “Even his most unyielding political opponents never believed him guilty of adopting any course for the sake of mere party interests or of taking up any cause for the reason that it was popular.”

The Liberal Inheritance

Gladstone’s retirement from public life, followed so soon by his death, left the Liberal Party in a peculiar position. There was no man who could claim to be his rightful successor. As McCarthy observes:

“It must happen from time to time to every great political party to lose a leader of supreme ability whose loss it may appear just then hardly possible to repair.”

The Liberal Party was not only leaderless but divided. Some influential Liberals had formally withdrawn from the party over Home Rule. A section of Liberal opinion believed that “the policy of Home Rule for Ireland lay buried in that statesman’s grave.” Yet McCarthy himself perceived the flaw in this reasoning:

“The reflective and impartial reader may find it hard to understand how this idea came into existence, seeing that Mr. Gladstone’s measure of reform for Ireland had passed through just the same stages of progress which had to be accomplished by almost every great measure of reform for England.”

Historical Assessment

William Ewart Gladstone bestrode the late Victorian world like a colossus. He had been a great statesman, one of the greatest known to modern times; an orator entitled to rank among the highest of all ages; a profound student of history and literature, a lover of poetry and all the arts; a man who understood and appreciated the discoveries and influences of modern science; and a philanthropist of the purest order.

His life was fortunate in its biographer. As McCarthy notes, John Morley’s Life of Gladstone remains “the authentic and authoritative record of that life of unsurpassed activity and on the whole of superb success.” In its pages, “we see how his many-sided nature came to realise the demands of each new claim made upon his attention, and to seek the best way of bringing each cause to full success when once it had secured his adhesion.”

Gladstone was, in the most profound sense, the author of modern Liberalism. The reforms of his first ministry — disestablishment of the Irish Church, the secret ballot, national education, land reform — transformed the relationship between the state and the citizen. The Midlothian Campaign created a new model of popular political engagement. The Home Rule crusade, though it failed in his lifetime, defined the Irish question for a generation.

He passed from the stage leaving behind him the enduring conviction — expressed in the closing passages of his last parliamentary speech — that the cause for which he fought was not dead, but sleeping; that “the hereditary chamber had done with the Home Rule Bill just what it had done at first with every important measure brought forward in the cause of political advancement.”
