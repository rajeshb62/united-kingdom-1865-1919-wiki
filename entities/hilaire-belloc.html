Hilaire Belloc

Hilaire Belloc is the joint author, with Cecil Chesterton, of The Party System (London: Stephen Swift, 1911) and the sole author of The Servile State (first issued October 1912; reprinted February and October 1913, with a preface to the second edition signed from Kings Land, Shipley, Horsham, Sussex). In those two books he presents himself as a polemicist against the working of the British party machine and against the drift of industrial capitalism toward legally compulsory labour. He does not narrate a private life. What he does state, in the first person or as joint author, is the object of each book, the circumstances of its publication, and the political stance from which the argument is written.

Biography

The late election and The Party System

The Party System opens in the dust of the second general election of 1910. Belloc and Chesterton write as men who have just watched both sides proclaim that “the Will of the People must prevail,” with only “slight variations as to the direction” of that will. They record the poster-war in which Lloyd George and Winston Churchill appear on one sheet as patriots confronting a haughty aristocracy (Churchill’s cousin the Duke of Marlborough standing in for the order) and on the other as “low-born demagogues hallooing on their ragged and illiterate associates to the plunder of the wealthy.” Conservatives profess fear that John Redmond “should buy up the whole Liberal Front Bench with the sum of £40,000 (or $200,000, which sounds at once larger and more insidiously wicked)”; Liberals sing “One with us is He who leads us, / Asquith, God and right!” The voters, the authors say, are already looking at one another and wondering what the contest was about [source: belloc-party-system].

Their key to that stage-play is the year since the Lords threw out Lloyd George’s Budget in 1909. Asquith at the Albert Hall pledged that he would neither assume nor retain office without “guarantees” that the veto would be limited. He won the January 1910 election, assumed office, retained office, and produced no guarantees. He entered a “conference” with his alleged opponents; six months were supposed to have been spent bridging the “unbridgable gulf” which “one of his humbler salaried followers discovered, in a notable speech, to exist between the views of his uncle on the one hand, and of his first cousin on the other.” Both sides then announced that the Conference had failed, and another election was declared necessary, “though, as matter of fact, there was absolutely nothing to vote about.” “The Conference did not fail. It did exactly what it was intended to do. It saved for a moment the life of the moribund Party System.” The 1906 mandate had been squandered—Chinese Labour, the Licensing Bill, “the collapse and absorption of the Labour Party”—and “Down with the Lords!” had been raised to rally supporters, then smothered when a handful of Radicals demanded fulfilment [source: belloc-party-system].

The object of the book, they write, is “to expose and ridicule as it deserves, to destroy and to supplant the system under which Parliament, the governing institution of this country, has been rendered null.” Governments abandon causes because they consult “the sense of the Governing Group”; Oppositions tolerate the abandonment because they wish to preserve the system that will, “by the connivance of those rivals,” bring them office in turn. “Practically every move in the silly and dangerous game is arranged beforehand by the confederates on the two Front Benches” [source: belloc-party-system].

Belloc’s argument, as joint author, is that democracy means government by the general will, and that “there is no democracy in England to-day.” Representation requires free selection of representatives, strict responsibility to constituents, and independence of the Executive; none obtain. Ministers nominate themselves and fill vacancies by co-option. The two Front Benches are “one close oligarchical corporation,” bound by kinship, private secretaryships, and make-weights, playing a feud kept up “mainly for the dramatic entertainment of the people of Verona.” The House of Commons has lost control of its time-table; five-sixths or more of time is at the Government’s disposal; the authors’ own motion on the secrecy of the Party Funds was nullified, after consultation with the Opposition Front Bench, by a shelving amendment. Two “huge war-chests,” secretly subscribed and secretly disbursed, buy honours and policies. The Central Office and the caucus select candidates and programmes. Unreal issues, defined by the politicians themselves, give “a semblance of reality to their empty competition.” The authors “decidedly disagree” on Free Trade versus Protection, which they treat as proof that the machine, not conviction, frames the electoral question [source: belloc-party-system].

They reject the defence that the system has made England everything a citizen can desire: conditions “are not good, but bad”; continental peoples regard the English poor with “pity and horror.” Franchise tinkering, payment of members, and auditing of funds will not restore representation. What they propose is exposure and ridicule; pledges inside the machine to vote against whatever Government unless scheduled measures pass by a date; a short, indissoluble Parliament; and, if the people will organise, something like Jowett’s departmental committees. If they will not, “the law-making institution of England … is done with.” Until a successor organ appears, the country faces “one of those evil spaces, subject to foreign insult and domestic misfortune,” when nations “lose grip over their own destinies” [source: belloc-party-system].

Kings Land and The Servile State

The second book is signed, for the second-edition preface, from Kings Land, Shipley, Horsham, Sussex. Belloc will not alter a completed book to meet reviewers. He states three corrections of misconception. First, the argument “bears no relation to the common accusation levelled against Socialists (that is, Collectivists) that life in a Socialist State would be so subject to regulation and order as to be unduly oppressive.” He does “not discuss the Socialist State.” “It is the very heart of my thesis that we are not, as a fact, approaching Socialism at all, but a very different state of society; to wit, a society in which the Capitalist class shall be even more powerful and far more secure than it is at present: a society in which the proletarian mass shall not suffer from particular regulations, oppressive or beneficent, but shall change their status, lose their present legal freedom, and be subject to compulsory labour.” Second, he has not set up the thesis “as a warning or as a piece of gloom.” He says nowhere that “the re-establishment of slavery would be a bad thing as compared with our present insecurity”; the tendency exists “because the new conditions may be found more tolerable than those obtaining under Capitalism.” Third, he has not mistaken professional politicians’ “Social Reform” for Socialism. The Socialist object—“the putting of the means of production into the hands of politicians to hold in trust for the community”—“is not in practice being approached”; what is approaching is “compulsory labour among an unfree majority of non-owners for the benefit of a free minority of owners.” The Socialist ideal, “in conflict with and yet informing the body of Capitalism, produces a third thing very different from the Socialist ideal—to wit, the Servile State” [source: belloc-servile-state].

His metaphor is a traveller who would escape mountain cold by going south on a river: the mountains will “divert the course of this River northward again”; “the big bend has already begun.” The traveller is the Socialist; the South is the Collectivist State; the River is modern “Organised Reform”; the northern bed is “a society reposing upon compulsory labour.” The only discrepancy is that the traveller could leave the river and go south by land—corresponding to “a bold policy of Confiscation,” which he nowhere denies is “ideally possible,” as it is ideally possible that “to-morrow all Englishmen shall take and preserve for twenty-four hours a vow of silence.” “Nothing like it or approaching it has ever been done or is now being done.” With every step along existing lines “we are making it more and more difficult to retrace such steps.” “The path of Confiscation, the only way by which Socialists can reach their goal, gets more and more remote with every new and positive economic reform, undertaken, remember, with the aid and under the advice of Socialists themselves” [source: belloc-servile-state].

Labour is “servile” which is undertaken “not in fulfilment of contract but under the compulsion of positive law and which attaches to the status of the labourer, and is performed for the benefit of others who are under no such compulsion.” The historical claim that slavery receded “under the influence of the Catholic Church” is, he writes, “not a piece of special pleading put forward to please my co-religionists” but “a plain piece of historical fact.” He has not said that a “completely free Distributive State” was ever reached: it was “in process of formation when the disruption of our united European civilisation in the sixteenth century arrested its development and slowly produced, in this country especially, Capitalism in its stead.” State regulation and municipal enterprise, until based on Confiscation, “obviously do not affect my argument.” “You cannot ‘buy out’ Capitalism” [source: belloc-servile-state].

Since first publication he notes the facts he treats as confirmation: “We already have Wages Boards in one great industry; we shall shortly have them in more.” Registration of the proletariat—name, address, movement, illness, supposed or real “malingering,” drink, domestic habits, employment—is “very nearly complete,” imposed by “the wealthier classes who are the actual gatherers of the Poll Tax upon which this registration is based.” Labour Exchanges will register every proletarian as a worker, “his tendencies to rebellion against Capital,” whether and when he has “refused service.” No change in European society arrives at completion unless it is universal throughout Europe; Capitalism is not thus universal, so “the advent of servitude is therefore a probability differing in degree.” The example of economic freedom elsewhere “may in the future transform, and will certainly limit,” sections drifting toward slavery. “But the tendency to the re-establishment of slavery as a necessary development of Capitalism is patent wherever Capitalism has power, and nowhere more than in this country” [source: belloc-servile-state].

The body of the book is written to maintain one proposition: that a free modern society in which the means of production are owned by a few is in unstable equilibrium, and tends to stable equilibrium “BY THE ESTABLISHMENT OF COMPULSORY LABOUR LEGALLY ENFORCEABLE UPON THOSE WHO DO NOT OWN THE MEANS OF PRODUCTION FOR THE ADVANTAGE OF THOSE WHO DO.” He will not judge whether the approaching organisation be “good or evil,” only show “the necessary tendency towards it” and “the recent social provisions which show that it has actually begun.” He proves the thesis from industrial Great Britain, “including that small, alien, and exceptional corner of Ireland, which suffers or enjoys industrial conditions to-day.” Definitions of wealth, labour, land, capital, proletariat, property, Collectivism, Capitalism, and the Servile State occupy the first section. Europe, he argues, sprang from a pagan servile basis; the Church slowly released it toward a mediaeval Distributive State of owners (serf becoming peasant, guilds checking monopoly); that arrangement was wrecked in England not by machinery but by the sixteenth-century transfer of monastic land to an oligarchy, so that by 1700 England was already capitalist and the later Industrial Revolution took a capitalist form. Capitalism is unstable because law still presupposes free owners while “nineteen out of twenty” have no property, and because it cannot of itself give sufficiency and security. The only three solutions are Collectivism, the Distributive State, or the Servile State. Collectivism is the easiest apparent solution; in action, pursued without confiscation, it produces the third thing. The Socialist who cannot confiscate, the reformer who loves tabulation, the “Practical Man,” and a proletariat that fears “the sack” more than the Courts, all make for status in exchange for a guaranteed wage. “The Servile State is, in industrial England to-day, no longer a menace but something in actual existence.” Employers’ liability, the Insurance Act (compulsion by employment and collection of the tax by the capitalist class), wages boards and a legal minimum in mining, labour exchanges, and the prospect of labour colonies are the English illustrations. Municipalisation by loan is “sham Socialism.” “Mere Capitalist anarchy cannot endure.” He does not believe theoretical Collectivism “will ever inform a real and living society.” The remaining alternatives are “a reaction towards well-divided property, or the re-establishment of servitude.” France and Ireland he records as societies that may check the tide making in Prussia and England. “As I am upon the whole hopeful that the Faith will recover its intimate and guiding place in the heart of Europe, so I believe that this sinking back into our original Paganism (for the tendency to the Servile State is nothing less) will in due time be halted and reversed.” Videat Deus [source: belloc-servile-state].

The two books are one political stance. In 1911 he treats Parliament as already null under a self-elected governing group and secret funds. In 1912–13 he treats industrial law as already laying the corner-stone of a new status: not the Collectivist trust he says Socialists intend, and not the widely distributed property he names as the only free alternative, but compulsory labour of non-owners for owners, sweetened by security. The Party System is the machine of the professional politicians; Social Reform is the river that does not run south.
