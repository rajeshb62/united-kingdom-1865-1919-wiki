Arthur James Balfour (1848–1930)

Chief Secretary for Ireland · First Lord of the Treasury and Leader of the House of Commons · Prime Minister (1902–1905) · First Lord of the Admiralty (1915–1916) · Foreign Secretary (1916–1919)

Arthur James Balfour stood at the hinge between Salisbury’s late-Victorian Unionism and the Edwardian crisis of tariff, defence, and party. He led the House of Commons for his uncle, succeeded him as Prime Minister in 1902, held office through fiscal schism and the approach of the Entente with France, resigned on the eve of the 1906 landslide, and returned in war as Admiralty chief and Foreign Secretary under Asquith’s coalition and Lloyd George’s War Cabinet. Contemporaries noted dialectical brilliance and philosophical detachment in equal measure—qualities that made him indispensable in council and exasperating to those who wanted a simpler party line.

Early Career under Salisbury

Balfour entered the front rank of Conservative politics as a member of the “Fourth Party,” the small parliamentary group associated with Lord Randolph Churchill that harried both Liberal and orthodox Conservative front benches in the early 1880s (McCarthy, 1903–1905). Under Lord Salisbury he became Chief Secretary for Ireland, a post that fixed his reputation as a firm Unionist administrator, and later First Lord of the Treasury and Leader of the House of Commons while Salisbury retained the Premiership and, for long periods, the Foreign Office. When Salisbury returned to power with Balfour as Leader of the Commons, Sir Michael Hicks Beach as Chancellor, and Gerald Balfour among the junior offices, the pattern of family and party government that would last until 1905 was already set (McCarthy, 1903–1905).

As Leader of the House he managed procedure, Irish business, and the long Unionist majority with a coolness that contrasted with Chamberlain’s platform energy. Education legislation of the 1890s, including the 1897 measure associated with Balfour and Sir John Gorst and the grant in aid of voluntary elementary schools, showed him at work on the religious and local-government settlements that would reappear, enlarged, in the Education Act of 1902 (McCarthy, 1903–1905).

Premiership (1902–1905)

Accession and Reconstruction

Lord Salisbury retired in the summer of 1902. Balfour became Prime Minister, reconstructed the ministry, and faced at once the unfinished business of the South African settlement, the Education Bill in committee, and the reorganisation of imperial defence (Annual Register, 1902). In the Commons he wound up debates on the Cape constitution, Imperial defence, and the conduct of the late war; he insisted that, so long as the republics refused to surrender their independence and Britain was equally determined that they should surrender it, they “must be conquered and subjugated,” while defending the government against charges of insufficient information from the field (Annual Register, 1902). Procedure reform occupied a notable parliamentary day when he described the Ministerial scheme for modernising the rules of the House of Commons—arguing that standing orders suited to the mid-eighteenth century no longer fitted a crowded modern chamber—and steered the new rules through successive sittings despite illness and adjournments (Annual Register, 1902).

Education, Licensing, and Domestic Legislation

The Education Bill of 1902 became a prolonged conflict with Nonconformist opinion. A Nonconformist deputation waited on Balfour; committee stages enlarged grants to elementary schools and struck out the option clause; management of voluntary schools filled weeks of debate; and Balfour’s Manchester speech rallied Unionist support as Chamberlain addressed meetings in Birmingham (Annual Register, 1902). Licensing policy in the following sessions drew him into another contested field: deputations pressed him on the treatment of licence-holders, and he replied with a mixture of sympathy for orderly trade and caution against precipitate inquiry while party and local passions ran high (Annual Register, 1903–1904). Irish land purchase under the Wyndham Act of 1903 and the subsequent MacDonnell–Wyndham correspondence kept Ireland continually before the House; Balfour’s statements as to the Viceroy’s opinions and the working of the Land Act were central to the Address debates of 1905 (Annual Register, 1905).

Imperial Defence and the Committee of Imperial Defence

Defence reorganisation was the constructive core of the premiership. In 1902–1903 Balfour explained the reorganisation of the Council of Defence and insisted that the results of its deliberations would be handed to successors of whatever party, so that the military policy of the country might have continuity beyond party tenure (Annual Register, 1903). The Committee of Imperial Defence, which he had informally established in 1902, received legal status and a permanent secretary on the recommendation of the Esher reforms; Balfour backed a full-time secretariat and treated the CID as the centre for strategic coordination (Politics of Grand Strategy). He accepted the “blue water” contention that the Navy could block invasion, so that by 1903–1904 most of the Cabinet, including Balfour and Arnold-Forster, held that the Army existed not for home defence but for India and South Africa—and only “barely conceivably” for Canada (Politics of Grand Strategy). In public he stressed naval and military questions together: at Glasgow in January 1905 he claimed that War Office changes would enable Britain “more effectively to intervene” in imperial emergencies, defended artillery rearmament against charges of delay, and spoke with enthusiasm of Selborne’s Admiralty in striking 130 obsolete vessels from the active list (Annual Register, 1905). Colonial conferences, he hoped, would become “as much a part of the ordinary working of the machinery of the Empire as the House of Commons” itself (Annual Register, 1905).

Fiscal Schism: Chamberlain, Retaliation, and the Half-Sheet of Notepaper

The fiscal controversy that began with Chamberlain’s Birmingham pronouncement of 1903 dominated the rest of the premiership. Balfour defended the abolition of the corn duty; public confusion followed; Free Trade Unionists organised; Tariff Reformers pressed for preference and protection; and in September 1903 Chamberlain, Ritchie, and Lord George Hamilton resigned, while the Duke of Devonshire soon followed (Annual Register, 1903). Balfour’s own pamphlet and his Sheffield declarations sought to define a Ministerial middle line of “retaliation” and an unfettered colonial conference without committing the Cabinet to Chamberlain’s full programme (Annual Register, 1903–1904).

That line never satisfied either wing. At East Manchester in January 1904 he attempted to define and defend the government’s position to his constituents; a luncheon speech the next day hinted at alternative means of imperial union and left even friendly listeners unsure whether he had one policy or two (Annual Register, 1904). In the Commons, Gerald Balfour’s Board of Trade speeches were read by Free Food Unionists as ruling Chamberlain’s specific proposals out of government policy, only for later Ministerial speeches to re-cloud the issue (Annual Register, 1904). Devonshire and Lord Balfour of Burleigh explained their resignations by the want of limit and definition in fiscal policy and by the danger that electioneering would pledge the party to a course the Prime Minister himself had called dangerous (Annual Register, 1904). Liberals made it their cue to identify Balfour’s subtleties with Chamberlain’s Protection; Chamberlain, for his part, sometimes insisted that differences with Balfour were only of “method and tactics” (Annual Register, 1905).

In January 1905, answering the demand that someone state the Prime Minister’s fiscal views on a sheet of notepaper, Balfour produced a “half-sheet of notepaper” of his own drafting—a fighting speech to his constituents that still, critics noted, said nothing clear on whether he thought a tax on food wise or unwise (Annual Register, 1905). Lord George Hamilton appealed publicly against the disintegration of the party; bye-elections went against the government; the Conservative conference at Newcastle showed the strength of the Chamberlainite section; and Balfour’s appeals for unity competed with Chamberlain’s contrary appeals from Bristol (Annual Register, 1905). Churchill later judged that Balfour never had a fair chance: he succeeded to an “exhausted inheritance,” kept office for two years against clamour for dissolution, and regarded a party split as the worst of domestic catastrophes (Churchill, The World Crisis, vol. 1).

Foreign Policy under Lansdowne

Foreign affairs in these years were formally Lansdowne’s, but Balfour’s Cabinet weight was constant. After the Boxer rising, Chamberlain, Balfour, and Lansdowne persuaded Salisbury toward a modest departure from isolation in the Anglo-German Yangtze agreement; Balfour, more than most British statesmen of the hour, is portrayed as seeing the Anglo-German problem in a larger setting and working for gradual amelioration, even while Salisbury retained the deciding vote and remained soured by past German experience (Langer, The Diplomacy of Imperialism, vol. 2). In the Far East, Balfour’s Manchester speech of January 1898 had already set commercial rather than territorial interests at the centre of China policy; he later took a sharp line against Russian railway exclusiveness in Manchuria, and after prolonged Cabinet debate joined the Weihaiwei decision once Port Arthur was judged not worth war (Langer, vol. 2). Critics nicknamed him “Port Arthur Balfour” in the same season that Curzon was “Cocksure Curzon,” reflecting public anger at the handling of Russian advances (Langer, vol. 2).

With France, conversations that had begun under Salisbury grew more specific after Balfour’s accession; by mid-1903 the Moroccan status quo and related colonial adjustments were on the table, and the Balfour–Lansdowne government embarked on the settlements that issued in the Entente Cordiale (Politics of Grand Strategy; Churchill, World Crisis, vol. 1). During the first Moroccan crisis Balfour wrote to Edward VII that Delcassé’s fall showed France could no longer be trusted as an effective power—an early, bleak reading of the Tangier aftermath (Politics of Grand Strategy). Venezuela, the Bagdad Railway, and the Anglo-Japanese Treaty all drew Commons statements from the Prime Minister; he insisted, with Lansdowne, that the Japanese alliance was made for peace, and he was careful not to give the Firth of Forth naval base an inflammatory international colouring when announcing it (Annual Register, 1902–1905).

Resignation, December 1905

Ten years of Conservative rule ended in December 1905. Late in November Balfour tendered his resignation to the King; Campbell-Bannerman formed an administration and appealed to the country in January 1906 (Churchill, World Crisis, vol. 1; Annual Register, 1905). The departure of the Cabinet was neither widely lamented nor unexpected; political circles had for weeks anticipated a Liberal government (Politics of Grand Strategy). Balfour defended the timing of resignation against the charge of clinging to office, while the Opposition had long denounced the “unconstitutional and humiliating fashion” in which, as they alleged, the ministry held on (Annual Register, 1905; Churchill, World Crisis, vol. 1).

Opposition and the Unionist Party after 1906

The general election of 1906 was a Unionist catastrophe. In Manchester, “one of the principal battle-grounds,” Balfour and eight Conservative colleagues were dismissed and replaced by nine Liberals or Labour men (Churchill, World Crisis, vol. 1). He found a seat for the City of London and resumed leadership of a shattered Opposition. Tariff Reform remained the open wound: he published a volume of fiscal speeches and writings from 1880 to 1905 with a preface on the changed attitude he recommended, while Free Trade Unionists under Devonshire’s letter insisted that loyalty to Balfour was compatible with rejecting pure Protection (Annual Register, 1906). He attacked the new Liberal programme as a “surprise packet,” denounced the campaign against Chinese labour in South Africa, and fought the Education Bill of 1906 clause by clause in Commons and Lords (Annual Register, 1906).

In the constitutional crisis of 1909–1911 he led Unionist resistance to the People’s Budget and to the Parliament Bill that stripped the Lords of their absolute veto. The Annual Register for 1911 records him throughout the prolonged struggle—in the Commons, in party councils, and in the country—as the principal Conservative spokesman against the Liberal–Irish combination that forced the Bill through under the threat of a mass creation of peers (Annual Register, 1911). The same years saw him at the centre of Unionist policy on Irish Home Rule and on the naval and military estimates that tracked the Anglo-German rivalry.

The Great War: Admiralty and Foreign Office

On the outbreak of war Balfour was brought into closer defence consultation. Churchill, then First Lord, asked that Balfour—who had retired from the formal leadership of the Unionist party—be added to the Committee of Imperial Defence; the request was granted, and the CID continued to meet not infrequently after August 1914 (Churchill, World Crisis, vol. 1). On 4 August he exchanged notes with Haldane on the employment of the Expeditionary Force; he was among those who thought a hundred thousand men enough for foreseeable emergencies and who resisted schemes for a much larger army that would take six months to raise (Politics of Grand Strategy).

In the First Coalition of May 1915 he went to the Admiralty as First Lord. Hankey’s diary shows him as a dominant dialectical presence on the War Council and War Committee: Asquith, Balfour, Kitchener, and Lloyd George insisted on a secretary for the Dardanelles Committee; Kitchener ranked Asquith and Balfour as the only “honest and capable” ministers and considered even Grey “stupid”; when Lloyd George later demanded Balfour’s removal from the Admiralty as the price of a reconstructed War Committee, he gave as reason only “too much wool,” while Hankey judged that Lloyd George wanted a free hand and feared a colleague “too strong and dialectically too skilful” to allow dictatorship (Hankey, Man of Secrets, vol. 1). Asquith’s difficulty was the greater because he had recently pressed Balfour to substitute Jellicoe for Jackson as First Sea Lord and had expressed a strong wish that Balfour remain in office (Hankey, vol. 1).

When Asquith fell in December 1916, Balfour was among the four—Asquith, Lloyd George, Bonar Law, and Balfour—whom the King was to see in the attempt to form a Coalition; after that negotiation failed and Lloyd George took the Premiership, Balfour moved to the Foreign Office (Hankey, vol. 1). He was not a member of the small War Cabinet but “always enjoyed ready access” to it (Hankey, vol. 1). Hankey trusted him above almost any other minister—“the only man I really trust”—even when others complained of Foreign Office inefficiency or of Balfour’s tendency to sit “on the hedge” between extremes, as in the Russian debate between hard anti-Bolshevism and more elastic approaches (Hankey, vol. 1). He delivered an able statement of war aims that Hankey found a summary of desiderata “entirely divorced from realities,” yet he remained the steady professional presence at the Foreign Office through 1917–1919, the recipient of War Cabinet thanks and congratulations, and a constant interlocutor on Mesopotamia, oil, Austria, and the American connection (Hankey, vol. 1; Annual Register, 1917–1919).

Character and Reputation

The sources paint a consistent portrait: a first-class mind in debate, a philosopher in politics, and a leader who preferred definition by pamphlet and half-sheet to the platform certainties of Chamberlain or the constructive radicalism of Lloyd George. Churchill called him a “veritable rock” in the Irish crisis of 1914, when Unionist leaders tendered written offers to the Prime Minister, and also the man who refused both abdication on accession and the wresting of power from his grasp (Churchill, World Crisis, vol. 1). Liberals mocked the fog in which two Conservatives, going opposite ways, each claimed to be following Balfour (Annual Register, 1905). Lloyd George’s “too much wool” and Hankey’s opposite loyalty mark the same quality from different angles: dialectical skill that could paralyse a dictator and reassure a secretary of the Committee of Imperial Defence. In the longer arc of the period 1865–1919 he is the Unionist who closed the Salisbury age, opened the Edwardian argument over tariffs and the Lords, built the modern machinery of imperial defence, and returned in war as the elder statesman of Admiralty and Foreign Office.

Sources

Primary contemporary and near-contemporary narrative for the premiership and Opposition years is drawn from the Annual Register (1902–1906, 1911, 1915–1917, 1919). Justin McCarthy’s History of Our Own Times and Diamond Jubilee volumes supply the Salisbury-era career and Irish Chief Secretaryship. Churchill’s World Crisis, volume 1, frames the succession, the fiscal struggle, the 1906 rout, and the wartime CID invitation. Hankey’s Man of Secrets, volume 1, is the principal war diary for Admiralty, War Council, and Foreign Office. The Politics of Grand Strategy documents CID doctrine, Morocco, and the Indian versus Continental strategic debate. Langer’s Diplomacy of Imperialism, volume 2, covers Far Eastern and Anglo-German policy in the late Salisbury–early Balfour Cabinets. Clayton’s Rise of Democracy records a 1910 Commons quotation.
