Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman (1836–1908)

Liberal statesman who served as Secretary of State for War under Gladstone and Rosebery, led the Liberal Party from 1899, and was Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from December 1905 until his resignation in April 1908. He presided over the greatest Liberal electoral landslide in history in 1906, granted self-government to the Transvaal, and reconciled the warring factions of his party. His firm opposition to the South African War and his denunciation of “methods of barbarism” defined his leadership in Opposition.

Biography

Early Career and Liberal Leadership

Campbell-Bannerman served as Secretary of State for War in the Gladstone and Rosebery administrations of the 1890s. In that office he was responsible for a scheme of army reform and was involved in the celebrated cordite debate (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, vol. 5). He was also a member of the South Africa Committee (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, vol. 5).

In the early spring of 1899, following the resignation of Sir William Harcourt as leader of the Liberal Party in the House of Commons, Campbell-Bannerman was chosen as his successor. The choice was made at a meeting of the Liberal Party held at the Reform Club, at which it was declared that Harcourt’s resignation was “absolute and final” (McCarthy, Diamond Jubilee, vol. 1). His election occurred at a time of deep division within Liberalism over the question of Home Rule for Ireland. The “Imperialist” wing, while not openly seceding, sought to create a new Liberal party concerned mainly with Imperial interests and prepared to abandon Home Rule. Campbell-Bannerman was “certainly not one of those men,” but his election “did not proclaim as impossible the development of the new policy” (McCarthy, Diamond Jubilee, vol. 1). He was chosen to lead his party in circumstances of great difficulty (Annual Register, 1908).

His first appearance as leader of the Opposition came in a debate on the Address, in which he found fault with the Government’s lack of interest and sympathy in accepting the Russian Emperor’s invitation to the Peace Conference, and with the insufficient description of the domestic measures about to be introduced (McCarthy, Diamond Jubilee, vol. 1).

South Africa, the Boer War and the “Methods of Barbarism”

During the South African War, Campbell-Bannerman emerged as the most prominent Liberal critic of the Government’s conduct of the conflict. At a National Reform Union dinner to Sir William Harcourt and himself on 14 June 1901, Campbell-Bannerman used the phrase “methods of barbarism” in reference to the conduct of the war in South Africa (Annual Register, 1901). This phrase, which referred to the British army’s scorched-earth tactics and the concentration camps, became a rallying cry for the anti-war wing of the Liberal Party and earned him the lasting enmity of Unionists. Years later, at his death, the Annual Register noted that the displays of respect from the Continent stood “in striking contrast to the savage attacks of some Unionists a few years before” (Annual Register, 1908).

Campbell-Bannerman also had dealings with Joseph Chamberlain in the pre-war period. In 1899, Chamberlain attempted to secure Liberal support for a forward policy in South Africa, telling Campbell-Bannerman that he was striving for a peaceful settlement but that “a demonstration…would be necessary. It would, however, be a game of bluff” (Langer, Diplomacy of Imperialism, vol. 2).

The Chinese Labour Controversy

The issue of Chinese labour in the Transvaal gold mines became central to Campbell-Bannerman’s campaign against the Unionist government after the war. In January 1904, speaking at Maidstone, he commented severely on the Government’s uncertainty of attitude on fiscal reform and stressed the danger of political corruption involved in protectionist policy (Annual Register, 1904). Addressing the Eighty Club on 22 January 1904, he declared that the country “was sick of the fiscal question” and that, despite the temptations offered by Chamberlain’s policy to manufacturers, the Liberal Party would resist (Annual Register, 1904).

Throughout 1904 and 1905, Campbell-Bannerman repeatedly questioned the Government in Parliament about the conditions of Chinese labour in the Transvaal. In the Address debate of 1905, he asked whether the Government were satisfied with its effects on the general peace (Annual Register, 1905). Balfour, in a speech defending his own administration, cited Campbell-Bannerman’s phrase “methods of barbarism” as an example of language “not likely to endear him to the Army” (Annual Register, 1905).

The Premiership (1905–1908)

Formation of Government. On Monday, 4 December 1905, Mr. Balfour tendered his resignation to the King. The following day, Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman was invited by the King to form a new Administration. He at once accepted the commission and kissed hands on his appointment as Prime Minister and First Lord of the Treasury. A Royal Warrant was issued directing that in future the Prime Minister should have precedence next after the Archbishop of York (Annual Register, 1905). Despite rumours of difficulties in Cabinet-making, on Sunday, 10 December, he submitted his Cabinet to the King. The appointments included Mr. Asquith as Chancellor of the Exchequer, Sir Edward Grey as Foreign Secretary, Mr. Haldane as Secretary for War, Mr. John Morley as Indian Secretary, Mr. Lloyd-George at the Board of Trade, Mr. John Burns at the Local Government Board, and the Earl of Elgin at the Colonial Office. “On the whole the composition of the new Ministry was favourably regarded by public opinion” (Annual Register, 1905).

The 1906 General Election. The new year found the country immersed in preparations for “a general election, which proved the most exciting and startling since 1880, if not since 1832” (Annual Register, 1906). The Opposition attacked the outgoing Ministers on charges of reckless extravagance, misjudgment of South African affairs, the introduction of “Chinese slavery” into the Transvaal, and the endowment of denominational education. Campbell-Bannerman had “somewhat unexpectedly revived” the question of Home Rule, but the Duke of Devonshire judged it improbable the new Government would introduce a Home Rule Bill (Annual Register, 1906).

The results were a landslide for the Liberal Party. The final tally stood at: Ministerialists — Liberals, 377; Labour, 53; Nationalists, 83 — total 513. Opposition — Conservatives, 132; Liberal Unionists, 25 — total 157. The Liberal and Labour majority over all other groups combined was 134, “and the prediction that the Government would be dependent on Nationalist votes was falsified” (Annual Register, 1906). Wales did not return a single Unionist, and Scotland became predominantly Liberal.

South African Settlement. One of the most important acts of Campbell-Bannerman’s government was the grant of responsible government to the Transvaal. On 31 July 1906, the main provisions of the new Transvaal Constitution were announced — in the Commons by Mr. Winston Churchill, the Colonial Under-Secretary, and in the Lords by the Earl of Elgin. The Lyttelton Constitution was declared “stillborn” and unworkable; under the new arrangement “Boer and Briton alike would receive the full privileges and rights of British citizenship” (Annual Register, 1906). The decision was fiercely attacked by the Opposition, who warned that the Boers would use their voting power as a substitute for arms. Campbell-Bannerman, rising near the close of the debate, declared he had “never heard a more unworthy speech” — his words drowned by Opposition protests — and the vote was carried by 316 to 83 (Annual Register, 1906). The following year, General Botha, in a speech in London, expressed the gratitude of the Transvaal for its constitution, assuring his audience “that it would be fully repaid” (Annual Register, 1907).

Domestic Policy. Campbell-Bannerman’s government linked the Free-Trade campaign with social reform, “gratifying his advanced followers” (Annual Register, 1908). He supported the enfranchisement of women, stating in 1907 that “personally, he warmly supported the enfranchisement of women. They were taxed without direct representation, and had to obey laws they did not make” (Annual Register, 1907). His government made twenty new peers between 1905 and 1907, exceeding “all previous records of the last century” (Clayton, Rise of Democracy). On naval policy, Campbell-Bannerman declared himself “entirely in favour of the two-Power standard” (Annual Register, 1907). His government proposed reductions in the Navy estimates, a policy noted in the Hankey papers as reflecting the view that the navy was “prodigiously strong,” having been built “to fight a combination of France and Russia” (Hankey, Man of Secrets, vol. 1).

Foreign Affairs and the Military Conversations. In the early weeks of his government, Campbell-Bannerman was drawn into the question of Anglo-French military conversations following the Algeciras Conference. Grey’s proposal for staff talks was communicated to the Prime Minister, but Campbell-Bannerman’s reaction was cautious: he insisted Paris “could not expect an answer until the elections were over” (Politics of Grand Strategy). The record of important conversations went only to King Edward, Campbell-Bannerman, and Lord Ripon, and only in an abbreviated version that made no mention of staff talks. Later, when the issue was examined retrospectively, it was noted that Campbell-Bannerman as Prime Minister, not Grey, “had the final responsibility for bringing the talks before the Cabinet” (Politics of Grand Strategy).

Death of Lady Campbell-Bannerman. On 30 August 1906, Lady Campbell-Bannerman died at Marienbad. The event “evoked expressions of profound sorrow and sympathy from politicians of all parties alike, as well as from foreign and Colonial Ministers and other eminent personages” (Annual Register, 1906).

Resignation and Death

By early 1908, Campbell-Bannerman’s health had deteriorated. On Sunday, 5 April 1908, a courier arrived at Biarritz, where the King had been for a month, bearing the Prime Minister’s resignation, tendered “on the urgent representations of his medical advisers.” Departing from constitutional tradition, the King did not return; having accepted the resignation with expressions of regret and esteem, he summoned Mr. Asquith to Biarritz (Annual Register, 1908).

The resignation was announced in both Houses on Monday, 6 April. The Marquess of Ripon alluded to the late Prime Minister’s “singular influence over the Commons, based on perfect temper, earnest sympathy with the feelings and principles of his party, and unfailing courtesy to opponents.” In the Commons, Mr. Asquith declared that “no Prime Minister resigning had ever been more universally or deservedly beloved” (Annual Register, 1908).

Death and Obituary. The Annual Register’s obituary observed: “It was generally admitted that Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman had not reached the first rank of statesmen: but he had shown himself an excellent administrator in several departments; he had been chosen to lead his party in circumstances of great difficulty, and he had acquired a singular ascendancy both in it and in the House. He had reconciled its conflicting sections, had conciliated the Irish, and had gratified his advanced followers by linking the Free-Trade campaign with Social Reform. The King’s personal regard for him was exhibited in the farewell visit paid on March 5 before his Majesty left for Biarritz.”

His funeral service in Westminster Abbey on 27 April was attended by the Prince of Wales (the King and Queen being absent at Copenhagen), the French Prime Minister M. Clemenceau, who came specially from Paris, and representatives of the various departments of State, the Diplomatic Corps, the Universities, and leading Liberal organisations. By his own wish he was buried at Meigle, Perthshire, near his home and beside his wife. The House of Commons adjourned out of respect, after tributes by Mr. Asquith, Mr. Akers-Douglas (in the absence through illness of Mr. Balfour, who expressed his feelings in a letter), and representatives of the Irish, Welsh, and Labour members. Asquith laid stress on “the acuteness of the late Premier’s sympathies, his sensitiveness to suffering, his reliance on moral force, his passionate love of peace, his calm, persistent, and indomitable courage, and his idealism and optimism” (Annual Register, 1908). The Stirling by-election which followed showed a larger Liberal majority than had ever been obtained by Campbell-Bannerman, who had held the seat since 1885 (Annual Register, 1908).
