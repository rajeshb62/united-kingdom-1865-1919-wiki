Admiral Sir John Rushworth Jellicoe (1859–1935)

Commander-in-Chief, Grand Fleet (1914–1916) · First Sea Lord (1916–1917) · Admiral of the Fleet (1919) · 1st Earl Jellicoe of Scapa (1925)

Command of the Grand Fleet

John Rushworth Jellicoe was appointed Second Sea Lord in 1912, where he “vehemently pressed for very large increases” in oil-fuel reserves, recognising the strategic importance of petroleum for the fleet. (Churchill, The World Crisis Vol. 1) In June 1914, Winston Churchill — then First Lord of the Admiralty — arranged for Jellicoe to become Second-in-Command of the Home Fleet under Sir George Callaghan, with Churchill writing privately that Jellicoe “is not yet sufficiently in command of the confidence of the Sea Service, to justify what would necessarily be a very startling promotion” but that the appointment would “see what fitness he will develop for the succession.” (Churchill, The World Crisis Vol. 1) The appointment passed Jellicoe “over the heads of four or five of the most important senior Admirals on the active list” and made him “virtually designated for the supreme command.” (Churchill, The World Crisis Vol. 1)

On the eve of war, Vice-Admiral Sir John Jellicoe was appointed Second-in-Command of the Home Fleet on 31 July 1914. Corbett’s official history records that “to strengthen the staff of the Fleet, Vice-Admiral Sir John R. Jellicoe had been appointed Second in Command,” with Rear-Admiral Charles E. Madden as Chief of the Staff. (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. I)

When war broke out, Admiral Callaghan was relieved and Jellicoe succeeded to the supreme command with the acting rank of Admiral. Corbett notes that Callaghan had brought the Fleet “to the high state of efficiency it was showing” but “it was thought better to commit the arduous work ahead to a younger officer.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. I) The Annual Register recorded that “Admiral Sir John Jellicoe was appointed to the supreme command of the Home Fleets, with Rear-Admiral C. E. Madden as his Chief of Staff.” (Annual Register 1914)

When Admiral Jellicoe took over the Grand Fleet, it “consisted of twenty Dreadnoughts, eight King Edwards, four battle cruisers, two squadrons of cruisers and one of light cruisers.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. I) He received notice of the ultimatum at sea on 4 August and immediately commenced the precautionary movements laid down for covering the passage of the British Expeditionary Force. By 5 August he had completed his sweep of the North Sea and was directed to keep his fleet to the northward to hold the entrance to the North Sea. (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. I)

Jellicoe established his headquarters at Scapa Flow in the Orkney Islands, the principal wartime base of the Grand Fleet. The base was still being fortified when war began, and Jellicoe repeatedly pressed for improved defences. In November 1914, “Admiral Sir John Jellicoe was instructed to return the twelve detached destroyers as soon as the Scapa defences were secure.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. II) The insecurity of the northern anchorages “continued in some measure to cramp Admiral Jellicoe’s freedom of action” in the early months of the war. (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. II)

Throughout 1915 and early 1916, Jellicoe maintained the deterrent posture on which British grand strategy rested. He urged the creation of “special flotillas” for hunting submarines, pressed for more trawlers for minesweeping ahead of the fleet, and faced constant demands on his destroyer force. In the summer of 1915, King George V visited the Grand Fleet, “under the command of Admiral Sir John Jellicoe,” expressing “his pride and admiration at the splendid aspect of the Navy.” (Annual Register 1915) Jellicoe was promoted G.C.B. in the same year. (Annual Register 1915)

Battle of Jutland

The Battle of Jutland (31 May – 1 June 1916) was the only major fleet action of the war. Jellicoe commanded the Grand Fleet’s battle squadrons while Vice-Admiral Sir David Beatty led the Battle Cruiser Fleet in advance. The German High Seas Fleet under Admiral Scheer had sortied in an attempt to cut off and destroy a portion of the British forces.

Corbett’s official history of the battle (Volume IV) describes the outcome from the strategic perspective: “Admiral Scheer had failed in his object of cutting off and overwhelming part of our advanced forces, and had found himself unexpectedly entrapped into meeting the Grand Fleet. From this encounter he had succeeded in extricating himself at considerable expense, but, on the other hand, both before and after the main action he had inflicted upon us more serious losses than he could ever have contemplated.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. IV)

The critical assessment recorded by Corbett was that “Admiral Jellicoe had outmanœuvred and surprised the High Seas Fleet, and for him the net result of the action had been to increase the Grand Fleet’s large margin of superiority as a combative force, a defence against invasion, and an instrument of blockade. The control of the North Sea remained in our hands, and any expectation that this control might be weakened or taken from us had been finally dissipated.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. IV)

Jellicoe’s battle fleet returned to Scapa Flow between 10.30 a.m. and noon on 2 June. By 9.45 p.m. that evening, Jellicoe reported to the Admiralty that “the Grand Fleet was at its base, re-fuelled, with steam at four hours’ notice, and ready for sea.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. IV) This rapid return to readiness drew admiration from neutral observers: “The Dutch naval officers were reported to be ‘filled with admiration for the speed with which Jellicoe was ready for action again.‘” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. IV) A German naval commentator wrote that “Jellicoe did not for an instant surrender the command of the sea.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. IV)

Almost immediately upon return to harbour, Jellicoe “appointed committees of gunnery, torpedo and signalling experts to report upon the manner in which the fleet material had stood the test.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. IV) His tactical caution — the decision to decline night action against the retiring German fleet — became the subject of lasting debate, but reflected his understanding of the strategic stakes: he commanded the margin of British naval supremacy, and its destruction in an ill-timed engagement could have been catastrophic for the Allied cause. The contemporary view, as summarised by Winston Churchill, was that Jellicoe was “the only man on either side who could lose the war in an afternoon.”

First Sea Lord & Convoy System

In November 1916, as the strain of command and the mounting threat of unrestricted submarine warfare demanded fresh leadership at the Admiralty, Jellicoe was appointed First Sea Lord. The Annual Register recorded the announcement: “Admiral Sir John Jellicoe would become First Sea Lord in place of Admiral Sir Henry Jackson. Vice-Admiral Sir David Beatty had already assumed command of the Grand Fleet in succession to Sir John Jellicoe.” (Annual Register 1916) Balfour stated that “the decision involving these appointments was taken some time previously, but that for military reasons the announcement had been delayed.” (Annual Register 1916)

Jellicoe’s appointment came at a critical moment. Maurice Hankey, Secretary of the War Cabinet, had noted a “marked strain of pessimism in Jellicoe’s character” and recorded that “Lloyd George, Beaverbrook and Dr. Thomas Jones all expressed opinions identical to Hankey’s on this matter; yet Jellicoe was appointed First Sea Lord in succession to Admiral Jackson in the following December.” (Hankey, Man of Secrets Vol. I)

The submarine crisis was the overriding problem. As early as October 1916 — while still C-in-C of the Grand Fleet — Jellicoe had written to the Prime Minister “expressing anxiety with regard to the danger to the Allied cause from submarine attacks on merchant ships, which with notable prescience he anticipated would increase in the following spring.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V) At the War Committee meeting on 2 November 1916, Jellicoe was asked by Lloyd George if he had any plan against German submarines. Corbett records that “Sir John Jellicoe said that he had not” and that when Lloyd George suggested a convoy system with a dozen ships escorted by three warships, Jellicoe replied “that they would never be able to keep merchant ships sufficiently together to enable a few destroyers to screen them.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V)

Corbett’s history provides careful context: “It was, however, probable that the view put forward by Sir John Jellicoe on this occasion was more the Admiralty’s than his own. He was at this time commanding the Grand Fleet and had not studied the question of the protection of ocean trade.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V) When he became First Sea Lord, Jellicoe “was bound to give great weight and consideration to Admiralty opinion as he found it; and, since the November Conference, opinion had not changed at all. Indeed, it had hardened.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V) An official Admiralty pamphlet issued in January 1917 stated “quite emphatically, that a convoy system was not a sound measure of trade defence.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V)

Nevertheless, Jellicoe himself was “still clear and decided on one point: that if the existing system of trade defence needed reinforcing and supplementing, then this could only be done by instituting some kind of convoy system. One of his earliest minutes was a warning to the Staff that they might be obliged to place some of the Atlantic trade under convoy.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V) The practical difficulties were formidable: destroyer resources were stretched to breaking point, and Jellicoe “was constantly held back by the reflection that the destroyer forces required for convoy work would have to be collected from commands both at home and abroad.” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V)

In April 1917, following the Longhope Conference on the Scandinavian trade, Jellicoe “endorsed the findings of the Conference and gave orders that the ‘system was to be tried, and a report sent fortnightly on its working.‘” (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V) The experimental convoy on the Scandinavian route proved successful, and the system was gradually extended to Atlantic and Mediterranean trade. The convoy system is widely regarded as the decisive strategic innovation that defeated the U-boat offensive in 1917–1918.

Jellicoe also co-operated with the reorganisation of the Admiralty’s War Staff in May 1917, when “Sir John Jellicoe, as Chief of the Staff, was freed of all administrative detail in order that he might give his undivided attention to questions of policy and strategy.” (Annual Register 1917)

Jellicoe’s tenure as First Sea Lord ended abruptly. In December 1917, Sir Eric Geddes (the new First Lord) dismissed him. Hankey’s account records that Geddes asked Admiral Sir Rosslyn Wemyss “whether he would be prepared to step into Jellicoe’s shoes,” and “on the evening of Monday 24th Jellicoe found on his desk a letter of dismissal.” (Hankey, Man of Secrets Vol. I) The Sea Lords threatened resignation, and “a somewhat heated correspondence followed.” (Hankey, Man of Secrets Vol. I) The King conferred a peerage on Jellicoe, and Wemyss succeeded him as First Sea Lord. (Annual Register 1917) Hankey wrote privately to Jellicoe expressing his admiration for “the way you have tackled the difficult problems with which you were confronted, and literally put a new face on the submarine warfare.” (Hankey, Man of Secrets Vol. I)

Later Career

Jellicoe was promoted to Admiral of the Fleet on 3 April 1919, alongside Sir David Beatty. (Annual Register 1919) He was raised to the peerage as Viscount Jellicoe of Scapa in 1918 and later created 1st Earl Jellicoe of Scapa in 1925.

In 1919, Jellicoe published The Grand Fleet: 1914–16, described by the Annual Register as “one of the most important books published during the year” and “of the first historical importance.” (Annual Register 1919) The work dealt with “the whole period of his command, from the beginning of the war down to his return to the Admiralty in 1917.” It gave “a very interesting account of the Battle of Jutland — ‘the first Fleet action since Trafalgar, if we except the action in the Russo-Japanese War’” and described “the defects in the material of the Fleet,” noting that “in many respects the Germans had the advantage.” (Annual Register 1919)

Jellicoe undertook an extended naval mission to India and the Dominions (1919–1920), advising on post-war naval organisation and imperial defence. He remained a prominent public figure and an authoritative voice on naval affairs until his death on 20 November 1935.

His papers were preserved in the British Museum, and his official correspondence was later edited by A. Temple Patterson and published by the Navy Records Society as The Jellicoe Papers (1966–1968). (Hankey, Man of Secrets Vol. I; Politics and Grand Strategy)

Assessment: Jellicoe commanded the Grand Fleet at its moment of maximum responsibility, from the outbreak of war through the Battle of Jutland — the only major fleet engagement between dreadnought battleships. His strategic caution at Jutland reflected a clear understanding that the destruction of the Grand Fleet meant the loss of the war. As First Sea Lord, he presided over the Admiralty during the most dangerous phase of the unrestricted submarine campaign and laid the groundwork for the convoy system that ultimately defeated the U-boat. His dismissal in December 1917 was a brutal end to a pivotal wartime career, but the convoy measures set in motion under his tenure proved decisive in the final year of the war.
