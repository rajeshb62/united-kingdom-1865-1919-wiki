Winston Spencer Churchill (1874–1965)

President of the Board of Trade (1908–1910) · Home Secretary (1910–1911) · First Lord of the Admiralty (1911–1915) · Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster (1915) · Minister of Munitions (1917–)

Winston Spencer Churchill moved from the Unionist benches to the Liberal front rank, then from social and industrial office to the Admiralty at the height of the Anglo-German naval race. As First Lord he held the Fleet together through the July Crisis, ordered its night passage to war stations, and shared with the Board the first months of war at sea—Antwerp, the Channel ports, Coronel and the Falklands, and the recall of Lord Fisher. The Dardanelles enterprise and Fisher’s resignation in May 1915 broke the Admiralty partnership and forced him from office when Asquith reconstructed the ministry as a Coalition. He returned under Lloyd George as Minister of Munitions. Within the years of this wiki he is the statesman of Board of Trade and Home Office reform, of the 1911–15 Admiralty, and of the political fall that closed the pure Liberal war ministry.

From Soldier-Journalist to Liberal Minister

Churchill’s early fame was military and literary rather than ministerial. He had ridden with the 21st Lancers in the Sudan and later recorded Lord Kitchener’s disapproval of the young lieutenant who had forced his way into the campaign—a “dislike before first sight” that did not prevent later wartime cooperation when Kitchener took the War Office (The World Crisis, Vol. I). By the mid-Edwardian years he sat as a Liberal after leaving the Unionists over Free Trade. The Annual Register of 1908 places him among the speakers who made light of Mid-Devon as a storm in a teacup after his African tour, predicted land-value taxation and a measure against the Lords’ veto, and stressed Free Trade’s advantages for British commerce and international peace. At Swansea in August he repudiated the notion that an Anglo-German war was inevitable; the war party in Germany, he said, did not number ten thousand, and in England it existed only in lunatic asylums and on the staff of the National Review—language the Standard treated as undermining Sir Edward Grey, a misrepresentation Churchill denounced in the Daily Chronicle (Annual Register, 1908).

Belloc and Cecil Chesterton’s The Party System (1911) pairs him with Lloyd George as the two faces of the late 1910 election: “patriots confronting a haughty aristocracy (as represented to Mr Churchill by his cousin the Duke of Marlborough)” on one poster, “low-born demagogues” on the other. Enthusiastic Liberals still look on Asquith and Churchill as “tribunes of a people rightly struggling to be free”; the “credulous Radical” believes “Mr Churchill has become an outcast from his order by bravely taking the side of the people.” In the kinship list of the Governing Group, Churchill “as a member of the family whose name he bears, and as heir to his father’s career, has a double right.” “As all the world knows,” he is “not only the cousin of a former Conservative Minister, the Duke of Marlborough, but directly succeeded the head of his own family in the post he held, that of Under Secretary for the Colonies.” A Cecil, a Howard, a Churchill, or a Rothschild is the sort of name the machines prefer when they place candidates. The book stops at that Front-Bench network; it does not forecast later office.

When Campbell-Bannerman resigned in April 1908 and Asquith became Prime Minister, Churchill entered the Cabinet as President of the Board of Trade while Lloyd George took the Exchequer. His re-election for North-West Manchester that spring failed: Joynson Hicks defeated him by 5,517 to 4,988, with a Socialist far behind; Free-Trade Unionists and Irish electors had been recognised as swing factors, and Irish leaders only advised support after Churchill’s Easter Monday profession of Home Rule faith with the Prime Minister’s concurrence. He was at once invited to stand for Dundee and held that seat as the ministerial programme of old-age pensions, Free Trade finance, and social reform unfolded (Annual Register, 1908).

Board of Trade and Home Office

At the Board of Trade Churchill spoke for Free Trade at international congresses, foreshadowed bankruptcy and insurance reforms, and stood with Lloyd George in the radical wing that resisted the full Admiralty demand in the 1909 naval scare. Fisher and the Board wanted six new dreadnoughts; the economists offered four; the Cabinet compromised on eight—“We want eight and we won’t wait” having already seized the platform. Churchill later recorded the compromise in The World Crisis without concealing that he had been among those who doubted the scale of the German acceleration (Politics of Grand Strategy; The World Crisis, Vol. I; Annual Register, 1909).

As Home Secretary he bore the brunt of industrial disorder and of the constitutional struggle over the Lords’ veto. In the railway crisis of August 1911 troops occupied stations and parks; Churchill told the Commons that outrages on railway property were crimes punishable by penal servitude and that the Government would secure food, fuel, and services vital to the community, the working classes being the worst sufferers from famine and a general arrest of industry. The Annual Register later noted that the peremptory tone of the Prime Minister and of the Home Secretary owed something to the still half-secret strain of Anglo-German relations after Agadir (Annual Register, 1911). He also took Asquith’s place in major Parliament Bill debates when the Prime Minister was away, denied rumours of ministerial disunion, and defended the Bill as the answer to a legislative deadlock created by the Lords (Annual Register, 1911).

First Lord of the Admiralty (1911–1915)

Agadir and Appointment

The Agadir crisis of 1911—the Panther off the Moroccan Atlantic coast—pushed Churchill from the radical economy wing toward the centre of war preparation. He sat with Asquith, Grey, McKenna, and Lloyd George in the “very secret defence meeting” that managed the crisis; he drafted the memorandum of 13 August on the “Twentieth Day” and “Fortieth Day” of a continental campaign; and he wrote to Grey at the end of August as the crisis closed. The Prime Minister then invited him to the Admiralty. From 25 October 1911 to 28 May 1915 he was, in the words of the Royal Letters Patent, responsible to Crown and Parliament for all the business of the Admiralty (The World Crisis, Vol. I; Hankey, Man of Secrets, Vol. I).

Naval Policy: Sixty Per Cent and the Naval Holiday

At the Admiralty he found the Board, the incomplete War Staff, and the legacy of Fisher’s concentration and of Sir Arthur Wilson’s tenure. He pressed oil fuel, the 15-inch gun, and the Queen Elizabeth class; he reoriented dispositions toward the North Sea and the strategic geography of Scapa, Cromarty, and Rosyth. In March 1912 he announced that the Admiralty would maintain a sixty per cent margin over the dreadnoughts authorised by the 1908 German Naval Law, and would meet any German increases above that law on a two-to-one basis. The Haldane Mission to Berlin had already failed to trade a slowing of German construction for a British neutrality pledge Grey could not give without abandoning France. In 1913 Churchill proposed a “naval holiday”—a blank year of capital-ship building that would, he argued, wipe out five British super-Dreadnoughts if Germany built none—but German opinion received the suggestion as an attempt to wreck the Navy Law. By early 1914 he judged that naval rivalry had for the moment ceased to be a cause of friction: Britain was proceeding inflexibly with declared programmes, and Germany had made no further increases since 1912 (The World Crisis, Vol. I; Politics of Grand Strategy; Annual Register, 1913–1914).

Anglo-French naval conversations under his First Lordship concentrated French strength in the Mediterranean and British strength in the Channel and North Sea—the strategic counterpart of the staff talks Grey had authorised years earlier. Mediterranean withdrawals and the Grey–Cambon exchange of November 1912 on “moral obligation” remained politically delicate inside the Liberal Cabinet (Politics of Grand Strategy).

July Crisis and Mobilisation

On Friday 24 July 1914 the Cabinet was still immersed in the Irish boundary question when Grey brought in the Austrian note to Serbia. Churchill estimated that at least three-quarters of the members were determined not to be drawn into a European quarrel unless Britain herself were attacked. Against that current he pressed naval precautions. By Sunday 26 July Prince Louis of Battenberg, on Churchill’s instruction, had told the Fleet not to disperse after its practice mobilisation; at Grey’s and Tyrrell’s insistence the Admiralty published a communiqué that the First Fleet at Portland would not disperse for manœuvre leave. On 30 July Churchill obtained authority for the Fleet to pass the Dover Straits by night and take up war stations. When the German ultimatum to Belgium settled the issue for the majority of Asquith’s colleagues, formal sanction was given to completed naval mobilisation and to army mobilisation. Churchill sought authority to engage the Goeben if she attacked French transports; the Cabinet held that no act of war should precede the expiry of the British ultimatum—“The moral integrity of the British Empire,” he recorded, “must not be compromised at this solemn moment for the sake of sinking a single ship.” At eleven on the night of 4 August the war telegram “Commence hostilities against Germany” flashed to ships and establishments under the White Ensign; Churchill walked across the Horse Guards Parade and reported to the Prime Minister that the deed was done (The World Crisis, Vol. I; outbreak-of-the-great-war).

Opening Months of War: Antwerp, Oceans, Fisher

The passage of the Army, the crisis during the retreat from Mons, Antwerp, and the struggle for the Channel ports filled the autumn. Churchill’s personal mission to Antwerp, the Royal Naval Division’s part in the defence, and the fall of the city drew sharp Press criticism—the Morning Post called it “a costly blunder, for which Mr. Winston Churchill was primarily responsible”—while French’s despatch treated the enterprise as having delayed the enemy and facilitated the Belgian Army’s withdrawal (Annual Register, 1914). On the oceans, the destruction of Cradock’s squadron at Coronel and the despatch of Invincible and Inflexible to the South Atlantic ended in the annihilation of von Spee at the Falklands. Fisher returned as First Sea Lord in October 1914; Hankey’s biographer records that the combination of Fisher’s dynamism and Churchill’s strategic insight was not likely to accept a western deadlock tamely—yet the two already diverged over Baltic projects and over the Dardanelles (The World Crisis, Vol. I; Hankey, Man of Secrets, Vol. I).

Dardanelles, Resignation, and Return under Lloyd George

The naval attack on the Dardanelles, pressed in early 1915, divided counsel. Fisher was sceptical; Asquith later admitted “some doubt and hesitation” in Fisher’s mind; the Admiralty went ahead. By mid-May the relationship had broken over further reinforcements. Fisher resigned on 15 May; Corbett records that the resignations of Fisher and Churchill, coinciding with Coalition formation, closed the first phase of the naval war of 1915. Churchill left the Admiralty for the Chancellorship of the Duchy of Lancaster, defended himself in the Commons, and watched Balfour take the Admiralty in the reconstructed ministry. In July 1917 Lloyd George, long wishing to recall him, made him Minister of Munitions in the first reconstruction of the War Cabinet period (Annual Register, 1915, 1917; Corbett, Naval Operations, Vol. II; Hankey, Man of Secrets, Vol. I; first-coalition-1915; gallipoli).

Sources

Churchill’s own The World Crisis, Part I (1911–1914), is the densest narrative for Agadir, the Admiralty years, July–August 1914, Antwerp, and the opening war at sea; it is a participant’s book and is used here for sequence and official acts he records as First Lord, not as final judgment on every controversy. The Annual Register for 1908–1915 and 1917 supplies the contemporary parliamentary and electoral frame—Board of Trade and Home Office speeches, railway crisis, Admiralty appointment, Antwerp controversy, resignation and defence, and the 1917 return. Williamson’s Politics of Grand Strategy anchors the naval conversations, Mediterranean redistribution, sixty per cent standard, and Haldane Mission. Roskill’s Hankey: Man of Secrets, Vol. I, covers the CID circle, the Churchill–Fisher partnership, and the sulphurous War Council of May 1915. Corbett’s Naval Operations, Vol. II, marks the close of the first naval phase of 1915 with the double resignation.
