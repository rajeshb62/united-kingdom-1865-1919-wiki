Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948) — to 1919

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (2 October 1869 – 30 January 1948), known from the late 1910s as Mahatma Gandhi, was the foremost leader of the Indian community in South Africa (1893–1914) and, after his return to India in 1915, the rising force in Indian nationalist politics. Within the 1865–1919 period, Gandhi is significant as the pioneer of satyagraha — non-violent resistance — first developed against the discriminatory laws of the Transvaal and Natal, and subsequently applied in his early Indian campaigns at Champaran (1917) and Ahmedabad (1918). By 1919 he had become the most prominent opponent of the Rowlatt Acts and had begun to transform the Indian National Congress into a mass movement.

South African Lawyer & Activist

Gandhi arrived in South Africa in 1893 as a young barrister to represent an Indian trading firm in a legal dispute. What he encountered there — a system of racial discrimination directed against Indian merchants, indentured labourers, and their descendants — shaped the remainder of his life. He remained in South Africa for twenty-one years, building a legal practice in Durban and Johannesburg and emerging as the principal spokesman and organiser of the Indian population.

In 1894 he founded the Natal Indian Congress, the first permanent political organisation representing Indian interests in the colony. The Congress agitated against legislation designed to disenfranchise and marginalise the Indian community, including bills to restrict Indian trading rights and franchise. During the Second Boer War (1899–1902), Gandhi organised the Indian Ambulance Corps, a body of over a thousand Indian volunteers who served with British forces at the relief of Ladysmith and elsewhere. The corps’ service was cited as evidence of Indian loyalty to the Crown, though it did little to improve the community’s position when peace returned.

The system of indentured Indian labour — under which men and women were bonded to work on Natal’s sugar plantations for fixed terms — was the demographic foundation of the Indian presence in South Africa. As the Annual Register observed, the Indian community in Natal was “the fruit of the indenture system,” and it would become a primary constituency for Gandhi’s campaigns [source: annual-register-1908].

Satyagraha Campaigns

The Transvaal Registration Act (1906–1908)

The crisis that gave birth to satyagraha arose from the Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act of 1907, which required every Indian in the Transvaal to register and submit to fingerprinting of all ten digits — a requirement perceived as degrading and criminalising. “At the close of 1907 the situation was that the Indian population of the Transvaal refused to register under the new Act,” the Annual Register recorded. “Mr. Gandhi, the leader, and other Indians who had refused to register, were arrested and ordered to leave the Colony. To these orders they offered a ‘passive resistance,’ and raised the legal point that the Government had no power to put them over the border by force” [source: annual-register-1908].

Mass meetings were held in Johannesburg; the entire Indian population seemed resolved upon resistance. The Natal government objected to Indians being dumped across its border, and negotiations opened for transit to the coast. Lord Ampthill, president of the South Africa British Indian Committee, and Mr. L. W. Ritch, its secretary, agitated vigorously in England on the Indians’ behalf [source: annual-register-1908].

The Transvaal Colonial Secretary, General Jan Smuts, defended the Act: he claimed thousands of false permits were in circulation and that white opinion was “unanimous in demanding the cessation of the system.” The Act, he said, had driven 5,000 unauthorised Indians from the colony; 9,500 remained who refused to register, of whom 5,000 held trade licences. The Government would “show no mercy to the leaders” [source: annual-register-1908].

Gandhi offered to secure universal registration within a month if the registration form could be mutually agreed: the Indians maintained that a thumb-impression was sufficient and that the requirement of ten-digit prints — as for criminals — was offensive to Indian sentiment. He also demanded a judicial inquiry into allegations of surreptitious entry. Meanwhile deportation orders continued, their impact sharpened by the fact that some of the deported men had “fought with distinction under the Crown” in the late war [source: annual-register-1908].

The crisis reached its peak on 10 January 1908, when Gandhi and five other leaders were sentenced to two months’ imprisonment without hard labour. Thereafter “the daily news was of batches of other arrests, and wholesale deportations, those failing to comply being heavily fined or imprisoned” [source: annual-register-1908].

The 1908 Compromise and its Collapse

By February 1908, letters passed and interviews were held between Smuts and Gandhi. The outcome was a compromise: Indians could “voluntarily” register without ten-digit prints; children under sixteen were exempt. The Indian negotiators understood Smuts to have pledged that, if all Asiatics registered voluntarily, the 1907 Act would be repealed.

Voluntary registration proceeded freely — but the repeal never came. The Government introduced a new Bill “to validate the voluntary registration of certain Asiatics who failed to comply with the Act of 1907.” The Indians protested that the pledge had been broken. Smuts “denied emphatically that any such pledge had been given.” The Annual Register noted that “the text of the letters embodying the compromise contained no statement inconsistent with Mr. Smuts’s denial,” and that a letter signed by Gandhi and two other leaders “expressly noted Mr. Smuts’s repeated public declarations that there was no likelihood of the repeal of the Act” [source: annual-register-1908].

Smuts then added a further condition: the 1907 Act could be repealed only if the Immigrants Restriction Act were simultaneously amended to exclude all future Indian immigration — a policy that implied “in the long run the elimination of the Indian community.” The amended Act passed the Transvaal Legislature in a single day’s sitting. Lord Crewe, the Colonial Secretary, declined to advise the Crown to disallow it, accepting the view that the Act represented “the deliberate policy of the white community, which had been vested with the power of self-government” [source: annual-register-1908].

Renewal (1911)

Passive resistance was suspended in April 1911 after a fresh arrangement between Smuts and Gandhi. In return for abandoning resistance to the 1907 registration law, the Indians obtained an undertaking to repeal it. New legislation would rest on “equality to all immigrants, but differential treatment by the application of a language test” — the Australian model. The Indians reluctantly accepted a language test and gave Gandhi “a free hand in negotiating over the new Bill.” Concessions included the right of thirty deported Asiatics to return from India, the annual admission of six educated Indians without registration, and exemption from thumb-prints for educated Indians of repute. General Botha “reminded Asiatics of the determination of the Union not to admit further Indians except those specially coming under this settlement.” The settlement appeared to have “worked satisfactorily” for the remainder of 1911 [source: annual-register-1911].

The Final Campaign (1913)

The peace was short-lived. In 1913 the Union Government’s refusal to modify the racial bar in immigration legislation provoked a renewal of passive resistance. Mr. G. K. Gokhale, the Indian leader then visiting South Africa, complained that the Government had not carried out understandings he had reached with Ministers earlier in the year, and appealed to the Government of India to intervene [source: annual-register-1913].

In October the movement intensified. Early in November, Gandhi led 2,500 Indians through Laing’s Nek and past Majuba Hill towards Volksrust, where a vigilance committee had been formed to prevent his entry into the Transvaal by force. He was arrested on 6 November and sentenced at Dundee (Natal) to a month’s imprisonment for inducing Indians to leave the Province. A strike had broken out among Indian colliery workers, and for abetting this Gandhi was sentenced to nine months’ imprisonment. He received a further three months at Volksrust for violating the Immigration Act [source: annual-register-1913].

At the Volksrust court, Gandhi explained his strategy: he knew his actions meant “great risk and personal suffering for those who acted with him, but, after mature consideration and twenty years’ experience of South Africa, he had concluded that nothing short of suffering would move the conscience of the Government and of the people of the Union.” He claimed to be a law-abiding citizen [source: annual-register-1913].

The strike spread from the collieries to the sugar plantations of Natal, with serious disturbances at Ladysmith — the police were “roughly handled until they were provided with rifles and reinforced by citizens with revolvers and shot guns.” Simultaneously, 5,000 Indian workers in Durban went on strike. The Indians demanded the remission of the £3 annual poll tax on those whose indentures had expired, and the right to bring in one wife. Plantations in the Durban district were fired. By 19 November, 116 free and indentured Indians had been sentenced to seven days’ imprisonment, and fifty-four to fines [source: annual-register-1913].

This final wave of resistance, with its combination of labour strikes, mass marches, and coordinated civil disobedience, forced the Union Government to negotiate. The result was the Indian Relief Act of 1914, which abolished the £3 poll tax, recognised Indian marriages, and removed the most objectionable features of earlier legislation. Gandhi considered the campaign a qualified success and, his South African work concluded, left for England and then India in July 1914.

Return to India

Gandhi arrived back in India in January 1915, after twenty-one years abroad. He was welcomed as a hero by G. K. Gokhale and the moderate wing of the Indian National Congress, who saw in him a disciplined organiser capable of bridging the gap between the educated elite and the rural masses. He spent his first year travelling the country, studying conditions, and establishing Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad, his base of operations for the next decade and a half.

The Indian political landscape he returned to was in flux. The Indian National Congress had renewed its declarations of loyalty in 1915 as the war continued [source: annual-register-1915]. By July 1917 the Congress and the Moslem League had jointly demanded that the Imperial Government “pledge itself to the policy of making India a self-governing unit in the Empire” [source: annual-register-1917]. Annie Besant, the Irish-born advocate of Indian Home Rule, had been imprisoned for her agitation, and her release became a rallying cause. It was into this environment — of rising nationalist expectation constrained by imperial wartime security — that Gandhi began to assert his leadership.

Champaran & Ahmedabad

Champaran (1917)

Gandhi’s first major intervention in Indian politics was the Champaran indigo protests of 1917. In the Champaran district of Bihar, European planters had long compelled tenant farmers to grow indigo on a portion of their land under a system of exploitative contracts (tinkathia). Gandhi was invited by local peasants to investigate their grievances. Despite official attempts to have him expelled from the district, he refused to leave, accepted service of a summons, and pleaded guilty to a charge of disobeying orders to depart. His willingness to face imprisonment electrified the region. The Viceroy’s government, unwilling to create a martyr, eventually appointed a commission of inquiry — with Gandhi as a member — which found in favour of the peasants and led to the abolition of the tinkathia system.

Champaran established the template for Gandhi’s method: careful factual investigation of grievances, non-cooperation with unjust orders, a willingness to suffer imprisonment, and the mobilisation of public opinion through the press and mass meetings.

Ahmedabad (1918)

In February–March 1918, Gandhi took up the cause of cotton-mill workers in Ahmedabad who were demanding a 35 per cent wage increase to cope with wartime inflation. When the mill-owners refused to arbitrate, Gandhi led the workers in a hunger strike — his first use of this weapon in India — and fasted until the mill-owners agreed to a compromise settlement of a 35 per cent increase. The strike cemented his bond with the industrial working class and added another instrument to the Gandhian repertoire.

Later in 1918, Gandhi undertook a recruiting campaign for the British Indian Army in Kheda district (Gujarat), where he urged peasants to volunteer for service in the belief that such loyalty would be rewarded with post-war reforms. The campaign was a failure — few volunteered — and Gandhi later acknowledged it as a misjudgment.

Rowlatt & Post-War Politics

The Rowlatt Acts (1919)

The turning point in Gandhi’s relationship with the British Raj came with the Rowlatt Acts (the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act of 1919), which extended wartime emergency powers into peacetime — permitting trial without jury, internment without trial, and press censorship. Gandhi saw the Acts as an intolerable violation of civil liberties and a betrayal of the reformist promises contained in the Montagu-Chelmsford Report (1918), which had proposed gradual Indian self-government.

Gandhi organised a nationwide hartal — a general strike and day of fasting and prayer — for 30 March 1919 (later rescheduled to 6 April). The response was unprecedented: shops closed, markets were deserted, and processions filled the streets of Bombay, Delhi, Lahore, Ahmedabad, and Calcutta. Gandhi declared the beginning of satyagraha against the Rowlatt Acts, the first all-India campaign of non-violent resistance against British rule.

Jallianwala Bagh & the Aftermath

The hartal was largely peaceful in most places, but in the Punjab it sparked serious disturbances. In Amritsar, violence erupted on 10 April 1919 when a crowd, protesting the arrest of local leaders, clashed with troops and set fire to banks and government buildings. Five Europeans were killed. The authorities summoned Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer, who imposed a strict ban on public meetings.

On 13 April 1919, the day of the Baisakhi festival, thousands of unarmed men, women, and children gathered in the Jallianwala Bagh — a walled garden with a single narrow entrance — for a peaceful protest. Dyer arrived with fifty Gurkha and Baluchi riflemen and, without warning, ordered them to open fire. They continued firing for ten to fifteen minutes until the ammunition was nearly exhausted. Official estimates placed the dead at 379, though Indian sources put the figure much higher; over 1,200 were wounded. Dyer later stated he had acted to produce a “moral effect” throughout the Punjab.

News of the massacre spread rapidly despite censorship, and it produced a profound rupture between Indian public opinion and the Raj. Gandhi was staggered by the violence. He suspended the satyagraha campaign in mid-April, declaring that the movement had been disfigured by acts of mob violence that he could not countenance. He undertook a three-day fast as penance. But the damage was done: the Jallianwala Bagh massacre convinced Gandhi that the British Empire was fundamentally unreformable, and it set him on the path towards the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–22.

The Government of India Bill, enacting the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms (limited elected councils and Indian participation in provincial government), was passed in December 1919 — overshadowed and hollowed out by the events of April.

Legacy (to 1919)

By the close of 1919, Gandhi had completed a remarkable transformation. From a South African lawyer and community organiser, he had become the most recognisable mass leader in India. He had developed and tested the techniques — passive resistance, the hunger strike, the hartal, the disciplined march — that would define Indian nationalism for the next three decades. He had given the Indian National Congress a method that could mobilise peasants, workers, and the urban middle class alike. And he had arrived at the conviction, forged in Champaran and hardened at Jallianwala Bagh, that the Empire could not be reformed from within — that it would have to be confronted.

The Annual Register of 1915 had noted simply that “the Indian National Congress met again at the very end of December and renewed its declaration of loyalty.” Within four years, that loyalty had been shattered. The Gandhi who emerged from 1919 was no longer a petitioner for imperial justice; he was the architect of a movement that would, within a generation, end British rule in India.
