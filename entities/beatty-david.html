Admiral Sir David Beatty (1871–1936)

Admiral Sir David Beatty commanded the Battle Cruiser Fleet of the Grand Fleet from its formation in early 1915 through the great naval actions of the First World War — Heligoland Bight (1914), Dogger Bank (1915), and Jutland (1916) — and subsequently succeeded Sir John Jellicoe as Commander-in-Chief of the Grand Fleet in December 1916, a post he held until after the armistice. It fell to Beatty to receive the surrender of the German High Seas Fleet in November 1918 and to oversee its internment at Scapa Flow. A bold and aggressive commander, Beatty embodied the offensive spirit of the Royal Navy’s battle-cruiser arm, though the catastrophic loss of three battle-cruisers at Jutland shadowed his reputation.

Battle-Cruiser Actions (1914–1915)

Beatty first distinguished himself in the action off Heligoland on 28 August 1914. Commodore Reginald Tyrwhitt’s Harwich Force and submarines had drawn German light cruisers into battle, but by mid-morning the light cruiser Arethusa was heavily damaged and the situation was critical. Beatty, with his battle-cruiser squadron, had been stationed in reserve to the north-west. Hearing gunfire to the eastward, he made the difficult decision to plunge his heavy ships into the restricted waters near Heligoland against standing Admiralty caution. The Lion rushed in from the westward and immediately turned the tide. “Admiral Beatty reaped the fruit of his difficult decision. Having taken it he had held on after the light cruisers, until, just when they turned to close the beaten Mainz and finish her, there was a burst of firing away to the eastward, and he promptly steered for it” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol1]. Within minutes the German light cruisers Köln and Ariadne were sunk, and Beatty’s intervention transformed a confused skirmish into a decisive British victory. “Prompt as had been the action of the German Admiral, it was too late. Admiral Beatty had the speed of him and was in position to cut him off from Heligoland” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol1]. By 1.10 p.m. he made the general signal to retire, having achieved a striking success. The Annual Register recorded that at 12.30 “the Lion drove off and pursued a four-funnelled cruiser, the Köln … the Lion, after firing two salvoes at the German cruiser Ariadne, which disappeared into the mist, on fire and sinking, returned to the chase of the Köln, and sank her with all hands” [source: annual-register-1914].

On 24 January 1915, Beatty’s battle-cruisers fought the action off the Dogger Bank. Admiral Hipper’s German battle-cruisers had sortied to raid the English coast; Beatty intercepted them as they retired. In a running fight, the German armoured cruiser Blücher was sunk and the Seydlitz badly damaged. Beatty, flying his flag in the Lion, was himself hit and his flagship was forced to drop out of the line. “The unsatisfactory outcome of the Battle of Dogger Bank from the German point of view led to the supersession on 2nd Feb. of Admiral von Ingenohl by Admiral Hugo von Pohl as C-in-C, High Seas Fleet” [source: hankey-man-of-secrets-vol1]. The Annual Register noted that “the battle-cruiser action off the Dogger Bank had established the superiority of our guns to those of the Germans, and shown that our shooting was at least as good as theirs” [source: annual-register-1915]. The action confirmed Beatty’s battle-cruiser fleet as the Grand Fleet’s primary striking arm. After the action the Grand Fleet was reorganised: under Beatty, with the Lion as his flagship, the Battle Cruiser Fleet was established as a fast, self-contained force based at Rosyth, capable of independent offensive operations while the main battle fleet remained at Scapa Flow [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol2].

Jutland (1916)

The Battle of Jutland, fought on 31 May 1916 off the coast of Denmark, was the defining and most controversial episode of Beatty’s career. “The British battle-cruiser squadron, under the command of Admiral Sir David Beatty, was nearer the scene than the main battle-fleet under Sir John Jellicoe himself, and was also of course much faster, and hence it came up with the Germans long before” [source: annual-register-1916]. Beatty commanded six battle-cruisers — the Lion (flagship), Princess Royal, Queen Mary, Tiger, Indefatigable, and New Zealand — supported by the four fast battleships of the 5th Battle Squadron under Rear-Admiral Evan-Thomas.

The engagement opened shortly after 3.30 p.m. when Beatty sighted Hipper’s five battle-cruisers north-west of the Horn Reef. Within the first minutes the battle went catastrophically wrong for the British. “The battle opened badly, however, for the British, for within a few minutes of the commencement of the engagement between the two advanced squadrons both the Queen Mary and the Indefatigable were sunk” [source: annual-register-1916]. In each case a shell penetrated a magazine, causing a violent explosion that destroyed the ship almost instantly. “Curiously enough the loss of these two ships occurred, not when the British were fighting extremely heavy odds, as they subsequently were, but when the odds were somewhat in their favour” [source: annual-register-1916].

Despite these staggering losses, Beatty pressed his attack. “Sir David Beatty’s object was to get between von Scheer and his base, and thus compel him to fight a general action with Sir John Jellicoe. This manoeuvre gave the German Admiral the opportunity for which he was looking, that of dealing with a small detachment of the British fleet, a fact which was not lost upon Sir David Beatty, who knowingly took risks in order to force a general engagement” [source: annual-register-1916]. Hipper retreated eastwards towards Scheer’s main fleet, and Beatty followed, drawing the German battle fleet onto Jellicoe’s deploying battleships. Later in the action a third British battle-cruiser, the Invincible, also blew up, bringing the total loss in Beatty’s arm to three ships.

After the battle, Beatty was unequivocal about the central problem. “Neither the Commander-in-Chief nor Admiral Beatty had any hesitation in saying that the real significance of the battle was the destruction of the battle cruisers. It was, indeed, without historical precedent that a force of the first line, like Admiral Beatty’s battle cruiser fleet, should be so severely damaged by a numerically weaker squadron” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol4]. He asked that “a committee of scientific men should be sent to Rosyth without delay to investigate the causes of the disasters” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol4]. The subsequent investigation centred on inadequate armour protection and dangerous flash-management procedures in the magazines and handing-rooms.

Command of the Grand Fleet

In November 1916, with the submarine campaign intensifying and the Admiralty under growing political pressure, Jellicoe was called to Whitehall as First Sea Lord. “On November 22, just as he was about to take the fleet to sea for exercises, he received a telegram offering him the post of First Sea Lord, and asking him to take up his duties without delay; simultaneously, Admiral Beatty was offered the command of the Grand Fleet. Both offers were accepted” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol4]. Beatty strongly urged that Rear-Admiral Sir William Pakenham succeed him in command of the Battle Cruiser Force; “Admiral Pakenham’s experience of the work, Admiral Beatty’s views prevailed” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol4].

As Commander-in-Chief, Beatty confronted the most serious crisis of the war at sea. In late 1916 he warned that unrestricted submarine warfare was “jeopardising the fate of the nation and seriously interfering with the successful prosecution of the war” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol4]. He drove forward tactical and material reforms, including improvements in magazine safety, shell design, and gunnery practices that the Jutland experience had shown to be essential. Under his leadership the Grand Fleet maintained its crushing dominance over the North Sea, holding the German High Seas Fleet in check for the remainder of the war while the Royal Navy developed the convoy system to defeat the U-boat threat.

Surrender of the German Fleet

Beatty’s final and most symbolic act as Commander-in-Chief was to receive the surrender of the German High Seas Fleet. During the armistice negotiations in October 1918, Beatty was among the most uncompromising voices, urging terms that “included the surrender of virtually the whole High Seas Fleet” [source: hankey-man-of-secrets-vol1]. The Allied Naval Council ultimately agreed that “Admiral Beatty should enforce the execution of those clauses in the armistice which related to the surrender and internment of the German ships” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol5].

On 15 November, Rear-Admiral Hugo Meurer, the German plenipotentiary, was received on board Beatty’s flagship, the Queen Elizabeth, at Rosyth. Over three days of conferences the technical arrangements were settled. Beatty showed a measure of pragmatism: when Meurer pleaded that ships could not be readied owing to the disorganisation of German dockyards, Beatty agreed to substitutions [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol5].

On 21 November 1918, the German High Seas Fleet sailed into the Firth of Forth to surrender. “The Grand Fleet sailed at a quarter-past two on the morning of the 21st, and after passing into the open water outside the net obstructions it was formed into two gigantic columns, composed of no less than thirteen squadrons, and giving an almost illimitable vista of ordered power” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol5]. The light cruiser Cardiff led the German ships to their position between the columns of the Grand Fleet, and Beatty turned his fleet sixteen points to escort the enemy into harbour. The ships surrendered that day were the battleships Friedrich der Grosse (flagship of Rear-Admiral von Reuter), König Albert, Kaiser, Kronprinz Wilhelm, Kaiserin, Bayern, Markgraf, Prinzregent Luitpold, Grosser Kurfürst; the battle-cruisers Seydlitz, Derfflinger, Von der Tann, Hindenburg, Moltke; nine light cruisers; and forty-nine destroyers [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol5]. “When the last German ship was anchored, Admiral Beatty made a signal that the German flag was to be hauled down at sunset and that it must not again be rehoisted” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol5]. The Annual Register recorded that “Surrender off the Firth of Forth of five German battle cruisers, nine battleships, seven light cruisers, and forty-nine destroyers” took place on 21 November 1918 [source: annual-register-1918]. The ships were later moved to Scapa Flow, where on 21 June 1919 the German crews scuttled them. “All the German battleships and battle cruisers interned at Scapa Flow, with the exception of the battleship Baden and five light cruisers, had been sunk and abandoned by their crews” [source: annual-register-1919].

Legacy

Beatty’s career embodied the Royal Navy’s tradition of aggressive, offensive leadership. At Heligoland Bight and Dogger Bank he established himself as the premier battle-cruiser commander of his generation, a reputation for dash and decision that made him the natural successor to Jellicoe. Yet Jutland cast a long shadow. The loss of three battle-cruisers — Indefatigable, Queen Mary, and Invincible — was without precedent, and Beatty’s own observation that it was “without historical precedent that a force of the first line … should be so severely damaged by a numerically weaker squadron” [source: corbett-naval-ops-vol4] acknowledged a failure that technical inquiry traced to inadequate armour and unsafe magazine practices rather than any deficiency in personal courage. His willingness to press the pursuit of Hipper — knowingly accepting grave risks to force a general engagement — was consistent with his character but remains debated among naval historians.

As Commander-in-Chief of the Grand Fleet from December 1916 to the end of the war, Beatty presided over the fleet that kept the German surface navy contained and that finally received the surrender of the High Seas Fleet — a moment unprecedented since the Dutch Wars of the seventeenth century. The sight of the German ensigns being hauled down at sunset on 21 November 1918 marked the ultimate vindication of the naval strategy Britain had pursued since 1914. Beatty succeeded Jellicoe as First Sea Lord in 1919 and held the post until 1927, during which he oversaw the post-war contraction of the Royal Navy and the implementation of the Washington Naval Treaty. He was advanced to Admiral of the Fleet in 1919 and created Earl Beatty in the same year.
