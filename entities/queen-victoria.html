Queen Victoria (1819–1901)

Queen Victoria reigned as Sovereign of Great Britain and Ireland for sixty-four years, until her death on 22 January 1901 [source: annual-register-1901]. During the period 1865–1901 covered by this wiki, she exercised her constitutional role through successive ministries [source: mccarthy-history-vol4], presided over the expansion of the British Empire while the colonies looked up to her as their Queen-Mother [source: mccarthy-diamond-jubilee-vol1], and became the object of profound public reverence and affection, especially during her Golden and Diamond Jubilees [source: mccarthy-history-vol5-extended][source: mccarthy-diamond-jubilee-vol1].

Biography

Constitutional role and ministerial changes (1865–1880)

When Lord Russell’s Liberal ministry collapsed in June 1866 after the defeat of their Reform Bill, Russell and his colleagues tendered their resignation to the Queen, and after a little delay and some discussion the resignation was accepted. “The Queen, of course, sent for Lord Derby. There was no one else to send for. Somebody must carry on the Queen’s government” [source: mccarthy-history-vol4]. Lord Derby consented to relieve the sovereign and the country from difficulties by accepting the office of Prime Minister.

When Lord Derby later became gravely ill and determined to retire from office, he tendered his resignation, “and it was accepted by the Queen” [source: mccarthy-history-vol4].

In 1866, after the successful laying of the Atlantic telegraph cable, the Queen exchanged messages of congratulation with the President of the United States. The Queen congratulated the President upon “the successful completion of the great international work,” and was convinced that “the President will unite with her in fervently hoping that the electric cable which now connects Great Britain with the United States will prove an additional link between the nations whose friendship is founded in their common interest and reciprocal esteem” [source: mccarthy-history-vol4].

The Diamond Jubilee (1897)

The celebration of the Queen’s Diamond Jubilee in London in 1897 attracted great public attention. Unlike the Golden Jubilee procession of 1887, where “the Sovereigns and Princes of Europe and Asia were the most conspicuous figures,” the Diamond Jubilee procession was used “to show that semi-independent colonies and far-distant settlements in all parts of the globe looked up to the Sovereign of Great Britain as their Queen-Mother whose care and protection they cheerfully and loyally acknowledged.” After the “Empress Queen herself, the eyes and acclamations of the crowds which lined the route were directed to the Colonial Premiers, the Colonial troops, and the dark auxiliaries from our Asiatic and African dependencies” [source: mccarthy-diamond-jubilee-vol1].

Assessment of the reign (1897)

Looking back at her reign at the time of the Diamond Jubilee, McCarthy wrote that “the reign so far has given its measure, and no one can deny that on the whole it has been a reign of great success,” especially productive “of reform in political, in economical, and in social life,” with domestic reform and domestic advancement being particularly successful. “Never once during her time has the strength of the monarchy been shaken, or even threatened,” while many monarchies and republics abroad had fallen. “The monarchical system of Great Britain has not been seriously threatened in the slightest way since Queen Victoria came to the throne.” Though this was not “owing to any inspiration or any effort of the Queen herself,” it may be assumed “that the wisdom with which, as a constitutional sovereign, she discharged her duties, and acted in the end on the advice of her ministers, has had much to do with the stability of the Empire and the rule.” McCarthy concluded: “it would be unjust even to the history of the time not to give a word of praise to the steady, constitutional action of the sovereign” [source: mccarthy-history-vol5-extended].

Visit to Ireland (1900)

A memorable event in the closing years of Queen Victoria’s reign was her visit to Ireland in April 1900—the first in forty years. The visit “was projected by the Queen entirely of her own motion, and did not come from the promptings of any among her recognised official advisers,” who were disposed on the whole to discourage the project, fearing hostile demonstrations. “The Queen appeared to have made up her mind.” It seemed “that the Queen felt at heart a regret for the long estrangement between her and the Irish people.” To make the visit she gave up her annual visit to the South of France, taken to recruit her health in a climate which especially suited her [source: mccarthy-diamond-jubilee-vol2].

Her visit was preluded by the announcement of the Queen’s desire that on St. Patrick’s Day Irish soldiers in her service should wear the emblematic shamrock, “welcomed as a graceful act on the part of the Sovereign and as a recognition of the national feeling.” The Queen was received in Dublin “with every sign of respect, and even with a general expression of popular welcome.” She declared she had come “to this fair country to seek change and rest, and to revisit scenes which recall to my mind, among the thoughts of the losses which years must bring, the happiest recollection of a warm-hearted welcome given to me and my beloved husband and children” [source: mccarthy-diamond-jubilee-vol2].

No hostile demonstration interfered with the visit’s success. While the Irish National party leaders did not take part in the welcome, feeling they could not identify with demonstrations “to a ruler who, whatever her own personal feelings, was the representative of a power which had of late only made itself known in Ireland by the enforcement of exceptional penal laws,” there was “no hostile feeling to the Queen in person among the whole Irish population.” The visit “may, under all the circumstances, be regarded as a success,” creating throughout the country “a common and sincere desire that the Queen might again come to Ireland” [source: mccarthy-diamond-jubilee-vol2].

Final months (1900–1901)

In the early months of 1901, the opening of the new century was “clouded by a national sorrow altogether exceptional, alike in its intensity and in its diffusion.” The Queen’s “keen and watchful sympathy with the joys and sorrows, the cares and the hopes of all parts of” the Empire were “as vividly realised in the first few days of the new age as at any former period in her prolonged and gloriously eventful reign.” In a brief message of “characteristic simplicity and directness,” she conveyed through Lord Hopetoun her “heartfelt interest” in the inauguration of the Australian Commonwealth on 1 January 1901, and her “earnest wish that, under Divine Providence, it might ensure the increased prosperity and well-being of her loyal and beloved subjects in Australia” [source: annual-register-1901].

During 1900, the intensity of her participation in the South African anxieties of the nation, “the pains she took to show herself on many occasions to large numbers of the people, and the spirit in which she conceived” her role, further enhanced the hold she possessed upon the reverence and affection of her people [source: annual-register-1901].

In the closing weeks of her life, Lord Roberts, returning from South Africa, visited the Queen at Osborne, and she conferred upon him an earldom and the Order of the Garter [source: annual-register-1901].

Illness and death (January 1901)

The German Emperor travelled from the celebrations of the two-hundredth anniversary of the foundation of the Prussian Monarchy “as quickly as possible to reach the bedside of his Royal grandmother at Osborne.” The whole civilised world “seemed, and indeed was, united with the British race in their passionate craving for the preservation of the life of the venerable Monarch, whose influence was universally recognised as having been directed for sixty years and more, with a single eye, to the good of her own vast dominions, and through them to that of mankind at large.” The “slight rally” was not maintained. By four o’clock on Tuesday afternoon, 22 January, the Queen was announced to be sinking, having earlier in the day recognised the several members of her family, including the German Emperor, and at half-past six that evening she passed away [source: annual-register-1901].

“It is quite impossible to exaggerate the sense of bereavement which oppressed the whole British nation and Empire. To her subjects of every rank Queen Victoria’s death meant the removal of what had appeared an essential feature in the framework of life.” The feeling of forlornness which pervaded the country in its diffusion and depth was “of a kind such as has not been known in England since the death of King Alfred a thousand years before.” A beneficent and inspiring presence had disappeared, “one which seemed to come nearer to the British people of every grade individually and in their families than any personality outside their own immediate circles” [source: annual-register-1901].

The funeral pageant

The Queen’s body lay at Osborne, then was taken by gun-carriage to Trinity Pier at Cowes, placed aboard the Alberta, which slowly steamed through an avenue of British and foreign men-of-war to Portsmouth Harbour. The body was conveyed by train to London, then on a khaki-coloured gun-carriage in a great procession “by Buckingham Palace, St. James’s Street, Piccadilly and Hyde Park to Paddington,” lined by over 30,000 troops, with a column “representative of every branch of the land and sea forces of the Crown” stretching a mile or more. Behind the coffin rode the King in field-marshal’s uniform, with the German Emperor on his right. From Paddington the body was conveyed to Windsor, and the funeral service performed in St. George’s Chapel [source: annual-register-1901].

Accession of King Edward VII

The day after the Queen’s death, the King left Osborne for London, held his first Council at St. James’s Palace, and a proclamation was approved declaring the accession of King Edward VII, bearing ninety-one signatures. The King delivered a declaration: “My first and melancholy duty is to announce to you the death of my beloved mother the Queen… I need hardly say that my constant endeavour will be always to walk in her footsteps” [source: annual-register-1901].

Tributes

President McKinley of the United States ordered the flag on the White House half-masted and addressed a telegram expressing “the profound sorrow” with which the President had received the death of her Majesty the Queen, “whose noble life and beneficent influence have promoted peace and won the affection of the world” [source: annual-register-1901].

Ex-President Harrison of the United States affirmed that “no other death could have excited such general sorrow” [source: annual-register-1901].

Lord Salisbury, the Prime Minister, moving an address in the House of Lords, said the late Queen reigned “by sheer force of character and by the lovableness of her disposition over the hearts of her subjects, and exercised a greater influence in moulding their destinies than she could have done even if she had been invested with the most despotic power. She had been a great instance of government by example, by esteem, and by love.” As a Constitutional Monarch, “her Majesty always maintained and practised a rigorous supervision over public affairs, giving to her Ministers her frank advice, and warning them of danger if she saw there was danger ahead. She had an extraordinary power of divining what her people, and especially, Lord Salisbury thought, those of the middle classes, would think. Yet she never adhered to her own conception obstinately, but, on the contrary, she was full of concession and consideration.” Lord Salisbury concluded that future historians “will look to the Queen’s reign as the boundary which separates the two states of England” and recognise “that we have undergone” great change “so peaceably, so easily, and with so much prosperity and success” [source: annual-register-1901].

Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman alluded to “a certain homely sincerity of character and life and purpose” in Queen Victoria “which, amid all the pomp and dignity of her august position, seemed to make the whole world kin.” Between the Queen and her people existed “a friendly, tender, almost familiar mutual understanding, which it is almost impossible to put into words.” He held the Queen’s reign to have been “the most beneficent that had been seen in any nation and in any age of the world” [source: annual-register-1901].

Lord Rosebery said: “Probably every subject in Great Britain realises that he has lost his greatest and his best friend. But they do not understand of what enormous weight in the councils of the world we are deprived by the death of our late Sovereign. She gave to the councils of Great Britain an advantage which no talents, no brilliancy, no genius, could supply. She had reigned for sixty-three years. For sixty-three years she had known all that was to be known about the political condition of her country. For sixty-three years she had been in communication” [source: annual-register-1901].

The King’s message to Parliament stated: “Her devotion to the welfare of her country and her people and her wise and beneficent rule during the sixty-four years of her glorious reign will ever be held in affectionate memory by her loyal and devoted subjects throughout the dominions of the British Empire” [source: annual-register-1901].

Imperial and worldwide mourning

Within the English-speaking parts of the British Empire, grief manifested was of the profoundest quality. “Reverent devotion to the person of the Queen had been one of the main welding influences by which the self-governing Colonies, as they grew in population, wealth and importance, were drawn into increasingly close touch with the mother country.” Remarkable also were manifestations of respectful sorrow exhibited “by the various non-British communities within the allegiance of the British Crown. Certain it is that among the French Canadians, among the Cape Dutch, and even the Boer prisoners, among the peoples of India, of the most diverse creeds and races, among the Maoris of New Zealand, and many other tribes of Asiatic, African and Polynesian blood, the signs of mourning bore every mark of genuineness.” “One pulse of sorrow seemed to be uniting the whole Empire” [source: annual-register-1901].
