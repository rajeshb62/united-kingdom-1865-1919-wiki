Sir Edward Grey (1862–1933)

Parliamentary Under-Secretary, Foreign Office (1892–1895) · Foreign Secretary (1905–1916)

Sir Edward Grey held the Foreign Office from the formation of Campbell-Bannerman’s Liberal government in December 1905 until the fall of Asquith in December 1916—the longest continuous tenure of any British Foreign Secretary in the period of this wiki. He inherited the Entente Cordiale with France, authorised the military staff conversations that grew from the first Moroccan crisis, concluded the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, managed the Bosnian and Agadir crises and the London Ambassadors’ Conference of the Balkan Wars, and on 3 August 1914 stated the case for war to the House of Commons before the British ultimatum on Belgian neutrality expired. Contemporaries credited him with force of character, elegance of expression, and a liberal moral view of obligation; critics on the radical left and among Unionists charged that the entente system had entangled Britain without the safeguards of a formal alliance. By 1907, in the judgment of The Politics of Grand Strategy, preservation of the Entente Cordiale had become the cardinal feature of his diplomacy.

Liberal Imperialist and Accession (1905–1906)

Grey belonged to the Liberal Imperialist wing associated with Asquith and Haldane—the group that had parted from the main stream of Liberal opinion over the South African War yet returned to office under Campbell-Bannerman. Before the 1906 landslide he spoke against Unionist fiscal fog, for amendment of the Education Act, and for continuity of foreign engagements. In October 1905, already regarded as the next Foreign Secretary, he told J. A. Spender that Britain was “running a real risk of losing France and not gaining Germany,” and in a City speech outlined the main tenets of future Liberal foreign policy: the Japanese alliance and friendship with the United States first, then the maintenance of the French understanding (Politics of Grand Strategy; Annual Register, 1905).

When he took office the Algeciras Conference was in its throes. Campbell-Bannerman authorised him to support France strongly and, almost as a first act of an era of Peace, Retrenchment, and Reform, the beginning of military conversations between the British and French General Staffs. Churchill later stressed the profundity of that step: the minds of the military men were turned into a particular channel; mutual trust grew in one set of relationships and mutual precautions in the other (The World Crisis, Vol. I). Williamson records Grey’s letter to Haldane of 8 January 1906 that a situation might arise in which popular feeling would compel help to France, and Fisher was ready to drive the German fleet off the sea; Grey wanted the War Office preparing an answer. Official staff talks followed—Grierson and Huguet, then wider channels—while Grey insisted that the conversations created no formal alliance obligation (Politics of Grand Strategy).

Grey was the first Foreign Secretary to sit in the Commons since Lord Stanley in 1868. He arranged to answer questions on Tuesdays and Thursdays, to attend whenever foreign business was before the House, and to leave other days to a parliamentary under-secretary—an informal adjustment to combine office with the chamber (Annual Register, 1906). He told the country that the Government intended to keep, in letter and spirit, the engagements entered into by its predecessor, and he defended the refusal to aid Chinese labour recruiting under improper conditions as a moral limit on the use of diplomatic machinery (Annual Register, 1906).

Entente Consolidation and the Triple Understanding (1906–1908)

Through 1906–1907 Grey held the French entente against intermittent hopes of better relations with Berlin. Anti-German direction in the Foreign Office, strengthened by Eyre Crowe’s promotion, and French protests against any Anglo-German flirtation drove him closer to Paris; he assured Cambon that if a subject like Algeciras arose again, French support would be as firm as before (Politics of Grand Strategy). He had long wanted a settlement with Russia—to restore or overturn the balance against Germany, to check the Kaiser’s flirtations with the Tsar, and to quiet German intrigues in Persia and the Near East. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 divided Persia into spheres, recognised Afghanistan as outside the Russian sphere, and affirmed Chinese suzerainty over Tibet. Grey defended the Agreement in the Commons as a measure that would “make for peace”: without it Anglo-Russian relations must have got worse; an opportunity had been seized which might not have returned (Annual Register, 1908). The Dual Alliance, the Entente Cordiale, and the Convention of 1907 formed the triangle later called the Triple Entente—understandings, not a formal triple alliance.

Crises: Bosnia, Agadir, and the Naval Entente (1908–1912)

Bosnia-Herzegovina (1908–1909)

Austria-Hungary’s annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina tested the new alignment. Grey laboured to restrain Serbia, pacify Turkey, and give full diplomatic support to Russia while making clear that Britain would not be drawn into war on a Balkan quarrel. The crisis ended when Germany insisted that Russia advise Serbia to give way and the Powers recognised the annexation without a conference. The Annual Register recorded that the attitude of Germany and Austria-Hungary and the recalcitrance of Servia seemed to portend a great European war and emphasised the need of British attention to national defence. Grey had already, on 5 November 1908, alerted the Admiralty to prepare in case Germany sent France an ultimatum and the Cabinet decided that Britain must assist France (The World Crisis, Vol. I; Politics of Grand Strategy; Annual Register, 1909; eastern-question-balkans).

Agadir (1911)

The Panther’s arrival at Agadir in July 1911 transformed the Moroccan question into an international crisis. The Cabinet resolved to remain faithful to the 1904 obligations to France and to oppose German demands for tripartite negotiations, but firmly refused to send a British ship to Morocco. Grey stood between the Foreign Office’s pro-French pressure and the radical caution of Loreburn, Morley, and others—patient, wary, and persistently evasive in Williamson’s phrase, trying to bridge Cabinet and Office. Lloyd George’s Mansion House speech of 21 July, aligned with the opposite wing of the Government on foreign policy, was the “thunder-clap” that convinced Berlin Britain would stand by France. When Count Metternich demanded that Lloyd George be turned out of the Government, Grey replied that if that was the request the conversation must end. The secret defence meetings of Asquith, Grey, McKenna, Lloyd George, and Churchill managed the crisis; by November Asquith, Grey, and Haldane had resolved in principle to aid France if Germany attacked—Haldane telling Asquith that if Britain failed France he would not give ten years’ purchase for the British Empire (Politics of Grand Strategy; Hankey, Man of Secrets, Vol. I; The World Crisis, Vol. I).

Naval Conversations and Grey–Cambon Letters

From 1911–1912 Anglo-French naval staff conversations complemented the military talks: French concentration in the Mediterranean, British guard of the Channel and North Sea. The exchange of letters between Grey and Cambon in November 1912 defined consultation and “moral obligation” without converting the Entente into a formal alliance—language that remained central when war came. Grey’s management of the diplomatic consequences of the naval race sought detente without sacrificing France; he could not accept a German demand for unconditional British neutrality as the price of slowing Tirpitz’s programme (Politics of Grand Strategy).

Balkan Wars and the London Conference (1912–1913)

During the Balkan Wars Grey chaired the Ambassadors’ Conference of the Great Powers in London (December 1912–August 1913), working to localise the conflict, prevent a general European war, and secure an independent Albania that would deny Serbia an Adriatic port Austria-Hungary was determined to prevent. British policy, he stated, was to consolidate Turkish authority in Asiatic dominions behind the Enos-Midia line, resting on sound finance, reforms, and the consent of the Powers. The Earl of Crewe praised his reputation for straightforwardness and candour as a diplomatic asset. He worked with France and Russia while maintaining communication with Germany and Austria—the Concert method under Entente conditions (Annual Register, 1913; eastern-question-balkans).

July 1914 and British Entry into the War

The July Crisis found the Cabinet still deep in the Irish boundary question when Grey brought the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia into the room on 24 July. He held two fixed points: the obligation involved in Belgian neutrality, and the danger if France were left to face Germany alone. With Tyrrell he pressed the Admiralty communiqué that held the Fleet together. On 3 August, after the German ultimatum to Belgium, he made his statement in the Commons; that afternoon, outside the Chamber, Churchill asked what would happen next. “Now,” Grey said, “we shall send them an ultimatum to stop the invasion of Belgium within 24 hours.” Asquith later moved the Vote of Credit with tribute to Grey’s unremitting efforts for peace, quoting the German Chancellor’s appeal for British neutrality as infamous. The war, Asquith said, had been forced upon Britain—to fulfil a solemn international obligation and to vindicate the principle that small nationalities were not to be crushed by arbitrary power (The World Crisis, Vol. I; Annual Register, 1914; outbreak-of-the-great-war).

Grey remained Foreign Secretary through the first Coalition and the early war years, corresponding through the United States Ambassador on the treatment of submarine prisoners and other wartime diplomacy, until Asquith’s fall in December 1916 ended the ministry in which he had served for eleven years (Annual Register, 1915–1916).

Domestic Voice

Though foreign affairs were his office, Grey spoke on the industrial unrest of the pre-war years. At Darlington in October 1910 he ascribed restlessness among wage-earners to great hopes combined with consciousness of power, and thought foreign politics would not be the main consideration of the twentieth century as economic interdependence increased. In 1912 he wound up for the Government on the Minimum Wage Bill: the House faced a new demand that the miner be paid a fair wage when he failed to earn it from causes beyond his control; carried to its extreme, the strike meant starvation and the destruction of national life (Annual Register, 1910, 1912). His 1911 speech on Anglo-American arbitration was cordially received on both sides of the Atlantic (Annual Register, 1911).

Sources

Williamson’s The Politics of Grand Strategy is the structural guide to Grey’s role in military and naval conversations, Entente consolidation, Agadir, and the Grey–Cambon letters. Churchill’s The World Crisis, Vol. I, supplies the Cabinet and Admiralty vantage on Algeciras, Agadir, and the July days, including the exchange outside the Commons on the Belgian ultimatum. The Annual Register year by year records his public defences—Anglo-Russian Convention, arbitration, Balkan conference line, 3 August 1914, and wartime FO business. Roskill’s Hankey places him in the CID and secret defence circle. Langer’s diplomacy volumes frame the Eastern Question inheritance against which Grey’s Balkan management is read.
