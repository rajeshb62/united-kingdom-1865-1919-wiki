Field Marshal Lord Horatio Herbert Kitchener (1850–1916)

Commander of the Nile Expedition (1896–1898) · Chief of Staff, South Africa (1899–1900) · Commander-in-Chief, India (1902–1909) · Secretary of State for War (1914–1916) · Field Marshal (1909) · 1st Earl Kitchener of Khartoum (1914)

Sudan Campaign & Omdurman

Kitchener’s greatest military achievement before the Great War was the reconquest of the Sudan and the destruction of the Dervish power. As Sirdar (Commander-in-Chief) of the Egyptian Army, he meticulously prepared for a campaign to avenge the death of General Charles Gordon at Khartoum in 1885.

Early in 1898, Sir Herbert Kitchener, “the keenest observer of events within his sphere, believed he had good reason to know that the Dervishes were cautiously but steadily making preparations for a disturbing movement.” (McCarthy, Diamond Jubilee, Vol. 1) He sent for large reinforcements from Cairo, and “before many weeks he had organised a powerful military force on the banks of the Nile.” The Sudan Railway aided British movements, and in early April 1898 the Anglo-Egyptian army attacked the entrenched camp of the Dervishes, which held a force numbering nearly 10,000 men. Kitchener’s army “killed at least 3,000 Dervishes, captured 3,000 more, and made the leader of the Dervish band, Mahmoud, a prisoner.” (McCarthy)

This victory spurred Kitchener to prepare for the final confrontation. He resolved to make Khartoum “the point of his final attack.” The Khalifa’s forces numbered more than 60,000, “including in their mass some of the most warlike tribes known to that region, and were, it was said, well supplied with artillery.” The English troops numbered only some 23,000 men. (McCarthy)

On 2 September 1898, the decisive Battle of Omdurman was fought. The Dervishes came out to meet the British troops, but “after more than one long and desperate fight the skill and tactics of the British force, with an unfailing discipline and unbroken courage, accomplished a complete success over their enemies.” The Dervish force underwent “a complete defeat, and had to disperse and fly from the scene of action. The flight soon became an utter rout.” (McCarthy)

Kitchener entered Omdurman a triumphant conqueror, and on Sunday, 4 September, a thanksgiving service was held within Khartoum, where “the British and the Egyptian flag were displayed side by side over the shattered citadel where Gordon had closed his gallant and devoted life.” The victory was accepted in England as “the destruction of the Dervish power and the re-conquest for civilisation of the whole of the Egyptian Soudan.” (McCarthy)

Winston Churchill, who was present as a young lieutenant in the 21st Lancers, later recalled that he had been sent back “to report verbally to the Commander-in-Chief the position of the advancing Dervish Army.” Kitchener, Churchill wrote, “had disapproved of me severely in my youth, had endeavoured to prevent me from coming to the Sudan Campaign, and was indignant that I had succeeded in getting there. It was a case of dislike before first sight.” (Churchill, World Crisis, Vol. 1)

Following his victory, Kitchener proceeded up the Nile to Fashoda, where he confronted Captain Jean-Baptiste Marchand’s French expedition. “Lord Kitchener acted with his usual promptitude” and “at once went up the Nile with a flotilla of gun-boats and a military force,” informing Marchand that the territory belonged to Egypt. The confrontation brought Britain and France to the brink of war before the French government backed down. (McCarthy; Politics & Grand Strategy)

Kitchener returned to England to a national welcome “which was only in proportion with the splendid services he had rendered, his marvellous foresight, his patient organisation of details, and the efforts he had always made that there should be as little waste of human life on either side as was possible.” He received from Queen Victoria the title of Lord Kitchener of Khartoum. He also raised £100,000 to found the Gordon Memorial College in Khartoum, “the noblest and the most appropriate monument to the memory of the hero Gordon.” (McCarthy)

South African War & Indian Service

When the South African War broke out in 1899, Kitchener was appointed Chief of Staff to Field Marshal Lord Roberts, who became Commander-in-Chief in South Africa in December 1899. After Roberts’s recall in 1900, Kitchener succeeded him as Commander-in-Chief in South Africa, overseeing the difficult final phase of the war against Boer guerrilla resistance. (McCarthy, Diamond Jubilee, Vol. 2) His implementation of the scorched earth policy and the establishment of concentration camps remain deeply controversial aspects of his career.

From 1902 to 1909, Kitchener served as Commander-in-Chief in India, where he undertook a thorough reorganisation of the Indian Army. In 1907, the Committee of Imperial Defence declined to rule upon Indian defence needs “without the opinion of Lord Kitchener, head of British forces in India,” and Prime Minister Asquith ordered the Indian Secretary to sound Kitchener on the military situation. (Politics & Grand Strategy) His reforms created an army capable of contributing significantly to the Great War effort.

He was promoted to Field Marshal in 1909 and served as Agent and Consul-General in Egypt from 1911 to 1914. During this period, he “made a tour of the Sudan in January” 1914, and his report, written “with fresh knowledge,” surveyed the state of the territory he had conquered sixteen years earlier. (Annual Register 1914)

Secretary for War (1914–1916)

At the outbreak of the Great War, Prime Minister Asquith was also serving as Secretary of State for War but “could not possibly be burdened with the continuous flow of inter-departmental work.” He therefore “invited Lord Kitchener to undertake ministerial charge of the War Office, and the Field-Marshal, who had certainly not sought this post in any way, had no choice but to accept.” (Churchill, World Crisis, Vol. 1)

Churchill noted that “my relations with Lord Kitchener had been limited” before the war. “I was glad when he was appointed Secretary of State for War, and in those early days we worked together on close and cordial terms. He consulted me constantly on the political aspects of his work, and increasingly gave me his confidence in military matters.” (Churchill)

“The appointment of Earl Kitchener as a non-political War Minister” was noted as a significant departure, and “was followed by rumours of the impending establishment of a coalition Government.” (Annual Register 1914) In his maiden speech in the House of Lords on 26 August 1914, Kitchener declared that “as a soldier, he had no politics, and his term as War Secretary was that of the new Army — for the war, but not for longer than three years.” He reported that mobilisation “had taken place without a hitch” and that the Expeditionary Force “proved itself wholly efficient, thoroughly well equipped, and immediately ready to take the field.” (Annual Register 1914)

The New Armies

Kitchener’s most transformative contribution was the creation of the New Armies (popularly known as “Kitchener’s Armies” ). Almost alone among British leaders, he foresaw a long war requiring mass armies. Churchill recorded that when Kitchener joined the Cabinet:

“Lord Kitchener now came forward to the Cabinet, on almost the first occasion after he joined us, and in soldierly sentences proclaimed a series of inspiring and prophetic truths. Every one expected that the war would be short; but wars took unexpected courses, and we must now prepare for a long struggle. Such a conflict could not be ended on the sea or by sea-power alone. It could be ended only by great battles on the Continent. In these the British Empire must bear its part on a scale proportionate to its magnitude and power. We must be prepared to put armies of millions in the field and maintain them for several years.” (Churchill, World Crisis, Vol. 1)

Churchill believed that had Kitchener “proceeded to demand universal national service, his request would have been acceded to.” However, Kitchener “proposed to content himself with calling for volunteers, and in the first instance to form six new regular divisions.” The new Secretary of State “had little knowledge of and no faith in the British territorial system,” having witnessed the failure of French Territorial troops during the Franco-Prussian War. (Churchill)

“He set himself to create the cadres first of six, then of twelve, and ultimately of twenty-four ‘Kitchener Army’ divisions, at the same time that the recruits were pouring in upon him by the hundred thousand. That this vast feat of improvisation was accomplished must certainly rank among the wonders of the time.” (Churchill, World Crisis, Vol. 1)

The famous recruiting poster featuring Kitchener’s pointing finger and the words “YOUR COUNTRY NEEDS YOU” became one of the enduring images of the war. Within months, hundreds of thousands of volunteers flocked to the colours. By August 1915, Lord Kitchener could inform the House of Commons that “the results of recruiting during the last few months had been most satisfactory; and that the numbers were being maintained with surprising regularity.” (Annual Register 1915)

The Shell Crisis

By early 1915, the immense scale of modern industrial warfare had exposed critical shortages in munitions. The “Shell Crisis” of May 1915 forced a political crisis that led to the formation of the first Coalition Government under Asquith.

In the Army Estimates debate, Lord Kitchener made the frank admission that “the output is not equal to our necessities.” (Annual Register 1915) This statement caused deep alarm, with critics in Parliament charging that the Government had “proclaiming our shortage of munitions from the housetops” and calling for better news management: “Let the country know of reverses as well as successes; people can stand the truth.” (Annual Register 1915)

A few days after this debate, a War Office Armaments Output Committee was appointed, consisting of Lord Kitchener; Major-General Sir Stanley B. von Donop; Sir Herbert J. Walker, Chairman of the Railways Executive Committee; Sir Algernon Firth; Mr. George M. Booth; and Mr. Allan M. Smith. “The functions of the Committee were to arrange for the increase in the production of armament and munitions of war.” (Annual Register 1915)

The crisis contributed to the formation of a Ministry of Munitions under David Lloyd George, which removed responsibility for arms production from the War Office. Kitchener’s authority was effectively diminished as a result, though he remained Secretary of State for War. In a War Council meeting at 10 Downing Street on 29 October 1915, Kitchener, Asquith, Lloyd George and Balfour met with General Joffre to discuss strategic coordination. (Annual Register 1915) Kitchener also undertook a mission to the Eastern Mediterranean in November–December 1915, and visited Athens in November 1915 “as an official emissary of the British Government” to warn King Constantine of Greece against pro-German neutrality. (Annual Register 1915)

Death at Sea

By June 1916, Kitchener’s public standing remained immense despite the political eclipse of his authority. Accepting an invitation from Tsar Nicholas II to visit Russia for consultations, he embarked on the evening of 5 June 1916 from the north of England aboard H.M.S. Hampshire.

The Annual Register recorded the disaster:

“On June 5 there occurred what was perhaps the greatest disaster suffered by the country in the course of the year. Lord Kitchener, on the invitation of the Tsar of Russia, left the north of England on that day in H.M.S. Hampshire. At about 8 o’clock in the evening, the Hampshire struck a mine to the west of the Orkneys and sank. She had been accompanied on her voyage by two destroyers up to about an hour before the explosion occurred, but they had then been detached owing to the very heavy seas which were running. The ship sank within ten minutes. Destroyers and patrol vessels were immediately despatched to the scene, but in spite of every measure that could be taken, the boats which put off from the Hampshire were wrecked and lost, and only twelve survivors reached the shore on a raft.” (Annual Register 1916)

“The death of Lord Kitchener was deeply felt by all parties and persons throughout the Empire.” On 13 June, a memorial service was held at St. Paul’s Cathedral, at which the King and Queen were present, with other services at Westminster Abbey and across the country. “On the re-opening of Parliament the first concern of both Houses was to place on record their sense of the great loss which the country had sustained.” In the House of Lords, Lord French “related Lord Kitchener’s unsparing efforts to supply all his demands while he was Commander-in-Chief of the Army in France. So high was his estimate of Lord Kitchener as a soldier that when he was originally selected for the chief command in the field, he had urged Lord Kitchener to see the Prime Minister and arrange to take his place, Lord French himself acting as Chief-of-the-Staff.” In the House of Commons, a resolution was moved by Asquith and seconded by Bonar Law “that a national monument be erected to Lord Kitchener.” (Annual Register 1916)

Churchill, reflecting on Kitchener’s death, wrote poignantly:

“Both these two men were marked for death at the hands of the enemy, the young Minister grappling with his adversary in the high air, the old Field-Marshal choking in the icy sea.” (World Crisis, Vol. 1 — referring to Kitchener and Lord Lucas)

Legacy

Lord Kitchener was the most iconic British military figure of the first half of the Great War. His face, staring from recruiting posters with the command “Your Country Needs You,” became the visual symbol of national mobilisation. His strategic foresight in anticipating a war of years rather than months, and his creation of the New Armies of volunteers, laid the foundations for the mass citizen army that would ultimately win the war.

McCarthy’s contemporary account captured the national sentiment: Kitchener’s “marvellous foresight, his patient organisation of details, and the efforts he had always made that there should be as little waste of human life on either side as was possible” earned him “a national welcome which was only in proportion with the splendid services he had rendered.” (McCarthy, Diamond Jubilee, Vol. 1)

His tenure as Secretary of State for War was marked by immense achievements — the raising of thirty divisions of volunteer soldiers — and by equally immense strains: the shell crisis, the Dardanelles controversy, and the growing tension between his autocratic style and the demands of democratic war-making. Hankey’s committee work during the early 1900s had already revealed the tensions between the War Office and the Admiralty that would mark the first two years of the war. (Hankey, Man of Secrets, Vol. 1)

Lord Curzon, in a House of Lords debate on the voluntary system, noted the pressure on the War Office and urged “preliminary steps with a view to preventing chaos if it should become necessary later on to institute compulsory service.” (Annual Register 1915) Kitchener remained committed to voluntarism throughout his time at the War Office, resisting full conscription until the Military Service Act of 1916 was forced upon the Government.

His sudden death removed from the direction of the war the one figure whose prestige could command unquestioning public trust. The shock of his loss — a Field Marshal and War Minister drowned on a mission to Russia — sent reverberations through British society and remains one of the most dramatic single events of the war. The uncertainty surrounding the sinking of the Hampshire — whether it struck a drifting mine or was deliberately targeted by a U-boat — fuelled conspiracy theories that persist to this day.
