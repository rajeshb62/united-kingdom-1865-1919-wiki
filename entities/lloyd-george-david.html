David Lloyd George (1863–1945)

Chancellor of the Exchequer (1908–1915) · Minister of Munitions (1915–1916) · Prime Minister (1916–1922) · Leader of the Liberal Party

Early Career & the People’s Budget (1890–1911)

David Lloyd George entered Parliament in 1890 at the age of twenty-seven, not as a labour representative but as “the chosen mouthpiece of the working middle class, enthusiastic for Welsh nationalism.” A product of the industrious Nonconformist middle class, he came from a background untouched by wealth or distinguished family. In youth, full of the fervour of Mazzini’s democratic teaching, he dreamed of Wales as a nation, even a republic, with himself perhaps as its first president. Welsh nationalism could not breed a Home Rule party as Irish nationalism had done, however, and Lloyd George found greater scope for his talents in the Liberal Party. During the ten years of Conservative ascendancy (1895–1905) he fought his way into the front rank of the party, pitting himself more than once against Joseph Chamberlain and winning general acknowledgment for his powers as a parliamentary debater. (Clayton, The Rise of the Democracy)

With his entry into the Cabinet in 1905, alongside John Burns, government by aristocracy was effectively ended. When Lloyd George went from the Board of Trade to the Chancellorship of the Exchequer in 1908, “startling changes were predicted in national finance” — predictions that were fulfilled in the Budget of 1909. (Clayton)

The “People’s Budget” of 1909 was the great engine of the constitutional crisis that followed. It was described in the Annual Register as a Budget that “took a step” towards systematic social reform through radical taxation — including super-tax on high incomes, land value duties, and increased death duties. The House of Lords, dominated by Unionists, considered the financial proposals so revolutionary that it took the unprecedented course of rejecting the Bill, thus precipitating the dispute between the two Houses of Parliament. Winston Churchill later observed that the Lords’ rejection of the Budget in November 1909 was “a constitutional outrage and political blunder almost beyond compare,” which “led directly to the two General Elections of 1910, to the Parliament Act, and to the Irish struggle.” (Churchill, The World Crisis)

The crisis was brought to a satisfactory end by the Parliament Act of 1911, which stripped the Lords of their veto over money bills and reduced their power over other legislation to a suspensory veto of three sessions. The Act was the culmination of the struggle that Lloyd George’s Budget had ignited.

Belloc and Cecil Chesterton’s The Party System (1911) opens on the same election dust. On one poster Lloyd George and Winston Churchill appear as “patriots confronting a haughty aristocracy (as represented to Mr Churchill by his cousin the Duke of Marlborough)”; on the other as “a pair of low-born demagogues hallooing on their ragged and illiterate associates to the plunder of the wealthy.” The Lords’ rejection of “Mr Lloyd George’s Budget” is the first move of the stage-play that, in their account, the two Front Benches then manage by Conference and a second poll. They do not assign him a separate psychology; he is one of the paired election figures of the Governing Group. In the chapter on secret funds they instance the 1910 Liberal Birthday Honours: Sir Weetman Pearson (Lord Cowdray), “head of a great contracting company, to which Mr Lloyd-George, junior, son of the Chancellor of the Exchequer, has recently been articled. He is a very rich man, and there is an end of it.” The sale-of-policies pages name Rhodes, Schnadhorst, and Rutherfoord Harris, not the Chancellor. Later they write that it is “quite incredible” that such men as Asquith and Lloyd George, Balfour and F. E. Smith, could under any other circumstances give expression to the “imbecilities” of platform speech — again a Front-Bench type, not a private diagnosis. Belloc’s Servile State treats the Insurance Act and wages boards as marks of status compulsion; it does not name Lloyd George or attach those measures to his office.

Social Reform: The National Insurance Act 1911

With the constitutional question settled, Lloyd George turned to the ambitious programme of social reform that defined his Chancellorship. The National Insurance Bill of 1911 was his signature achievement. The Annual Register records that the Bill was “three years in the building” and affected millions of working people. Lloyd George advocated the Bill at Birmingham on 10 June 1911 to an audience of three thousand — though applications for tickets had numbered seventy thousand. He pointed to the existence, side by side, of “extreme wealth and extreme poverty,” dealing eloquently with the difficulties and efforts of the poor, and presenting the Bill as “a means of keeping up national efficiency.” In great cities, he said, half the outdoor pauperism was due to sickness, and the Bill aimed to break that cycle.

The National Liberal Federation at its annual meeting in Bath eulogised “the National Insurance Bill and Mr. Lloyd George’s services to it.” Lloyd George himself described the Bill as “swimming through a murky flood of misrepresentation,” predicting that its opponents would ultimately discover its advantages. The Bill provided for compulsory health insurance for workers, covering medical treatment and sick pay, and laid the groundwork for unemployment insurance. The scheme applied to some 2,400,000 workers initially and was financed by contributions from workers, employers, and the State. (Annual Register 1911)

Clayton, writing in 1911, observed that Lloyd George’s Budget and his speeches in its support had already made his name familiar to the people of Great Britain, and that “in the eager discussion on his Bill for National Insurance, that name is still more loudly spoken. Hated by opponents and praised by admirers, denounced and extolled, Mr. Lloyd George enjoys the tumult he arouses. His passionate speeches for the poor provoke the sympathy of the working class; his denunciations of the rich stir the anger of all who fear social revolution.”

The Agadir Crisis: The Mansion House Speech (1911)

In July 1911, the German gunboat Panther appeared at the Moroccan port of Agadir, precipitating a major international crisis. The German Government had assumed that Lloyd George would head the “peace party” in the British Cabinet and that British action would be neutralised. They were profoundly mistaken.

Lloyd George, whose foreign policy alignment had hitherto been uncertain, made a decisive intervention. As Churchill described it: “The accession of Mr. Lloyd George in foreign policy to the opposite wing of the Government was decisive.” On 21 July 1911, speaking at the Mansion House, the Chancellor of the Exchequer declared:

“I believe it is essential in the highest interests not merely of this country, but of the world, that Britain should at all hazards maintain her place and her prestige amongst the Great Powers of the world… If a situation were to be forced upon us in which peace could only be preserved by the surrender of the great and beneficent position Britain has won by centuries of heroism and achievement… then I say emphatically that peace at that price would be a humiliation intolerable for a great country like ours to endure.”

The speech was, in Churchill’s words, “a thunder-clap to the German Government.” The German Ambassador, Count Metternich, protested in acrid terms, and the German Government was forced to realise that Britain would stand by France. The crisis subsided, and the negotiations that followed resulted in colonial compensations for Germany rather than war. The speech marked Lloyd George’s emergence as a force in foreign policy and confirmed the Anglo-French Entente at a critical moment. (Churchill, The World Crisis; Politics and Grand Strategy)

Minister of Munitions & War Organisation (1915–1916)

By the spring of 1915, the scale of the war had exposed a catastrophic shortage of artillery shells and other munitions. The “Shells Scandal” brought down the Liberal government and forced the creation of the First Coalition in May 1915. Lloyd George was appointed the first Minister of Munitions, tasked with transforming the nation’s industrial output for war. Hankey recorded that before Lloyd George’s appointment, the deplorable state of munitions production had been known and pressing; Ottley had urged the appointment of “an official with plenary powers to commandeer the entire manufacturing potentialities of the Empire” as early as September 1914, but “nothing was done until May 1915 when Lloyd George was appointed Minister of Munitions in the First Coalition government.” (Hankey, Man of Secrets)

The Munitions of War Bill, introduced in June 1915, gave the new minister sweeping powers. Lloyd George declared that the Central European Powers were turning out 250,000 shells a day and insisted that Britain could surpass that output if organised properly. He secured “some of the best business brains of the country,” adopted a policy of decentralisation into ten munition areas each run by local business committees, and took measures to mobilise labour: no strikes or lock-outs, compulsory arbitration, suspension of trade union restrictions on output, and limitation of employers’ profits. (Annual Register 1915)

The Bill provoked fierce debate. Critics charged that it gave the Minister of Munitions “power to socialise labour,” and one member warned of “a slave-driver with absolute power to imprison by his warrant any man who he thought took too many glasses of whisky or refused to work overtime.” Nonetheless, the Bill passed, and under Lloyd George’s direction munitions output soared. When he reported on the Ministry’s work later, he could point to a transformation in Britain’s capacity to wage industrial war.

Lloyd George was also deeply involved in strategic questions. In November 1916, before the War Committee, he pressed the Admiralty on the question of convoy protection against German submarines, suggesting that a dozen ships be convoyed by three warships, though the naval leadership remained sceptical at that stage. (Corbett, Naval Operations Vol. V)

Premiership (1916–1922)

The Fall of Asquith and Formation of the War Cabinet

By December 1916, dissatisfaction with Asquith’s conduct of the war had reached a crisis. Lloyd George, then Minister of Munitions, presented a plan for a small War Committee to direct the war that effectively sidelined the Prime Minister. Asquith demurred at serving under Bonar Law, and after a Buckingham Palace conference on 5–6 December, the King turned to Lloyd George. Within twenty-four hours, Lloyd George had formed a new government. As one source records, “Asquith declined to serve in a subordinate post,” and the new administration was “principally, though not exclusively, Unionist, and no Liberal member of the late government (except Lloyd George himself) joined it.” (Asquith entity)

Lloyd George’s most immediate innovation was the creation of a small War Cabinet of four or five members, freed from all departmental duties. As Corbett’s Naval Operations records:

“On assuming office, he at once altered the existing machinery by creating a small War Cabinet of four or five members, freed of all departmental duties; and also by creating new ministries for the rapid despatch of the vast mass of additional business which resulted from the complete mobilisation of the nation’s resources. The first War Cabinet was composed of the Premier, Lord Curzon, Lord Milner, Mr. Bonar Law, and Mr. Arthur Henderson… this new War Cabinet was no longer an inner committee taking decisions which must be referred… its rulings, taken after consultation with the naval and military authorities, were absolute and final.”

Hankey was appointed Secretary of the War Cabinet, a body that would hold 650 meetings before its dissolution in October 1919. (Hankey, Man of Secrets)

War Direction (1917–1918)

Lloyd George’s War Cabinet governed with unprecedented executive authority throughout 1917 and 1918. In July 1917, Lloyd George carried out his first Cabinet reconstruction: Sir Edward Carson retired from the Admiralty to join the War Cabinet without portfolio, and Churchill — whom Lloyd George had long wished to recall to office — became Minister of Munitions. The War Cabinet at this time consisted of Lloyd George, Lord Milner, Lord Curzon, Carson, Bonar Law, George Barnes, and General Smuts. (Annual Register 1917)

In a major speech at Glasgow in July 1917, Lloyd George defined Britain’s peace terms with unprecedented clarity. He declared that Belgium and Serbia must be restored and compensated, that Mesopotamia and Armenia must be freed from Turkish rule, that the wishes of inhabitants of German colonies must be the dominant factor in their future, and that the war’s guarantee lay in “the destruction of Prussian military power, or better still from the democratisation of the German Government.” The Russian Revolution, he argued, had postponed victory but made it “more sure and of a better quality.” (Annual Register 1917)

The year 1918 opened with Lloyd George issuing a New Year’s appeal for sacrifice: “The road is clear before you; follow it, and it will lead ere long to safety for our people and victory for our cause.” The winter was the coldest on record; food queues formed; German air raids killed scores of civilians. Lloyd George introduced the last Man-Power Bill of the war, extending military service to meet the crisis of the German spring offensive. Compulsory rationing was introduced for meat and other staples. (Annual Register 1918)

The Peace Settlement

Lloyd George was the dominant British figure at the Paris Peace Conference of 1919, representing Britain alongside President Wilson and Clemenceau. Hankey served as secretary to the Council of Four, the inner body that shaped the peace. (Hankey, Man of Secrets)

At home, Lloyd George led a Coalition Government that faced the enormous task of demobilisation and transition from war to peace. The Annual Register for 1919 records that the year was “dominated by the conclusion of peace and the labour disputes that followed the war,” with waves of strikes across British industry. Lloyd George addressed a Joint Industrial Committee as a “Peace Congress,” warning that since “Russia had gone to pieces, and Germany showed symptoms of going to pieces also, it might fall upon this country — not for the first time — to save civilisation.” He insisted that “employers must take the long view” and that “Britain could not save civilisation by the triumph of any one class.” (Annual Register 1919)

Character and Reputation

Contemporaries saw in Lloyd George a figure of extraordinary energy, ambition, and talent, but also of danger. Clayton’s portrait of 1911 captures the ambivalence:

“A supreme self-confidence carries him along, and the spur of ambition is constantly pricking. Political co-operation is difficult for such a man, and an indifference to reforms that are not of his initiation, and a willingness to wreck legislation that cannot bear his name, are a weakness in Mr. Lloyd George that may easily produce a fall.”

Neutral minds, Clayton wrote, doubted whether Lloyd George “is sufficiently well-balanced for the responsibilities of high office,” and expressed misgivings “lest the era of social reform be inaugurated too rapidly.” Yet he concluded: “In this year of grace 1911, Mr. Lloyd George, incarnation of the hard-working middle class, is a very distinct personality in the government of the country, and his presence in the Cabinet a fact in the history of democracy.”

Churchill, who knew Lloyd George intimately, captured in his account of the Agadir crisis the qualities that made him formidable: his unpredictability, his decisiveness, his willingness to break with expectation. Until a few hours before the Mansion House speech, Churchill wrote, “his colleagues did not know. Working with him in close association, I did not know. No one knew. Until his mind was definitely made up, he did not know himself.”

Legacy

David Lloyd George was the most consequential British statesman of the early twentieth century after Asquith. As Chancellor of the Exchequer, his People’s Budget of 1909 destroyed the absolute veto of the House of Lords and inaugurated the modern system of progressive taxation. As the architect of the National Insurance Act 1911, he laid the foundations of the British welfare state. As Minister of Munitions, he organised Britain for industrial war and solved the shell crisis that had threatened the war effort. As Prime Minister from 1916, he created the War Cabinet system that directed the war to victory and was the principal British architect of the post-war settlement.

Yet the same ambition and independence that drove his achievements also isolated him from the Liberal Party he had once led. The Coalition he formed in 1916 had few Liberals in it; the party split and never recovered its former position. The pages of political history, as Clayton wrote in 1911, “are strewn with the stories of high careers wrecked in a feverish haste for fame.” Lloyd George’s career did not end in wreck, but neither did it reach the heights his early promise had suggested. He remained Prime Minister until 1922, but never held office again.
