Edward VII (1841–1910)

King of the United Kingdom and the British Dominions, Emperor of India. Succeeded Queen Victoria on 22 January 1901 and reigned until his death on 6 May 1910. The source texts describe him as a popular sovereign whose foreign travels promoted peace and whose reign was marked by constitutional crisis over the House of Lords.

Biography

Accession (1901)

Queen Victoria died at Osborne on 22 January 1901, and the Prince of Wales succeeded to the throne as King Edward VII. At his first meeting of the Privy Council he declared his intention to follow in his mother’s footsteps and to maintain constitutional government. The Annual Register records that “the style assumed by the King” renounced all titles “that might be held to imply dominion or supremacy over any part of the British Empire.” Messages of condolence arrived from foreign powers, including a telegram from President McKinley of the United States.

The new King came to the throne while the South African War was still in progress. McCarthy’s Diamond Jubilee volume notes that “a new Sovereign, King Edward VII., had come to the British throne” before the Boer peace terms were finalised at Pretoria. After the Peace of Vereeniging (31 May 1902), “King Edward sent a cordial message of thanks to Lord Kitchener.”

Coronation and Recovery from Illness (1902)

The coronation of King Edward VII and Queen Alexandra was originally fixed for 26 June 1902 at Westminster Abbey. Two days before the ceremony the King was attacked with perityphlitis (appendicitis). An operation was performed and proved successful. The coronation was postponed to 9 August, and the King convalesced at Windsor. Parliament opened on 16 August. The Annual Register records that “All had equal cause for thankfulness” at the King’s recovery, and in October the King and Queen visited the City of London. The King also visited his German relatives.

Foreign Policy and the Entente with France (1903–1905)

King Edward’s foreign travels became a distinctive feature of his reign. In the spring of 1903 he visited the King of Italy in Rome, where his “personal popularity abroad” delighted the British public and “gave additional solidarity to the relations between the two States.”

From Rome the King went to Paris, arriving on 1 May 1903. The visit was “singularly fruitful in the improvement of international relations.” He was welcomed to a gaily decorated capital by enormous crowds and drove with President Loubet to the British Embassy. Replying to the British Chamber of Commerce, he dwelt on the importance of Anglo-French friendship; at a State dinner at the Elysée he toasted “the rapprochement between the two peoples.” The visit had “a marked influence in dissipating the last traces of such ill-will towards England as had been evoked by Lord Salisbury’s necessarily uncompromising resistance to French ambitions” over the Nile. An Arbitration Agreement with France followed later in the year.

In 1904 the Anglo-French Agreement was concluded. In 1905, when Germany raised a controversy over the Moroccan portion of that agreement and despatched an envoy to Fez, the King made another visit to Paris. The American Ambassador, Mr. Choate, paid tribute to King Edward’s “unceasing instinct for peace” and “perfect genius for conciliation.” The King’s visit, the Annual Register records, “had tended appreciably to diminish the tension of the situation.”

The Liberal Landslide and Later Travels (1906–1909)

In January 1906 the King formally dissolved Parliament, and the ensuing general election produced a Liberal landslide victory. In his speech at the opening of the new Parliament, the King addressed the new ministry. In 1907, King Edward left London for Biarritz in March, travelling incognito, and was joined by the Queen at Toulon. They cruised the Mediterranean on the royal yacht Victoria and Albert, escorted by the Mediterranean fleet. At Cartagena (8 April) the King was greeted by the King of Spain and the Queen Mother. At Gaeta (18 April) he met the King of Italy, and again near Rome on 30 April. Continental opinion, especially in Germany, connected these visits with “a presumed Anglo-Spanish alliance” and “a new grouping of the Western Powers” that would exclude Germany, though the Annual Register notes there was “no real evidence of such a combination.”

In 1908 the King visited the Tsar at Reval. The visit was controversial among Labour and advanced Liberal MPs, who disapproved of the Tsarist regime. The Prime Minister, Mr. Asquith, defended the visit in Parliament as “overdue” and likely to do good to Anglo-Russian relations. Sir Edward Grey, the Foreign Secretary, argued that the visit could not be postponed “without marked discourtesy” and would “signify the existence of friendly relations.” The visit went ahead despite strong protests.

In 1909 the King and Queen opened Parliament in February. The King’s Speech referred to the cordial reception of the King and Queen at Berlin and its expected effect on Anglo-German friendship. The speech also signalled the coming constitutional crisis, noting the increase in expenditure for old age pensions and the Navy.

Final Illness and Death (1910)

The King spent most of March and April 1910 at Biarritz — after his death it was stated that this was to avoid the danger of bronchitis from the March winds in England. While in Paris he caught cold while witnessing Rostand’s Chantecler, and had been more seriously ill for a week at Biarritz than the public suspected. He seemed to recover and returned to Buckingham Palace on 27 April. A day or two later he visited the Royal Academy Exhibition, looking “tired and pale.”

On Sunday, 1 May, he was at Sandringham; on Tuesday, 2 May, he was confined to his rooms but continued to transact business. He gave an audience on 5 May to Lord Islington and discussed the reception planned for Prince Fushimi of Japan. That afternoon a bulletin announced he was suffering from bronchitis and that his condition “causes some anxiety.” Queen Alexandra arrived from Corfu that evening. On the morning of 6 May the symptoms had worsened; his condition “gave rise to grave anxiety,” and a throat specialist was summoned.

Nevertheless, the King got up and transacted business, “suffering from painful fits of coughing and attacks of heart failure,” receiving oxygen to relieve his symptoms. At 1 p.m. he said: “I shall not give in; I shall go on; I shall work to the end.” Shortly afterwards he passed into a state of coma, though occasionally reviving. In the afternoon he left his armchair for his bed. At half-past nine the Prince and Princess of Wales, the Princess Royal, and Princess Louise were summoned to stay the night, and the Primate (the Archbishop of Canterbury) was sent for. Shortly after eleven the Primate conducted a short service in the sick-room, and at 11:45 p.m. “King Edward the Peacemaker passed away without pain.” The announcement “The King is dead” was made to the waiting and anxious crowd at the Palace gates, “received in silence, followed by manifold and heartfelt expressions of grief.”

Mourning and Tributes

The Annual Register declares: “The unexpected death of King Edward VII was felt throughout the Empire and even in the United States almost as a personal bereavement, and was received all over the world with profound manifestations of sympathy and regret. Even at the death of Queen Victoria the sorrow had not been so widespread or so fully manifested, at any rate outside the British Empire.” Messages of condolence poured in from the Dominions, India, and foreign states. Throughout the United Kingdom and the Empire, places of entertainment were closed, social events cancelled, and funeral peals tolled. On Sunday, 8 May, pulpit tributes were paid to the King’s memory, with special stress on “his tact, his promotion of the welfare of his people, and his efforts for the peace of the world.”

In Ireland, the Nationalist leader John Redmond stated that “the King never showed any hostility to Ireland, and the general feeling was that he was a frank, manly, and friendly Sovereign.” Mourning was general across Ireland, with memorial services marking the day of the funeral.

The King was succeeded by his son, George V.
