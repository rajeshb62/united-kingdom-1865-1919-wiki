Early Career & Rise to Leadership

Benjamin Disraeli entered the political world as a man apart — a literary figure turned politician, a Jew by race though a Christian in belief, and a romantic who wrote novels before he mastered the House of Commons. His first appearance before the electors of Wycombe was met with hooting and laughter; his maiden speech in the House of Commons was a disaster. But Disraeli possessed something that mere ridicule could not extinguish: a boundless faith in himself, and a constitution that seemed to find joy in the very heat of political strife. He had, as one contemporary observed, all the joy of the fight; the next best thing to a victory brilliantly won was a defeat gallantly sustained.

For many sessions he endured what seemed utter failure, and then suddenly found his chance and was recognised. From that time his career became one of steady advancement and growing fame. He was not, by the highest standard, a great orator — his more rhetorical efforts often bore an air of artificiality that marred their effect. But as a debater he ranked among the very foremost. He possessed an unfailing gift of sarcasm, a quickness in devising humorous illustrations at the expense of an antagonist, and a command of scornful phrases that lived in the memory of the House and became familiar quotations in debate and in literature. His voice was impressive and varied in tone.

Disraeli had, moreover, a literary career unique among statesmen. His political novels — Coningsby, Sybil, Tancred — were done half so well by anyone else in England. Indeed, it might be said that his are the only political novels in the English language. He contrived to blend his literary and political qualities into a curiously compact, characteristic, and original form of expression, and seemed as if he never could be languid, commonplace, or unimpressive; he was ever alive and ever vivid.

His rise to leadership of the Conservative Party came during the long Palmerstonian era, which Disraeli accurately described as the rule of a “Tory chief of a Radical Cabinet.” When Palmerston died in 1865, the question of parliamentary reform could no longer be postponed. The Liberal government under Earl Russell and Gladstone introduced a moderate Reform Bill in 1866, but it was defeated by a combination of old Palmerstonian Whigs and Conservatives. The Liberals retired, and a Conservative ministry under Lord Derby was formed, with Disraeli leading the House of Commons.

The Hyde Park riots of July 1866 — in which crowds tore down half a mile of iron railings and swarmed over the park — convinced Her Majesty’s ministers of the necessity of an immediate adoption of the reform principle. Disraeli, who had proposed reform as early as 1859 without support, now saw a great opportunity for constructive Conservative policy. “You cannot establish a party of mere resistance to change,” he told his followers, “for change is inevitable in a progressive country.” When Parliament met in February 1867, a Reform Bill was promised in the Queen’s Speech. Lord Derby called it frankly a “leap in the dark.” Several Conservative ministers, including Cranborne — the future Lord Salisbury — left the Government in disgust. But the Conservatives generally chuckled at “dishing the Whigs,” and the Bill, with considerable revision, passed through both Houses by August.

The Reform Act of 1867 enfranchised all male householders in boroughs and all male lodgers paying £10 a year for unfurnished rooms. The town workman was enfranchised by this Act as the middle-class man had been enfranchised by the Act of 1832, and the electorate was increased from about one hundred thousand to two million. It was a masterstroke of political tactics: the Conservative Party, under Disraeli’s inspiration, had carried a measure of reform more expansive than the Liberal bill it had defeated the year before. The measure was, as historians later noted, one of those instances where Disraeli’s most memorable successes in legislation were but the carrying out of a policy originated by his great rival Gladstone, opposed and overthrown by Conservative opposition in the previous session, and then carried to success by the Conservatives themselves.

When Lord Derby retired in February 1868, Disraeli became Prime Minister for the first time — but only briefly. The general election of 1868 returned Gladstone and the Liberals to power with a commanding majority, and Disraeli resigned at the end of the year without having had the opportunity to leave any deep mark upon domestic policy. He went into opposition, and for six patient years he held his party together, waiting for the tide to turn.

Premiership (1874-1880): Domestic Reform

The tide turned dramatically in 1874. Mr. Gladstone, in an impulsive and quixotic decision, suddenly dissolved Parliament in January of that year, appealing to the country for a renewal of confidence. The result surprised everyone. The Liberal majority was swept away; the Conservatives came back with a majority of about fifty. Mr. Gladstone resigned at once, and Mr. Disraeli — now sixty-nine years old — was installed in office at the head of what many called a “Conservative Reaction.”

Disraeli reduced the Cabinet to twelve. Lord Cairns became Lord Chancellor; Lord Derby, who gratified sober-minded men, was made Foreign Secretary; Lord Salisbury, despite the differences that had once separated them over the Reform Bill, was entrusted with the Indian Department. Mr. Cross, a Lancashire lawyer who had never held office before, was lifted into the Home Office. Sir Stafford Northcote, trained in finance by Gladstone himself, became Chancellor of the Exchequer.

For a time the new Government plodded steadily on with quiet schemes of domestic legislation, though these were not usually very comprehensive or drastic. Critics described them as tinkering. The Government tinkered at a measure for the security of improvements made by agricultural tenants, but made it purely permissive and therefore thoroughly worthless. This inclination to make every reform permissive tainted many of their domestic schemes: a bill was introduced professedly to remove some grievance, and then all parties concerned were allowed to contract themselves out of it.

Mr. Cross, however, proved a very efficient minister and introduced many useful schemes of legislation, among them an Artisans’ Dwellings Bill, the object of which was to enable local authorities to pull down houses unfit for human habitation and rebuild on the sites. The Government also restored the appellate jurisdiction of the House of Lords, which had seemed actually doomed.

One of the most dramatic episodes of the early ministry concerned the merchant seaman Samuel Plimsoll, who had for years agitated against the scandal of overloaded and unseaworthy ships. Plimsoll’s passionate denunciation of ship-owners in the House of Commons, and his readiness to defy parliamentary order itself, roused the country. Great meetings were held all over England. Disraeli, perceiving the direction of public feeling, quietly claimed some of the credit for having stirred the public temper, and the Government brought in — and torcibly pushed through — a Merchant Shipping Bill that met some of the worst abuses, though it did not prove to be a very strong measure.

But Disraeli’s attention was never long fixed on the dusty paths of domestic reform. He soon turned away from such homely business, and his Government’s energies were absorbed instead by questions of imperial interest in all parts of the world.

Imperial & Foreign Policy: The Berlin Congress, Suez, India

The first indication of the new foreign policy was the purchase of the shares which the Khedive of Egypt held in the Suez Canal. The Khedive was on the brink of bankruptcy, having lived in the true fashion of an Eastern prince, gratifying every expensive whim. His 176,000 shares came into the market; and on November 25th, 1875, the world was astonished by the news that the English Government had bought them for four millions sterling. The idea was not the Government’s own: it was Mr. Frederick Greenwood, the editor of a London evening paper, who first conceived the thought. He made it known to a member of the Cabinet, who threw cold water on it, but Greenwood then tried the Prime Minister himself — and the shares were instantly bought up.

Seldom had any act of government been received with such general approbation. The London newspapers broke into a chorus of applause; the clubs were delighted. When a faint voice here and there suggested that the purchase was foolish, useless, undignified, a shout of offended patriotism drowned the remonstrance. Some continental newspapers condemned the act as audacious, arrogant, and ominous — and this only stirred Englishmen the more. There was a general sense of satisfaction at the idea that England was again regarded as an arrogant and dominating power. Men held up their heads grandly. Disraeli himself spoke in a grand and mysterious way, giving people to understand that the buying of the shares was part of some great scheme destined to make England mistress of the East. The act was of historical importance as the first of a series of strokes by which the Government sought to keep the public continually excited by the luxury of a new sensation.

The Eastern Question soon absorbed everything. When the Bulgarian atrocities of 1876 stirred the conscience of Europe, Disraeli famously tried to dismiss the reports with a laugh and a light word. It was a serious miscalculation. Mr. Gladstone, emerging from his retirement, published his famous pamphlet on the Bulgarian horrors and threw himself into a great moral crusade against Turkish misrule. The controversy that followed became bitterly personal. Disraeli and Gladstone were thrown into as sharp an antagonism as two gladiators in a Roman arena. The policy each represented was clear: Disraeli stood for maintaining Turkey at all risks as a barrier against Russia; Gladstone for renouncing all responsibility for Turkey and taking the consequences. “The interests of England,” said one; “the public conscience of England,” said the other. Men who prided themselves on being practical politicians went naturally with Disraeli; men who held that sound politics cannot exist without sound morals went with Gladstone.

Each statesman made a mistake characteristic of the man. Disraeli misunderstood the gravity of public feeling on the Bulgarian question. Gladstone later acted on the assumption that mere sympathy could long prevail against the traditional English distrust of Russia — and when Disraeli and his supporters once had their opportunity of playing that card, they had the game absolutely in their hands.

At the Guildhall banquet on November 9th, 1876, Disraeli spoke in tones of open challenge to Russia. “If the struggle comes,” he declared, “in a righteous cause England will commence a fight that will not end until right is done.” The Russian Emperor, Alexander, replied the very next day, speaking to the nobles of Moscow in words that sounded like a direct answer. The decision of peace or war seemed to rest with Disraeli alone. Every eye was turned upon him.

The Constantinople Conference came to nothing, as the Turks, perhaps encouraged by private hints from English diplomatists, refused to come to terms. War broke out between Russia and Turkey. When the Treaty of San Stefano in 1878 threatened to put the whole of south-east Europe under Russian influence, the English Government refused to recognise it. Lord Derby resigned rather than approve the measures of resistance that Disraeli resolved upon. The Government called out the reserves, summoned Indian troops to Europe, occupied Cyprus, and prepared for war.

Lord Salisbury became Foreign Secretary. After tense negotiations, a Congress was summoned to meet in Berlin on June 13th, 1878. To the surprise of the public, Disraeli announced that he himself would attend — an event without precedent. Never before had an English Prime Minister left the country while Parliament was sitting to act as the representative of England in a foreign capital. The part suited Disraeli’s love for the picturesque and the theatrical. His journey to Berlin was a triumphal progress: at every great city, almost at every railway station, crowds turned out to see the English statesman whose strange and varied career had so long excited the wondering attention of Europe.

Prince Bismarck presided over the Congress, and departed from custom by opening the proceedings in English — a kindly deference to the English Prime Minister, whose French was said to have fallen into decay. The Congress dealt with the condition of the provinces under Turkish suzerainty, the populations of alien race under Turkish dominion, the claims of Greece, and the Turkish possessions in Asia.

The Treaty of Berlin recognised the complete independence of Roumania, Servia, and Montenegro. It created, north of the Balkans, a self-governing state of Bulgaria, tributary to the Sultan but practically independent; and south of the Balkans, Eastern Roumelia, remaining under Turkish authority but with a Christian governor. The Sultan undertook to apply reform to Crete, and Austria was to occupy Bosnia and the Herzegovina.

A secret engagement with Turkey gave England the island of Cyprus, which Disraeli later described as “a place of arms.” England now formally pledged herself to defend Turkey’s Asiatic possessions against invasion, and occupied Cyprus as a vantage-ground. The difference between the Conservative and Liberal policies was now thrown into the strongest relief. Disraeli had made it the cardinal principle of his policy that England above all was concerned to maintain the integrity of the Turkish Empire.

Disraeli’s return from Berlin was the climax of his career. He got a great public reception in London. Crowds awaited him at the gaudily decorated railway station. He made a conquering hero’s progress through the streets, and from the windows of the Foreign Office addressed an excited and tumultuous crowd. His words became memorable: he had brought back “Peace with Honour.”

From the day when he first addressed the electors of Wycombe, hooted and laughed at, up to this triumphal reception in the streets of London — what a distance he had traversed! Years of struggle against almost insurmountable difficulties, years of steady faith in himself undisturbed by almost universal ridicule, years of rise and fall, of success and disaster, had conducted him to this climax. At that moment he was probably the most conspicuous public man in the world, with the single exception of Prince Bismarck. He had attained a position of almost unrivalled popularity in England; not even Palmerston in his most successful days was pursued by such clamour of public acclamation. He ought to have followed classic advice, one observer remarked, and sacrificed his dearest possession to the gods at that moment, for no man could buy the lease of such a position without sacrifice.

The Royal Titles Act of 1876, by which Queen Victoria assumed the title of Empress of India, was another measure closely associated with Disraeli’s name. It was a piece of statecraft entirely in keeping with his love of ceremony, his concern for imperial prestige, and his desire to bind the Crown more closely to the great dependencies of the Empire.

Legacy & Reputation

Lord Beaconsfield — for he had accepted an earldom and removed to the House of Lords in 1876 — died on the morning of Tuesday, April 19, 1881, at his house in Curzon Street. He had suffered from a severe attack of bronchial asthma, and the east wind, always trying to him, continued to blow. Crowds of inquirers came to his door every day; amongst them many who had been thoroughly opposed to his public policy, and who believed him an ambitious and self-seeking man. But the thought that so great a career was about to close anticipated that burial of all hostility which death brings about.

Those who watched over him said that a few minutes before his death he raised himself a little in his bed, stretched himself out in the attitude he was accustomed to assume when he rose in the House of Commons, and his lips moved without sending out any words. His dying fancy was giving him back his old place in Parliamentary debate. No end could be more characteristic of the man.

He was undoubtedly one of the most interesting and fascinating figures in the European history of his time. He had made a great and quite a unique success in literature. He had been for many years the most splendid gladiator in the House of Commons. He had boundless ambition and followed his own star. Men of thorough sincerity — men like Gladstone, Cobden, and Bright — felt shocked at what seemed to them his lack of earnestness. When John Bright once spoke privately of “sated ambition,” Disraeli replied: “You know what you and I come here for. We come here for fame.” He could not be convinced that any man would waste his days in the House of Commons merely for the sake of serving a public movement.

Yet, with all the temptations that fame and society offered him, he seems to have led personally a blameless life. His bitterest enemy never suggested that any question of pecuniary gain ever occupied his mind. He was a good friend to his friends, and especially kind to young men of promise who seemed to have an ambition like that of his own early days. In society he was capricious of mood — sometimes sparkling with epigram and paradox, sometimes falling into a fit of brooding silence. He had, one might almost say, no friends among the outer public. For all the splendour of his opportunities and successes, he was a lonely, self-sufficing man. He had had all he wanted; everything came to him in the end.

Historians have judged that as a statesman Disraeli did not leave much record of great measures for the advancement of national prosperity. Even where his Government did introduce genuine reform or some new principle of legislation, it was often only after the rival party had come near to carrying a like measure and, failing because of Conservative opposition, had gone out of office and given Disraeli and his colleagues a tempting chance of carrying the work to success. This was notably the case with the Reform Bill of 1867.

As a debater, he was among the very foremost known to modern Parliaments, with an unfailing gift of sarcasm, a quickness in devising humorous illustrations, and a command of scornful phrases that lived in the memory. His voice was impressive and varied in tone. He seemed as if he could never be languid, commonplace, or unimpressive. He was ever alive and ever vivid.

The long rivalry between Gladstone and Disraeli did not leave any painful memories behind it for the admirers of either man. Those who followed Gladstone might remember with satisfaction that some of Disraeli’s most memorable successes in legislation were but the carrying out of a policy originated by Gladstone himself. But the name of Disraeli will always be regarded as that of one who brought into the House of Commons a new and peculiar style of eloquence; who contrived to blend his literary and his political qualities into a curiously compact, characteristic, and original form of expression. Destiny had given him everything for which he could possibly have asked.
