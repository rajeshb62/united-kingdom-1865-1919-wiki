CECIL JOHN RHODES (1853–1902)

Cecil John Rhodes was an English-born businessman, mining magnate, and politician who became the dominant figure in southern African affairs in the late nineteenth century. He made his fortune in diamond mining, founded De Beers Consolidated Mines, served as Prime Minister of Cape Colony, expanded British territory northward through the British South Africa Company (the “Chartered Company”), and left a lasting educational legacy through the Rhodes Scholarships. His role in the Jameson Raid (1895–1896) brought his political career to a halt and intensified the antagonisms that led to the South African War.

Diamond Mining & De Beers

Rhodes went out to South Africa as a young man for the benefit of his health and found his way into finance and mining. The discovery of diamonds in the Kimberley region in the 1870s drew a flood of adventurers and capitalists, and Rhodes — described by contemporaries as “above all things an adventurer by temperament and by career” — was among those who saw the opportunity for consolidation. Through a series of acquisitions and amalgamations, he brought the disparate diamond claims under unified control, founding the De Beers Consolidated Mines, which came to dominate the world diamond trade. McCarthy’s account records that “Mr. Cecil Rhodes might undoubtedly, in a certain sense, be called a man of genius” in his capacity to combine business enterprise with imperial vision.

By the 1890s Rhodes was managing director of De Beers Consolidated Mines and also held a controlling interest in the Gold Fields of South Africa. The combination of these financial powers placed him at the centre of southern Africa’s mineral economy and gave him the resources to pursue his political and territorial ambitions. The South Africa Committee’s report of 1897 later noted that as “managing director of the British South African Company, as director of the De Beers Consolidated Mines and the Gold Fields of South Africa, Mr. Rhodes controlled a great combination of interests.”

Prime Minister of Cape Colony

Rhodes entered politics in Cape Colony, rising rapidly to become its Prime Minister — a position he held from 1890 to 1896. He was described as “in every way its foremost man.” His political career was inseparable from his financial and imperial ambitions. As Prime Minister, he worked to extend British influence across southern Africa, seeking to contain Boer expansion and link the Cape to the interior territories.

McCarthy’s history presents Rhodes as one of “two great and conspicuous figures in South Africa” of his era, alongside Paul Kruger, President of the Transvaal Republic. Where Kruger was a Boer of the Boers — “rough and rugged in appearance, blunt in manner, but with a certain characteristic humor” — Rhodes was the English imperialist, university-educated, who embodied the energetic, acquisitive spirit of the British adventurer. “Power, position, greatness, money,” McCarthy observes, “these were of absolutely no account to President Kruger, and they were the natural pursuit of Mr. Cecil Rhodes.”

His premiership was marked by an aggressive policy of territorial expansion northward, carried out through the British South Africa Company (the Chartered Company), which received Imperial sanction and was “in itself like the founding of a State.”

The Jameson Raid & Aftermath

The defining crisis of Rhodes’s career came with the Jameson Raid of 1895–1896. Relations between the Transvaal Republic under President Kruger and the Uitlander (Outlander) population — the mainly English-speaking settlers drawn to the Transvaal’s gold and diamond fields — had deteriorated severely. The Uitlanders complained that the Republic’s laws denied them citizenship rights despite their large numbers and their contribution to the country’s prosperity. The Boers, in turn, regarded the newcomers as a threat to their independence. McCarthy records that the Boers became convinced “that the object of the Outlanders was to take possession of the Transvaal, and annex it in some form to the British Empire, with Mr. Cecil Rhodes as a sort of dictator.”

Rhodes, through the Chartered Company, was deeply involved in preparations for an uprising in Johannesburg to be supported by an armed incursion from the border. Dr. Leander Starr Jameson, a trusted lieutenant who held high position in the Company, put himself at the head of a force of Company policemen. On December 29, 1895, Jameson crossed the Transvaal border from Pitsani, intending to march on Johannesburg and trigger the planned insurrection. The raid failed disastrously: Jameson’s force was surrounded and forced to surrender to the Boers, and the Johannesburg uprising never materialised. President Kruger acted with clemency, sparing the lives of the invaders and sending the leaders to England for trial.

The Imperial Government, through Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain, had telegraphed the High Commissioner, Sir Hercules Robinson, authorising him “to take every step in his power to prevent the invasion.” Jameson refused to be stopped. A House of Commons Committee was appointed to investigate the affair. The inquiry was hampered by the refusal of Rhodes’s solicitor, Mr. Hawksley, to produce certain telegrams on grounds of legal professional privilege, and by the disappearance of Dr. Harris, the Company’s secretary in South Africa. One member, Mr. Edward Blake, was so unfavourably impressed by “the destruction or suppression of several telegrams” that he refused to take further part.

The Committee’s report, adopted in 1897, condemned Rhodes while carefully qualifying the condemnation. It declared that “as regards the Right Honorable C. J. Rhodes, he was thoroughly acquainted with the preparations that led to the inroad,” and that in his capacity as controller of the three great joint-stock companies — the British South Africa Company, De Beers Consolidated Mines, and the Gold Fields of South Africa — “he directed and controlled the combination which rendered such a proceeding as the Jameson Raid possible.” The report found “no justification for the conduct of a person in Mr. Rhodes’s position in subsidising, organising, and stimulating an armed insurrection against the Government of the South African Republic, and employing the forces and resources of the Chartered Company to support such a revolution.”

The Committee further concluded that Rhodes “deceived the High Commissioner representing the Imperial Government, he concealed his views from his colleagues in the Colonial Ministry and from the board of the British South African Company, and led his subordinates to believe that his plans were approved by his superiors.” The Raid, the report declared, had “for the time being grave injury to British influence in South Africa. Public confidence was shaken, race feeling embittered, and serious difficulties were created with neighbouring States.”

The Jameson Raid forced Rhodes to resign as Prime Minister of Cape Colony in January 1896. Though he survived the immediate political storm — owing in part to the reluctance of the Government to pursue the matter further — his reputation was permanently damaged. McCarthy notes that “there unquestionably remained among a large and intelligent proportion of the community a feeling that an unwise, unjust, and unintelligible objection to a full inquiry into this part of the subject had been shown by the majority of the Committee.”

The German Emperor Wilhelm II sent a telegram of congratulations to President Kruger on the defeat of the raiders, an act that caused widespread indignation in England and further heightened tensions between the European powers over South Africa.

Rhodesia

Even before the Jameson Raid, Rhodes had pursued the expansion of British territory north of the Limpopo River with extraordinary energy. Through the British South Africa Company, granted a Royal Charter in 1889, he acquired vast tracts of land, established settlements, and imposed British administration over the territories that would become Southern and Northern Rhodesia. McCarthy writes that “a vast territory was called Rhodesia after his name, and he became — we cannot describe it otherwise — the chief and the dictator and the idol of all the mixed and various adventurous population who streamed into South Africa, led by the longing to make money.”

The Chartered Company’s operations were conducted with a military arm, originally intended for defensive purposes against hostile native populations. The Company fought several fierce wars of conquest against the Ndebele (Matabele) and the Shona peoples to establish control over the region. McCarthy observes that “for a long time, all but unknown to the vast majority of Englishmen at home, the Chartered Company was writing out a new volume of history with a big pen, sometimes, it must be allowed, dipped in very red ink.”

The naming of Rhodesia after its founder was perhaps the most striking personal tribute ever paid to a British imperial figure during his own lifetime. The territory remained under the administration of the British South Africa Company until the 1920s, long after Rhodes’s death.

Rhodes himself was buried in the Matoppo Hills in Rhodesia, as he directed in his will — a burial-ground set apart for those who had rendered eminent services to South Africa.

Legacy (Rhodes Scholarships)

Cecil Rhodes died at Cape Town on March 26, 1902, while the South African War was still drawing to a close. The Annual Register records that “there was a universal feeling of tragedy” at his death. “Different estimates were held of his work as an Imperialist, and of the extent to which the good he had achieved had been neutralised by his connection with the Jameson Raid. But there was a very wide-spread admiration of his powers and his general aims, and deep regret was felt at his being denied the opportunity of exercising his influence to aid in the re-establishment of peace.”

The publication of Rhodes’s will in April 1902 produced “a deeply and universally favourable impression.” Even those who had looked “with the most severely critical eyes on the career of that remarkable man” were constrained to applaud “testamentary dispositions which were original alike in their substance and in the scale of the provision made for carrying them out.”

The most impressive provisions were those relating to university education. Rhodes directed his trustees to establish scholarships of £300 a year for three years, to be held at any college in the University of Oxford. Sixty colonial scholarships were assigned: nine to Rhodesia; twelve to Cape Colony; three to Natal; and three each to the Australian colonies, New Zealand, Canadian provinces, and other British possessions. He also established American scholarships, two for each State or Territory of the United States.

This magnificent bequest was “expressly inspired by the testator’s desire to encourage and foster an appreciation of the advantages which he implicitly believed would result from the union of the English-speaking peoples throughout the world.” In a codicil, Rhodes further established fifteen scholarships of £250 a year each for German students, holding “that good understanding between England, the United States of America, and Germany would secure the peace of the world.”

The criteria for selection reflected Rhodes’s ideals of character. Regard was to be had to the candidate’s “literary and scholastic attainments”; his “fondness of, and success in, manly outdoor sports, such as cricket, football and the like”; his “qualities of manhood, truth, courage, devotion to duty, sympathy for and protection of the weak, kindliness, unselfishness and fellowship”; and his “exhibition during schooldays of moral force of character and of instincts to lead.” No student was to be qualified or disqualified on account of race or religious opinions.

To his own college, Oriel, Rhodes bequeathed £100,000 free of duty. The residue of his real and personal estate was left to the Earl of Rosebery, Earl Grey, Lord Milner, Mr. A. Beit, Dr. L. S. Jameson, Mr. L. Ll. Michell, and Mr. B. F. Hawksley as joint tenants.

The Rhodes Scholarships became the most famous educational trust of the twentieth century, bringing thousands of students from across the English-speaking world and beyond to study at Oxford. The scheme remains the most enduring and widely admired element of Rhodes’s legacy — testamentary dispositions that, as the Annual Register noted at the time, “were original alike in their substance and in the scale of the provision made for carrying them out.”

Party Funds and Imperial Economy

Belloc and Chesterton, in the chapter of The Party System on secret funds, treat “the sale of policies” as worse than the sale of honours. “It is notorious that the late Mr Cecil Rhodes did this on a large scale.” Letters published between him and Schnadhorst, “then head of the Liberal Caucus,” offered “sums running into tens of thousands” on condition that Egypt remain under British government and that a Liberal Ministry look favourably on the Cape-to-Cairo railway. They do not find a written promise, but “Schnadhorst appears to have satisfied him.” The money was paid; Gladstone’s government did not evacuate Egypt, though Gladstone had publicly favoured evacuation. “Gladstone, though nominally leader, was at the mercy of Schnadhorst, and Schnadhorst was at the mercy of anyone who would give him money.” The authors do not argue the merits of remaining in Egypt; they argue that a policy ought not to be “dictated by a single rich subscriber to the Party Funds.”

J. A. Hobson’s Imperialism: A Study (1902) places Rhodes among the economic parasites of the new Imperialism. Financiers and their allied trades “are essentially parasites upon patriotism”; their “true attitude of mind is expressed by Mr. Rhodes in his famous description of ‘Her Majesty’s Flag’ as ‘the greatest commercial asset in the world.’” “To combine the commercial with the imaginative” was the aim Rhodes ascribed to himself; Hobson finds the conjunction “more sinister” when politics and private business were “inextricably interwoven” as in a career that “used the legislature of Cape Colony to support and strengthen the diamond monopoly of De Beers, while from De Beers he financed the Raid, debauched the constituencies of Cape Colony, and bought the public press, in order to engineer the war, which was to win him full possession of his great ‘thought’ the North.” Chartered-company government is “private despotism” established for dividends: a managing director may be “unscrupulous and short-sighted, as Mr. Rhodes in the South African Chartered Company.” Abercorn’s evidence to the South Africa Committee is quoted: Rhodes had “a power of attorney to do precisely what he liked without consultation with the Board.” The practices “writ large in Rhodes, Beit, and their parliamentary confederates” return home as the “South African millionaire.” Hobson’s table of the measure of Imperialism lists the British South Africa Charter (1889) and the Transvaal and Orange River Colony (1900) among the additions of 1884–1900.
