Constitutional Monarchy

Constitutional monarchy in late Victorian and Edwardian Britain meant a Crown that reigned as the symbol of national unity and continuity while ministers exercised the surviving royal prerogatives and the sovereign stood aloof from party. Influence replaced command. Popularity rested on neutrality, private virtue, and public ceremonial rather than on the old politics of “the King’s friends.” The Crown remained the pledge of national continuity, tempering the evils of absolute party government; such prerogatives as were not dead were carried out by ministers, while royal influence still ran through every branch of the State.

Development

Victoria and the doctrine of aloofness

The transformation was under way when Victoria came to the throne. William IV in 1830 had feared a hostile London demonstration at the Mansion House; his death in 1837 evoked no national mourning. Victoria, unknown to the people at her accession, had Lord Melbourne’s political advice in the early years of the reign. Her marriage in 1840 brought the Prince Consort, who studied the politics of the day with great industry, spoke openly to ministers on all subjects, and saw quickly that if monarchy was to rise in popularity it could only be by the sovereign leading a good life and keeping quite aloof from party. The days of a profligate court and of royal partisanship were past; the royal influence was to succeed the royal prerogative.

The Prince Consort did for the Crown what Wellington did for the House of Lords. Just as the Duke saw that the Lords must give up setting themselves against the national will strongly expressed, so the Prince saw that the Crown could no longer exercise those legal rights for which George III had fought. Like the Lords, the Crown became a checking and regulating, rather than a moving, force. It remained the pledge and symbol of the unity and continuity of the national life, tempering the evils of absolute party government. Such of the royal prerogatives as were not dead must be carried out by ministers. That doctrine—aloofness from partisanship, private virtue in place of court intrigue, ceremonial presence without party command—became the working theory of the Victorian monarchy and the inheritance of her successors.

Party government under the Crown

The Crown’s neutrality mattered because government itself had become party government. The orator who could sway multitudes was checked by the superior power of party organisation; yet the party organiser and political “boss” could as easily become a curse to representative institutions. Patronage became a method for keeping a party in power; the promise of rewards and spoils enabled an opposition to defeat the Government and obtain office. In England it had become practically impossible for a man to take any serious part in politics except by becoming part of the machine: an independent attitude meant isolation. A troublesome critic might be bought with office, a legal appointment, or a peerage. When public interest sank to “backing your candidate,” or lower still to mercenary calculation, the danger was the indifference of the average honest citizen—the warning John Stuart Mill had given against leaving politics to the politicians.

The Crown did not break that machine; it tempered its absolutism. Great as royal influence might be in the social life of the wealthier classes, it did not operate in the casting of votes at parliamentary elections. No one seriously suspected the King of desiring Liberal or Tory victories; that neutrality was the surest guarantee of continued popularity. The permanent Conservative majority in the Lords acted on the assumption that a Liberal ministry did not represent the will of the people—at variance with democratic theory and in contradiction to the constitutional practice of the Crown. The sovereign’s role was not to choose a party programme but to stand outside the spoils system.

Republicanism, Jubilee, and popular pageantry

Republicanism had a brief working-class Radical vogue in the late 1870s and early 1880s. Charles Bradlaugh was its chief exponent; Joseph Chamberlain and Sir Charles Dilke were regarded as Republicans before they entered Gladstone’s ministry in 1880. The movement waned before Bradlaugh’s death; he came to feel that agitation for an ideal form of government was less fruitful than agitation against class privilege, and in his last dozen years his work went mainly to reforms within the Constitution. With the rise of Socialism in England in 1884–5 and the Queen’s Jubilee in 1887, republicanism became utterly moribund. Nothing save an attempt by the sovereign to take a definite side in party politics, or a notorious lapse from the morals required of persons in office of State, seemed likely to revive it.

The Jubilee of 1887 marked the beginning of the popular revival in pageantry and official ceremonial—a parallel to the earlier ritual revival in the Church of England and the processions of revived Roman Catholicism. By gratifying the common human instinct for spectacle the monarchy gained the popular affections. The Whigs had scoffed at pageants; the earlier Puritans had proscribed ceremonial as idolatry. In the latter half of the nineteenth century England began to throw off the shackles of Puritanism and to lose all interest in Whiggery. The new democracy was neither coldly Deist nor austerely Republican. It showed no inclination to inaugurate a reign of “pure reason,” but boldly adopted symbolism and pageantry. Modern democracy did not desire that kings or priests should rule; it did require that on State occasions they should be clad in kingly and priestly robes.

Edward VII and the opening of the crisis

Victoria’s successors maintained that aloofness. Under Edward VII the Crown continued as a social and ceremonial presence of great weight among the wealthier classes, without operating as a party force at the polls. The fiscal and constitutional storm of 1909–10 tested the residual prerogative without overturning the Victorian settlement. Parliament opened on 16 February 1909 under Edward with a Speech already warning that old-age provision and the Navy would raise expenditure. Asquith insisted that finance must be the first business of the session. The Finance Bill introduced by David Lloyd George on 29 April—the People’s Budget—was rejected by the Lords on 30 November 1909 by 350 to 75, the first rejection of a finance measure since 1860. Lord Loreburn held that rejection was “legal, but not constitutional”: supply was the exclusive function of the Commons, and rejection invaded their privilege and the powers of the Crown.

The January–February 1910 general election left a hung Parliament—Liberals 275, Unionists 273, Labour 40, Irish Nationalists 82—with a united majority of about 124 for the ministry dependent on Irish and Labour votes. The Finance Bill then passed and was accepted by the Lords in April. The constitutional question—the Lords’ veto—now became the central issue. The death of Edward VII in May cast a temporary hush over party warfare. The lying-in-state and funeral (17–20 May) occupied the nation; Parliament paid its tributes; even industrial disputes were postponed. The Crown’s popularity as national symbol was displayed in mourning as in festival.

George V, Conference, and the peerage-creation guarantee

George V succeeded in the midst of deadlock. On 17 June 1910 the Constitutional Conference opened—an attempt by leading men from both sides to compose the differences between the Houses. On 23 June the Duke of Cornwall was created Prince of Wales and Earl of Chester. On 21 August a Royal Proclamation announced that the Coronation should take place in June 1911; on 26 August the King reviewed the Fleet in Torbay. The Accession Declaration Bill modified the form of Declaration required of a new Sovereign so as to remove offence to Roman Catholics while safeguarding the Protestant Succession. The Conference failed without public disclosure. The December 1910 election left the balance substantially as before (united majority about 126). The Liberal–Irish–Labour coalition now had a renewed mandate to reform the House of Lords through the struggle known as the People’s Budget and Parliament Act crisis.

Parliament was opened in state for the first time in the new reign by George V on 6 February 1911. Before delivering his Speech the King read and signed the Accession Declaration as established by the Act of 1910. Proposals would be submitted without delay “for settling the relations between the two Houses of Parliament, with the object of securing the more effective working of the Constitution.” The Parliament Bill embodied the Commons resolution of June 1907 (carried 432 to 147): that within a single Parliament the final decision of the Commons should prevail. Money Bills not passed unamended by the Lords within a month were to receive the Royal Assent notwithstanding; other bills passed by the Commons in three successive sessions were to become law in the third without the Lords’ assent; the maximum duration of a Parliament was cut from seven years to five. Leave to introduce was given on 22 February by 351 to 227. On 22 June 1911 the Coronation exhibited the ancient British Monarchy as the most popular and democratic: for three or four days party strife was suspended. The Imperial Conference received the King and the Dominion Premiers; the Royal visit to Scotland in July called forth a demonstration of loyalty.

When the Lords returned the measure with amendments the Commons refused, Asquith announced—as Grey and others had obtained from William IV in 1832—that he had received guarantees from the Crown that peers would be created in numbers sufficient to pass the Bill if it were again rejected. Behind the scenes the King’s pledge on peer creation was the residual prerogative used to resolve a deadlock between the Houses. Lord Midleton and the Earl of Rosebery pressed for a clear statement as to the “guarantees.” To avoid the making of some three or four hundred Liberal peers—in 1831 forty creations might have sufficed; by 1911 about four hundred were said to be required—Lord Lansdowne, following Wellington’s example, advised Conservative peers to refrain from opposition. Unionist peers divided between those who counselled surrender and the “die-hards” or “Halsburyites,” led by the Earl of Halsbury (100–150). On 10 August 1911 the Bill passed the Lords by 131 to 114; some 300 Unionist peers stayed away. The Royal Assent followed on 18 August. The threat, not the mass creation, had sufficed. The sovereign had acted on ministerial advice to make parliamentary government possible; he had not chosen a party programme. The permanent Conservative majority above the bar had long contradicted the Crown’s own practice of neutrality; the Parliament Act brought the second chamber into line with the constitutional monarchy that Victoria’s generation had defined—checking and regulating, not moving, when the national will was strongly expressed through the elected House.

War kingship

The same Crown that had underwritten the Parliament Act stood as the focus of national unity when European war broke in 1914. The spring of that year had already shown how thin was the line between constitutional agitation and force in Ireland: the Curragh incident, the Larne gun-running, and the Buckingham Palace Conference had divided Cabinet and Army while Europe moved toward war. Within twelve days the Cabinet that on 24 July was still debating Fermanagh and Tyrone found itself at war with the German Empire. On 2 August there was a patriotic demonstration before Buckingham Palace; a partial Moratorium was decreed by Royal Proclamation. The King held a Council at 4.30 p.m.; the Government took control of all wireless telegraphy; and the Admiralty called out the Naval Reserves. On the announcement of that last step a crowd of some 6,000 marched up the Mall to Buckingham Palace and sang the national anthems of Great Britain and France. On 3 August Cowes Regatta was abandoned by special desire of the King; Royal Proclamations authorised the Admiralty to requisition British ships and forbade the export of warlike stores. On 4 August an Order in Council placed the railways under Government control; British Army mobilisation was ordered by Proclamation; measures empowered the Crown to impose restrictions on aliens. At eleven o’clock at night—midnight by German time—the ultimatum to Germany expired. Along the Mall an immense concourse was singing “God save the King.” As the first stroke of Big Ben boomed out, the war telegram—“Commence hostilities against Germany”—was flashed to the ships and establishments under the White Ensign all over the world. The outbreak of the Great War found the constitutional monarchy not as a party arbiter but as the ceremonial and legal centre through which proclamations, Orders in Council, and the symbols of national resolve ran. Lord Kitchener’s acceptance of the War Office—not as a politician, his acceptance identifying him with no set of political opinions—illustrated the same settlement: the Crown and non-party national service standing above party rivalry while ministers exercised the prerogatives of mobilisation.

From Melbourne’s advice and the Prince Consort’s doctrine of aloofness, through the Jubilee revival of pageantry and the extinction of republicanism, through Edward VII’s death in the Budget crisis and George V’s peer-creation guarantee that broke the Lords’ absolute veto, to the Mall crowds of August 1914, the Crown’s path was consistent. Influence succeeded prerogative; neutrality secured popularity; residual prerogative was used, on ministerial advice, only to make parliamentary government work when the Houses deadlocked or the nation went to war. That was the constitutional monarchy of 1865–1919.
