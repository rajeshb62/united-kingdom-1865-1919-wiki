Financial Administration

Financial administration was the public counterpart of the private money-market: the machine by which the Crown, the Treasury, the House of Commons, the Banks of England and Ireland, the Paymaster-General, and the Comptroller and Auditor General turned revenue into authorised spend and spent money into audited accounts. Estimates, appropriation, custody, issue, accounting, and audit were treated as one chain. The document that bound needs, prior grants, and resources into a single plan for a future year was the Budget — a definite proposal for financing the business of the state on both sides of the account.

Development

Until 1854 England ran three financial years at once (estimates, appropriations, accounts). By 17 & 18 Vict. c. 94 s. 2 the year became 1 April–31 March for all purposes. Books closed on a cash basis at year-end: prompt treasury figures, less scientific cost information than the French accrual practice of keeping years open until all accruals were collected and paid. No expenditure was lawful except under parliamentary authority — annual appropriation or permanent statute. Commons had first controlled the total the Crown might take in tax, not the detail of spend; discretion in expenditure remained a Crown prerogative long after 1688. The modern principle that Parliament may determine purposes and manner of spending was complete by the late nineteenth century, yet the most characteristic English feature was how much discretion was still left to the Executive. Minute specification was refused because the Crown was thought better placed to decide details.

Two reforms made a general appropriation system possible. The Civil List separated the sovereign’s personal revenues from those of the state. William III’s grant with a £700,000 ceiling first put a Civil List on the statute book; George III surrendered most hereditary revenues for £800,000 a year; since William IV the sovereign received a salary covering personal and court expenses. The 1913–14 Finance Accounts showed a Civil List of £470,000 (privy purse £110,000; household salaries £125,800; household expenses £193,000; works £20,000; royal bounty £13,200; unappropriated £8,000), a permanent charge on the Consolidated Fund like debt interest and judges’ salaries. The Commissioners of Public Accounts in 1785 condemned earmarking — seventy-four charges on Customs, militia on the land tax, hereditary annuities on the Post Office — and recommended one fund into which every stream of revenue should flow and from which every service should be supplied. The Consolidated Fund Act of 1787 (27 Geo. III c. 13) created that fund; Irish revenues were similarly treated in 1816 and the two funds united.

The Fund stood to the credit of the Exchequer at the Bank of England and the Bank of Ireland (Scotch banks received and remitted in rotation). In 1834 revenues were ordered paid into the two banks to the Exchequer account. The banks were mere bankers of the Government: they received and paid to officers charged with settling obligations, taking the place of the old Teller and the three-lock chest.

No tax or expenditure was considered unless recommended from the Crown. Standing Orders 66 and 68 (early eighteenth century) barred petitions or motions for grants unless so recommended, and barred compounding Crown debts without official certificate. Members might not move to increase the Crown’s demand. The rare exception was an Address asking a special grant (monument, reward for service), still voted in committee. Permanent charges were Consolidated Fund Services; annually voted charges were Supply Services. Estimates, accompanying tables, and appropriation accounts covered only Supply. The 1913–14 Finance Accounts recapped Consolidated Fund issues of about £37.3 million against total Exchequer issues of £239.8 million — roughly one-sixth. Critics such as Harold Cox pointed out that public-office annuities, Queen’s Colleges, Irish land purchase, and Indian Army Pension Deficiency were split between the Fund and Supply votes, so estimates understated the total cost of particular services. Supply, when voted, was still paid from the Consolidated Fund.

Every appropriation rested on a formal estimate — both a calculation of need and a formulated demand of the Executive on Parliament. Estimates were the act of the Ministry as a whole. Except Army and Navy (presented by those departments only after Treasury approval), they were submitted by the Treasury. In disputes the Treasury decided, subject to rare Cabinet appeal on large policy (social reform, army or navy increase). About 1 October the Treasury issued an Estimates Circular to accounting officers of the civil services (the 1912–13 circular of 2 October 1911, signed C. Hobhouse, is the specimen). Forms were due by 1 December, latest 13 January; if detail would be late, an approximate total by 15 December so the Chancellor could foresee the aggregate. Even an estimate no larger than last year’s had to be justified; “the habit of regarding each year’s estimate as the starting point for the next” was singled out for vigilance. Departmental estimates were largely a summation of items already approved by the Treasury during the year.

Estimates were classified into Army, Navy, Civil Services, and Revenue Departments. The Treasury itself was treated as a civil service. Civil Services fell into seven historical classes: Public Works and Buildings; Salaries and Expenses of Civil Departments; Law and Justice; Education, Science and Art; Foreign and Colonial Services; Non-effective and Miscellaneous; Old-age Pensions, Labour Exchanges, Insurance, etc. Revenue Departments were Customs and Excise, Inland Revenue, and the Post Office. Appropriations were by votes — 15 Army, 15 Navy, 120 Civil, 3 Revenue, 153 in all. Classification was by organisation unit, one appropriation per branch, United Kingdom/England distinguished from Scotland and Ireland, effective from non-effective. Quarters, buildings, stationery, and printing were centralised in the Office of Works and the Stationery Office. Vote totals (except Army and Navy) could not be exceeded without Parliament; subhead allotments might be transferred with Treasury sanction (virement). Army and Navy might transfer even between votes. T. Gibson Bowles attacked army and navy vote transfers as defeating appropriation and creating a “huge pooled fund”; the reply was that without virement departments would pad every vote. Safeguards were Treasury authority, visible accounts, report to Commons, and Public Accounts Committee review.

“Appropriations in aid” assigned miscellaneous extra receipts of a service — fees, fines, sale of property, and, in a special case, War Office recoveries from Indian, colonial, and Egyptian governments of over £2½ million — as an aid to the formal grant from the Consolidated Fund. Extra receipts so treated did not pass through Exchequer receipt and issue accounts, though they were rigidly accounted for. Estimated extra receipts were the maximum usable; excess was surrendered as miscellaneous Consolidated Fund income. Where receipts exceeded needs (the Mint), only enough was appropriated in aid to cover expenditure, plus a token vote of £10 or £100 so the estimate still came before Parliament. The present form dated from Public Accounts Committee observations of 1881 and the Public Accounts and Charges Act 1891. Applied first to Army and Navy, then gradually to civil votes, the device avoided overstating revenue and expenditure (the Treasury in 1881 put the inflation at more than £4 million a year) and gave departments credit for realising receipts. Gross expenditure and appropriations in aid both appeared in the estimates. American observers still treated the practice as a breach of Consolidated Fund unity: net figures hid activity.

Financial power now resided wholly in the Commons. The Lords’ claim to amend money bills ended in the crisis over Lloyd George’s Budget of 1909 and the Parliament Act 1911. A Money Bill, certified by the Speaker, if sent up at least a month before the end of the session and not passed unamended within a month, was presented for Royal Assent without Lords consent. “Money Bill” covered taxation, charges on the Consolidated Fund or money provided by Parliament, supply, appropriation, receipt, custody, issue or audit of public money, and loans — not local rates. The Speaker’s certificate was conclusive and not questionable in any court. The People’s Budget struggle had shown how fiercely the financing of social reform and naval power could divide the constitution; the machinery of estimates and supply then ran on Commons rules alone.

Committees of Supply and Ways and Means were appointed at the beginning of the session (committee of the whole, not a select committee). Supply was first order on Thursdays. Not more than twenty days before 5 August were allotted to annual army, navy, and civil estimates including votes on account. On the last day but one the chairman put remaining class totals; on the last day the Speaker put outstanding reports. Authorization of expenditure (voting of supply) was distinguished from appropriation of funds (ways and means). Committee of Supply approved votes by resolution; the House agreed; Committee of Ways and Means voted the funds; only then was a bill drafted. Money was not available until the appropriation bill was signed, except votes on account (credit for use after 1 April while the session continued). Excess votes regularised overspending after the fact; votes of credit covered war or unforeseen large needs. The House in practice adopted estimates as submitted. Supply days were the opposition’s opportunity to debate policy and administration, not to recast figures; closure at twenty days forced remaining grants. The 1902–03 Select Committee on National Expenditure, barred from criticising policy, found Supply discussion partisan and more apt to urge increase than decrease. In 1912 a Select Committee on Estimates was established — scrutiny of detail without sharing ministerial responsibility for the programme of work.

Legislative control required a guardian of issues. Under the old Exchequer the Auditor held one of three keys; in 1811 Lord Grenville as Auditor refused a £500,000 issue despite five Lords of the Treasury, for want of Crown authority during George III’s incapacity. In 1834 Clerk of the Pells and Auditor were abolished and a Comptroller General of the Receipt and Issue of the Exchequer created. The Exchequer and Audit Departments Act 1866 consolidated issue-control and audit in one officer: Comptroller and Auditor General. The Treasury first obtained “credits” on the Exchequer. For Consolidated Fund services two Lords of the Treasury requisitioned the C&AG, who if satisfied granted credit by warrant. For Supply a royal order had to precede the requisition, because supply was granted to the Sovereign. Credit did not move cash. Issues went only to principal accountants: the Comptroller of the National Debt and the chief cashiers of the two banks (debt service); the accountants-general of the revenue departments; and the Paymaster-General, general paymaster for military and civil services. Principal accountants requisitioned the Treasury, which ordered the banks to transfer from credits to their accounts. The C&AG did not intervene at this second stage.

The Paymaster-General was banker, not accounting officer: he paid orders drawn by departmental accounting officers, satisfied as to form and supporting documents. He kept a separate book account for each civil vote and consolidated Army and Navy accounts, but one cash balance. Pay offices were consolidated 1830–56 to reduce idle balances. A Treasury minute of 21 February 1885 described his sources: extra receipts as appropriations in aid; Treasury Chest Fund; Civil Contingencies Fund; departmental deposit funds; and daily Consolidated Fund issues. He exhausted outside cash before drawing the Fund. Departmental accounting officers examined and settled claims and drew on the Paymaster. They were personally liable and were not bonded.

Expenditure of appropriations was not mandatory. Money was granted to the Crown and placed at ministers’ disposal; statute did not say “You shall spend so much.” The Treasury was the organ through which the Crown decided how far to use the grant. Theoretically every expenditure needed Treasury sanction. Normal continuing spend was taken as sanctioned unless the Treasury ordered retrenchment; any new clerk, new work, or departure required express sanction even if already in the printed estimate. Passing an estimate did not authorise an increased establishment; the Audit Office would not pass the spend without a separate Treasury letter. Control was over incurring liabilities — programme, plant, organisation, personnel, equipment — not over individual vouchers. The Treasury was technically a board. The Lord High Treasurer’s office had been put in commission in 1714; real authority passed to the Chancellor of the Exchequer. The First Lord was often Prime Minister and did not manage the office. The Chancellor was assisted by a Permanent Secretary and a Permanent Financial Secretary, both remaining through changes of ministry. The financial secretary’s staff grouped departments into seven divisions, each under a principal clerk who lived with those departments’ papers all year.

Civil works were concentrated in the Office of Works and Public Buildings: planning and construction of civil buildings at home and of diplomatic and consular buildings abroad; alteration, maintenance, and repair; royal palaces, parks, ancient monuments; renting, fixtures, furniture, fuel. No building was authorised except on the demand of the using service, presented not to Parliament but to Works, which decided whether to proceed after Treasury scrutiny — a single works programme. Estimates showed for each work original total, revised total, probable spend to date, amount voted this year, further amount to complete, and amount asked for next year. Military and naval works stayed with War Office and Admiralty. His Majesty’s Stationery Office supplied stationery and printing to all departments, military and naval as well as civil. It originated in Burke’s Administrative Reform Act of 1782. By 1914 it was a purchasing, contracting, distributing, and selling department with a turnover of £897,670. Departments submitted no stationery estimates. A single vote (1914–15 net £1,069,272 after £168,000 appropriations in aid) was accounted for under sixteen subheads. Until recently the lump under each subhead was available to all services; the Controller recommended, and the 1911 Select Committee on Publications adopted, allotment to departments on the basis of past spend, not to be exceeded without Treasury approval.

For a hundred and sixty years Commons had laboured under the illusion that it could control expenditure by putting checks upon issue from the Exchequer instead of ascertaining how the money had been spent. Tudor–Stuart Exchequer audit “limped leisurely” — in 1782 accounts twenty and thirty years old, some back to William III, were still open. Pitt created a Board of Audit that reported to the Treasury. Sir James Graham at the Admiralty in 1831 obtained an annual Navy Appropriation Account — the model of later appropriation accounts. The Public Monies Committee of 1856–57 “knocked on the head once for all” issue-watching as sufficient control. A standing order of 1861 (Gladstone) created the Committee of Public Accounts; the 1866 Act created the independent auditor. The C&AG was an officer of Parliament, appointed by the Crown, holding during good behaviour, removable only on address of both Houses, salary a Consolidated Fund charge — the same securities as judges. Yet the Treasury appointed his clerks and prescribed how accounts were kept and rendered. He audited appropriation accounts, might disallow items, and was expected to comment on unwise or extravagant expenditure, not merely legality. The Standing Committee of Public Accounts (originally eleven members, later fifteen; chairman by usage from the Opposition) examined those accounts. C&AG and a principal Treasury officer always attended. Functions extended to wisdom, faithfulness, and economy, to receipts as well as issues, and to the form of estimates. Committee recommendations had no force until the Treasury answered in a minute. The 1911 Epitome of the Reports from the Committees of Public Accounts 1857 to 1910 was the working code of accounting method.

Parliament received two sets of figures: Exchequer receipts and issues, and audited receipts and expenditure. Finance Accounts of the United Kingdom dated from 1802. From 1802 to 1866 the House learned only issues (imprests), not departmental expenditure. The 1913–14 volume was a compact cash statement of 101 small-octavo pages (ordered printed 25 May; the principal table as a special return 27 April). That return was the basis of the Chancellor’s budget proposals. The 1913–14 income-and-expenditure account showed tax and non-tax income totalling £198,242,897 against Consolidated Fund and Supply issues totalling £197,492,969, surplus £749,928. Appropriation Accounts were the C&AG’s audited statements of spend against votes, available many months later. There was no single general report that brought the two families of figures into one surveyed statement.

A budget was not a separate operation but the means of surveying both sides of the account together so that deficit or surplus was visible. Since the Revolution an annual statement “partaking of the nature of a budget” had been made; the phrase that the Chancellor “opens the budget” was in force soon after 1750 (from French bougette, a leather bag of papers). For 1914–15 three documents did the work: the Financial Statement 1914–1915 as laid before the House when the Chancellor opened the Budget on 4 May 1914 (seven pages — “in effect the real budget”); the Budget Speech of that day; and a six-page statement explaining rate changes. Tables compared 1913–14 estimated versus issued expenditure (Consolidated Fund £37.3 million issued; Supply £160.2 million; grand total issues £197.5 million; plus £4.4 million chargeable to capital — telegraphs, telephone transfer including National Telephone purchase, Post Office London Railway, Land Registry — total State responsibility £201.9 million); 1913–14 revenue versus budget (taxes £163.0 million against £160.0 million estimated; non-tax £35.2 million; total £198.2 million); and 1914–15 estimated expenditure (Supply up £9.9 million, of which Navy +£5.2 million, Civil +£2.1 million, Post Office +£1.8 million; total £206.0 million plus £5.3 million capital).

Ten working principles summarised the machine: a budget considering all needs at once; executive preparation of the plan; opposition opportunity to force answers in committee of the whole; residual responsibility remaining with the executive (Commons rules forbade motions to increase); no spend until Parliament approved, then rigid accounting; parliamentary audit by a C&AG with judicial tenure; a Public Accounts Committee chaired from the opposition; a Treasury not coordinate with spending departments but superior, acting as Parliament’s agent; distinction of votes, subheads, and supporting details, with virement under Treasury and audit safeguards; appropriations not mandatory — spend only on Treasury authorisation.
