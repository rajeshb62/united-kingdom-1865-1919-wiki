Free Trade and Tariff Reform

Free trade was the mid-Victorian orthodoxy of British commercial policy: the open import of food and manufactures without protective duties, and the belief that cheap bread and open markets served both the industrial nation and the consumer. Tariff reform was Joseph Chamberlain’s challenge to that orthodoxy—a programme of imperial preference and, if need be, retaliatory duties—which split the Unionist party, dominated electoral politics from 1903 to the Liberal landslide of 1906, and returned as the Unionist alternative to Free-Trade finance in the years of the People’s Budget and the struggle with the Lords.

Development

Cobdenite free trade had become almost a national creed after the repeal of the Corn Laws. By the early twentieth century, however, Germany and the United States had risen as protected industrial competitors, while self-governing Colonies offered preferential rates to British goods and asked for reciprocity. The orthodoxy that forbade Britain either to favour her Colonies or to retaliate when foreign Powers penalised them for doing so was still the “established fiscal policy of this country”—and it was precisely that policy which the Colonial Secretary set out to reopen.

On 15 May 1903 Chamberlain, Colonial Secretary and the most formidable Radical-turned-Imperialist of his generation, told his Birmingham constituents that his mind ran more on the future of the Empire than on smaller domestic controversies. The time had come, he said, deliberately to adopt or reject a policy of imperial unification. Preferential tariffs were the Colonies’ chosen instrument. A South African colonial conference had recommended a 25 per cent preference on dutiable goods; Australia and New Zealand had accepted the principle; Canada, already giving 25 and then 33⅓ per cent preference without equivalent, was ready to go further if Britain allowed a drawback on the shilling corn duty. Germany, treating Canada as a separate country, had penalised Canadian goods to deter other Colonies from following Canada’s example. Britain faced two alternatives: maintain “in all its severity” the interpretation of Free Trade placed on Cobden and Bright by a “small remnant of Little Englanders of the Manchester school,” and so remain precluded from favouring or protecting the Colonies; or refuse any purely technical definition of Free Trade, seek free interchange with the world, and yet “recover our freedom, resume that power of negotiation and, if necessary, retaliation whenever our own interests or our relations between our Colonies and ourselves are threatened by other people.” Make a mistake in Imperial policy, he warned, and it was irretrievable. He believed in a British Empire self-sustaining against the competition of all its rivals—not in a Little England dependent on the mercy of those who envied its prosperity.

The same day Arthur Balfour, Prime Minister, was defending the repeal of the corn duty to a deputation led by Henry Chaplin. He denied that the duty had ever been intended as protective; a preferential scheme was not yet possible, and until the mass of the people moved, a trifling food duty would only be the shuttlecock of opposing parties. The simultaneous appearance of these two positions—the Colonial Secretary calling for a revolution in fiscal policy while the Prime Minister dismantled the very tax that might have been its starting point—produced in the public mind “a condition of astonishment and perplexity bordering on stupefaction.” Chamberlain’s prestige as an Imperial statesman had never stood higher after his South African tour; yet within the Unionist party the divisions were immediate. Theoretical Protectionists, moderate “fiscal reformers,” and Free Traders separated at once. Vigorous sections discerned the danger of perpetual food taxes and the taint of Protection. The Spectator emerged as an energetic opponent; Liberals and the Liberal press opposed with almost complete unanimity. Rival fiscal organisations formed; Free Trade Unionist M.P.s met; Balfour refused a general fiscal discussion in the Commons.

The fissure proved irreparable. In September 1903, after Cabinet councils, Balfour’s pamphlet, and the Fiscal Blue-book, Chamberlain resigned to campaign openly, as did Mr. Ritchie and Lord George Hamilton; the Duke of Devonshire, Liberal Unionist leader, also left the Cabinet. Balfour’s Sheffield declarations and new appointments left the Unionists openly divided into three factions: Chamberlainite Tariff Reformers, Balfourite moderates who favoured a flexible but undefined “fiscal reform,” and Unionist Free Traders—later organised as the Unionist Free Food League and the Unionist Free Trade Club—who adhered to orthodox Cobdenite principles. Chamberlain threw himself into a nationwide speaking tour of extraordinary intensity: Glasgow and Greenock, Newcastle, South Wales, and, closing his first campaign on 18 January 1904 at the Guildhall in London, where four thousand hearers admitted by ticket drowned a band struggling to perform the National Anthem. Free imports, he argued, were not free exchange; Germany and the United States were rising as exporters while commercial powers that lost their industrial base—Holland, Venice, the Hanseatic cities—had decayed. The Tariff Reform League, founded in 1903, became a formidable propaganda machine; Devonshire, the Spectator, and Free Trade Unionists fought back. Asquith, Ritchie, Lord Spencer, Austen Chamberlain, and Rosebery filled the platforms on both sides. The fiscal question engrossed the public mind through 1904–5 “to such an extent as very perceptibly to draw off interest from” other questions, and reunited Liberals who had been disunited for twenty years on Home Rule. By-elections told against any tampering with free trade in food and raw materials: at Stalybridge in January 1905 the Liberal majority rose from 81 in 1900 to 951, a result attributed in cotton Lancashire to the conviction that free trade in the great industry would be prejudicially affected. Chinese labour in the Transvaal—denounced as “Chinese slavery”—the Education Act of 1902, and Irish embarrassments after Wyndham’s resignation compounded Unionist weakness. By the autumn of 1905 articles in the Times and the Daily Telegraph pointed to the Premier’s resignation.

On 4 December 1905 Balfour resigned. The King invited Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman to form a government, which he did by 10 December: Asquith at the Exchequer, Grey at the Foreign Office, Haldane at the War Office, Lloyd George at the Board of Trade, Morley at the India Office, and John Burns—the first working-class man to enter a British Cabinet—at the Local Government Board. The election campaign that followed was judged the most exciting and startling since 1880, if not since 1832. The late Ministers were arraigned for reckless extravagance, misjudgment of South African affairs, Chinese labour, the endowment of denominational education, and “crude and self-contradictory ‘fiscal reforms.‘” Liberals claimed to be the real Free Traders and apostles of economic progress. Among issues the fiscal question was probably the most important, especially in rural constituencies; next came Chinese labour; then modification of the Education Act of 1902. Devonshire, writing on 29 December 1905 to the Unionist Free Trade Club, declared the Tariff Reform League’s proposals “indistinguishable from pure Protection,” and that Free Trade Unionists should support Free Trade candidates irrespective of party. Chamberlain attacked the new ministry as a Home Rule and Little Englander Government which must exist, if at all, by Irish votes. Campbell-Bannerman declared that “Protection was immoral, oppressive and corrupting.” At Derby on 3 January Chamberlain claimed a million able-bodied male paupers under Free Trade—a misuse of the poor-law figures which Free Trade economists immediately challenged. Asquith at Perth insisted Ministers could not take steps that would raise the cost of food and lower the standard of living.

Polling for the general election of 1906 began on 12 January and ended on 13 February with Orkney and Shetland. Manchester and Salford were swept clear of Unionists; Balfour himself was defeated in East Manchester, for which he had sat since 1885, by 6,403 to 4,423. London, Tyneside, the West Riding, Glasgow (five of seven divisions), and the agricultural districts all swung against the Unionists. Lyttelton fell at Leamington; Chaplin at Sleaford; Brodrick at Guildford; Sir W. Hart Dyke, “Father of the House,” at Dartford to a Labour candidate. Birmingham alone among great industrial centres held solid for Unionism. In Greenwich and King’s Lynn the Unionist vote split between Free Trader and Tariff Reformer; Lord Hugh Cecil came bottom of the poll at Greenwich. Final results: Liberals 377, Labour 53 (29 from the Labour Representation Committee sitting as an independent party), Nationalists 83—Ministerialist total 513—against Conservatives 132 and Liberal Unionists 25, Opposition 157. The Liberal and Labour majority over all others was 134; the prediction that the Government would depend on Nationalist votes was falsified. Within the Opposition a Times analysis counted about 109 Chamberlainite Tariff Reformers, 32 Balfourites, and only 11 Unionist Free Fooders (later revised by Devonshire to 102, 36, and 16). Wales returned no Unionist; Scotland became predominantly Liberal. Campbell-Bannerman reaffirmed Free Trade as the settled doctrine of the Government; Asquith, as Chancellor, warned that a 2s. corn duty would mean a rise of about 2½d. on the four-pound loaf.

Free trade thus remained the commercial policy of the new Parliament, and the victory cleared the way for a decade of Liberal social legislation financed under Free-Trade assumptions—old-age pensions, progressive taxation, national insurance—while Tariff Reform survived as the chief item in the Conservative programme. Clayton, writing while these measures were still new, observed that legislative proposals for social reform were treated as non-party questions, and that Tariff Reform “was adopted and is advocated mainly as a social reform, a cure for industrial evils, and the misery of unemployment.” The two paths of Edwardian betterment stood in competition: Free-Trade finance taxing great incomes and land values for pensions and the Navy, versus imperial preference and duties on foreign manufactures framed as a remedy for industrial distress.

That competition returned with full force when Lloyd George introduced the Finance Bill of 1909—the People’s Budget—to fund old-age pensions and naval construction. Austen Chamberlain, moving rejection on 7 June, indicated that the Opposition alternative was Tariff Reform. Lord Curzon at Leeds in October called the Budget’s socialistic feature its intention to “side-track” Tariff Reform. When the House of Lords rejected the Bill by 350 to 75 on 30 November 1909, the constitutional crisis joined the financing of pensions and the Navy to free trade against Tariff Reform and to the Irish demand that Home Rule no longer be blocked by an hereditary chamber. The January 1910 election was fought with unexampled vigour—about 250 meetings by Peers in five weeks; the Tariff Reform League and Free Trade Union spending lavishly—and turned on the Lords’ rejection, Tariff Reform versus Free Trade, and Home Rule. The result was a hung Parliament—Liberals 275, Unionists 273, Labour 40, Irish Nationalists 82. The Finance Bill then passed and was accepted by the Lords in April 1910. Through the Constitutional Conference Free Trade Unionists urged that food taxes be postponed; the Morning Post ranked Tariff Reform above the constitutional issue. At Mile End on 21 November Lloyd George put the popular case: the Government needed money for defence and social reforms by taxing great incomes, while the Lords demanded that the burden fall on the bread and meat of the people. The Attorney-General warned that Tariff Reform, once imposed, could never be repealed if the Lords controlled finance. The December 1910 election left the balance substantially unchanged; the Parliament Act of 1911 stripped the Lords of absolute veto; Free-Trade finance and social reform advanced under Asquith and Lloyd George while Unionism retained Tariff Reform as its unfinished imperial and industrial programme.

By the outbreak of the Great War the electorate of 1906 had long since refused food taxes and a general tariff, yet the argument between open markets and imperial preference had not been settled inside the Unionist party or as a permanent settlement of commercial policy. Free trade remained the settled practice of Liberal government and the commercial face of mid-Victorian orthodoxy; Tariff Reform remained the commercial cement of Empire in the minds of Chamberlain’s followers, and the competing social cure for unemployment on the Unionist benches. The fiscal question that opened on 15 May 1903 thus framed a decade of party realignment, electoral upheaval, and the financing of the social state—without ever wholly leaving the unfinished business of Edwardian politics.

J. A. Hobson, writing Imperialism: A Study before Chamberlain’s 1903 campaign had yet remade Unionist politics, already treated protection and imperialism as allies rather than alternatives. Part I, chapter V—“Imperialism based on Protection”—argued that increased military and naval outlay was best read as insurance premiums for existing colonial markets and as current outlay on new ones. Any considerable, calculable increase of revenue by indirect taxation meant the abandonment of Free Trade: duties on necessaries and prime conveniences of life and trade had a protective effect whether or not they were labelled revenue. Imperialist finance was not yet prepared to admit the name; preparatory steps found other names—a countervailing duty on beet-sugar posing as an instrument of Free Trade, a tax on prison-made goods followed by protection against all “sweated” foreign industry, export duties on coal, military necessity as the most formidable mask (home food supply and a rural recruiting stock), bounties in the shipbuilding trade, and, above all, “Free Trade within the Empire,” a protectionist tariff masquerading as an Imperial Zollverein. Chamberlain’s attempt of 1897 was already named as a witness; the South African war merely precipitated the policy. “In many ways it thus appears that Protection is the natural ally of Imperialism.”

The economic root, stated in the following chapter as the taproot of Imperialism, was the desire of organised industrial and financial interests to secure, at the public expense and by the public force, private markets for surplus goods and surplus capital. Everywhere appeared excessive powers of production and capital in search of investment: more goods than could be sold at a profit, more capital than could find remunerative employment at home. Surplus savings that could not assist current consumption either lay idle or sought speculative use abroad “under the protection of the Government.” If the consuming public had raised its standard of consumption with every rise of productive power, there would have been no excess of goods or capital clamorous to use Imperialism in order to find markets. A Liberal party committed to militant Imperialism, Hobson added, was in a hopeless dilemma: it had neither the pluck nor the principle to renounce Imperialism or to insist that the classes who sought to benefit by it should pay for it by direct taxation on property. Protection thus fed imperialism, and imperialism fed protection—the two policies locking surplus capital into protected fields overseas and into a fiscal system that would hide its exclusive side under imperial loyalty.
