The Party System

The Party System was the machinery by which, after the enlargement of the electorate, two Front Benches in the House of Commons acted as one Governing Group: they filled ministries by co-option, allotted the time-table of the House, selected candidates and programmes through the caucus, and financed elections from unaudited secret funds. Representation, in the democratic sense of laws the mass desired, did not exist; the assembly had been rendered null, and the public, still taking sides as at a boat-race, mistook a sham contest for self-government.

Development

Democracy is government by the general will: wherever the laws the people desire are passed and those they dislike rejected, there is democracy; wherever the laws have no relation to that desire, there is none. Votes, elections, and assemblies are only machinery. Pure democracy is the elders under the village tree; representation is the indirect substitute, and it requires free selection of members, strict responsibility to constituents, and independence of the Executive. None of those conditions obtained. A member who voted opposite to Bethnal Green was merely an oligarch. The force that turned the assembly into an engine of oligarchy was the Party System. The “Will of the People” dispute of the two elections of 1910 reduced itself to whether that will was best represented by the Duke of Sussex or by his son-in-law, the Right Honourable James Blagg.

The public that the machine exploited was still, in the main, honest. A Conservative leader had to denounce land taxes his followers sincerely detested, even if he had no intention of repealing them. Enthusiasts remained: Conservatives who thought the Liberal Government was held from spoliation only by the Opposition; Liberals who saw Asquith and Churchill as tribunes of a people struggling to be free. Single-issue men—Tariff Reformers, churchmen, teetotalers, the Irish to whom nothing mattered but nationality—counted at elections. The mass took sides year after year as crowds take Oxford or Cambridge, with no control of the crew. An uneasy minority noticed laws they detested passed by both parties, desired measures never mentioned, the Jameson Raid inquiry stifled by both Front Benches, Balfour’s hesitations on Tariff Reform, and the meaningless Conference of 1910. They blamed Asquith for being too Whiggish or Balfour for being weak, not seeing that Balfour was strong in the traditions of party and the net of relationships that covered English politics.

The system had not always been the organised imposture of 1911. Seventeenth-century conflict was real because men were willing to fight. 1689 was made not only by but for wealthy intrigues with a mainly financial object; more than six million acres of common land were enclosed after Hanover. Parliament then represented a narrow class, but within that class Pitt and Fox did not speak to each other and did not, like Asquith and Balfour, belong to the same set. Direct bribery of the legislature ceased, perhaps because of the growing impotence of the Commons rather than virtue. Industrial wealth and French Revolutionary ideas produced 1832; later Reform Acts enlarged the electorate while power silently passed from the Commons to the Cabinet. Unreal issues, defined neither by the people nor by Parliament but by the politicians, gave a semblance of reality to an empty competition.

After the house-of-lords threw out Lloyd George’s Budget in 1909, Asquith at the Albert Hall pledged he would neither assume nor retain office without guarantees that the veto would be limited. He won the January 1910 election, assumed office, retained office, and produced no guarantees. Instead he entered a conference with his alleged opponents, six months supposedly spent bridging an “unbridgable gulf.” Both sides then announced that the Conference had failed, and another election was declared necessary, though there was nothing to vote about. The Conference did not fail: it saved for a moment the life of the moribund Party System. The 1906 mandate had been squandered—Chinese Labour, the Licensing Bill, the collapse and absorption of the Labour Party. “Down with the Lords!” rallied supporters, then was smothered when a handful of Radicals demanded fulfilment. The Opposition’s refusal to attack the broken Albert Hall pledge was evidence of collusion: practically every move was arranged beforehand by the confederates on the two Front Benches. Governments abandoned causes because they consulted the sense of the Governing Group; Oppositions tolerated the abandonment because they wished to preserve the system that would, by the connivance of those rivals, bring them office in turn.

Ministers were not nominated by the Crown or by Parliament: they nominated themselves. They formed a self-elected body, filling vacancies by co-option. The two Front Benches were one close oligarchical corporation. Admission cost complete surrender of independence. Recruits fell into Relations (blood and marriage—not to be confused with the old privileges of the Great Houses), Private Secretaries (sons of the new rich or of party subscribers; Montagu and Herbert Samuel, related to Lord Swaythling), and make-weights (lawyers, an ex-workman, anyone who had given annoyance while independent). Asquith in 1892 was unconnected by any direct tie; by 1911 he and his were connected by a dozen such ties. Office, unlike the kingdom of heaven, was not taken by storm.

The kinship list made the imposture visible: Norfolk as Conservative Postmaster-General; Austen Chamberlain inheriting rank from his father; the Prime Minister’s brother-in-law Tennant on the Treasury bench; Churchill with a double right; Selborne, son-in-law of Salisbury, governing South Africa while first cousin Arthur Balfour was Prime Minister and brother Gerald enjoyed office and pension; Liberal Whip Sir John Fuller brother-in-law of Hobhouse, both grandchildren by blood or marriage of Conservative Chancellor St Aldwyn; Masterman nephew by marriage of Conservative Colonial Secretary Lyttelton; Asquith and Lyttelton married sisters; Churchill cousin of Marlborough and his successor as Under Secretary for the Colonies. If Lady Capulet were a born Montague, Lady Montague first cousin of old Capulet, Mercutio nephew of a Capulet and brother-in-law of a Montague, the feud at Verona would be kept up mainly for the entertainment of the people. Tolerance was extended only to those who “played the game,” not to Parnell or Victor Grayson. Concealment of the relationships was sound moral proof that their motive was imposture.

The Placeman was as prominent as in Walpole’s day, only more decently veiled. He voted as the Whips dictated, tabled blocking motions, or played at independence without ever touching what was inconvenient to both Front Benches. Masterman, independent critic of Burns and of the Education Bill, voted for the Labour Right to Work Bill on 13 March 1908; in May he accepted office at £1,200 (later £1,500); on 20 April 1909 he voted against the same Bill. He was regarded as a man of exceptionally high ideals. Barristers abounded because prizes were in the gift of the group; Buckmaster moved the amendment that shelved secrecy of the Party Funds. Those funds, unaudited, were immediately withdrawn from any member who voted against the Government.

“Discipline” was a military word fatal to criticism. Rank and file were held under a rigidity nothing short of civil war could justify, while commanders concocted their plan of campaign in concert. The 1910 Conference was only one of a hundred conferences of leaders, secretaries, Chief Whips, confidential hangers-on, and principal official paymasters. Agreement was the normal method of governing the country. Three cases of halted victory showed the alliance: the Jameson Raid committee, always on the verge of a startling revelation, stopped like a newspaper serial because Harcourt, Campbell-Bannerman, and Ellis were Front Benchers as eager as any Tory to hush the Colonial Office; Chinese Labour, the issue of 1906, not followed by immediate repatriation—anxious negotiations to propitiate the South African Jews, 1,300 Chinese admitted after the change of government, and the new House not allowed to divide on the question; the Conference itself, which openly branded as a farce the rhetoric of five months before.

A prediction followed as a test. After sham demagogy and sham indignation, Lords’ powers would be altered so that the Lords could not stop measures concerted between the two Front Benches, and could stop end-of-Parliament “swing of the pendulum” bills. The reconstituted House would drop the country squires who were in one sense really representative of England and keep purchasers of peerages, professional politicians, and bishops; new elements would be nominees of the machine, or, as the pretty phrase went, “of the Crown.” Each Front Bench wanted the prizes of office, but that aim was subordinate to the essential aim of both: the maintenance of the Party System. Speaker and Chairman of Committees, impartial because they knew the fight was sham, supported them when they agreed. This governing group allotted the time of the House and what might be said.

Five-sixths or more of time was at the Government’s disposal under Standing Orders; closure ended discussion automatically. Opposition protest was merely ceremonial; Balfour showed a disposition to drop it. Private members had one afternoon a week, insecurely held; no opposed private member’s Bill had in recent years become law without ministerial facilities. After consultation with the Opposition Front Bench, officials approached Buckmaster to nullify the debate on secrecy of the Party Funds; his amendment confined the evil to the Tariff Reform League; Unionists moved a twin amendment on the Free Trade Union; the division became an ordinary party division. The “blocking motion”—notice of a motion never moved—prevented anyone else discussing the subject (Rees in the 1906 Parliament, especially India). Adjournment on a matter of urgent public importance required forty members; with Front Benches united this was hard to find, the House usually attended by a dozen or twenty. Foreign affairs got a few hours a year. Question time allowed nothing but a bare answer, which might be refused.

The repressive machinery was most infrequent—applied only to rare revolts—which is why the public did not see it. There could be all the sham fight desired upon the sham issues the bosses had arranged, but no initiative on the part of the electorate. A concrete example made the cheat exact: a supposed Distress Relief Bill of 1910, verbal amendments occupying pages of Hansard, the vital clause never reached, closure by the Front Benches. Substitute destitute people over seventy for South Lancashire, the Old Age Pensions Bill of 1908 for the Bill, and destitute people between sixty-five and seventy for the West Riding, and the account of the summer of 1908 was literal. The procedure had been used simply to cheat the people.

The tone of the House was stupid and rather degraded, much lower than that of the House of Lords, death to rhetoric, an air in which it was much easier not to bother. It was a far smaller factor than visitors supposed. A dozen determined men could have broken the Front Bench arrangements from within. Labour’s absorption was not caused by Tone but by a definite compact with the Executive by which places, advantage in moving motions, and ultimately perhaps Cabinet rank should be the price of compromise. That compact was the parliamentary fate of labour-representation. The real grip was secret Party Funds, the control of elections, and the choice of candidates.

The most important fact about English politics was the fact that nobody mentioned. Two huge war-chests were secretly subscribed and secretly disbursed. Light on collection came from the Honours List; on expenditure, from the division list. They were subscribed by rich men who wanted some advantage, financial or social, from the Government, and spent in paying the expenses of members—in other words, in corrupting the legislature. Organisers proclaimed perennial penury and issued bogus appeals to working men; there would be no lack of funds so long as the supply of peerages and baronetcies was unchecked. Expenditure was Walpole’s Secret Service, refined: instead of buying votes after election, the gang ensured that no one should be elected who was not prepared so to vote, eliminating the chance of an incorruptible member. The member answered to the caucus that paid him. Not one man in thirty knew that there were such things as Party Funds.

There was a market for peerages in Downing Street as there was a market for cabbages in Covent Garden. A peerage conveyed not only dignity but legislative power: a Peer was a Senator. Among ten creations in twenty years, a few (Peel, Kitchener, Curzon, Morley, Fisher) fit the senatorial idea. The 1910 Liberal Birthday Honours, issued while the Government denounced the Peers, created seven peers: Causton (Southwark), Foster (Ilkeston), Kearley (Devonport), Pearson (Cowdray), Holland (Rotherham), Freeman-Thomas (Willingdon), Furness. Ilkeston, President of the National Liberal Federation, was elevated mainly because he gave up a safe seat for Colonel Seely. Causton, unpaid Paymaster, rejected at Southwark in January 1910, received a consolation prize that was a right to make and unmake laws. Furness, unseated on petition, promptly received a coronet. Holland was an enormously rich cotton-owner. Pearson’s contracting company had recently articled Lloyd-George junior. Conservatives were no cleaner; some families paid both chests. The Guests received Wimborne in 1880, crossed in 1904, and in 1909, just when the politicians were loudest in denunciation of the Peers, Ivor Guest became Lord Ashby St Ledger. A Conservative Government made Alfred Harmsworth Lord Northcliffe; a Liberal Government gave a brother a baronetcy.

Worse than the sale of honours was traffic in policies. Cecil Rhodes’s letters to Schnadhorst offered sums running into tens of thousands on condition Egypt remain British and the Cape-to-Cairo railway be viewed favourably. Money was paid; Gladstone, publicly for evacuation, did not evacuate. In 1903 Dr Rutherfoord Harris announced publicly that he had sent £10,000 to the Conservative Party Funds; shortly afterwards a Conservative Government sanctioned Chinese labour on the Rand. Liberals, winning 1906 on the issue, still refused a division and negotiated so that Chinese stayed until they had done their work in reducing the wages of the Kaffirs. Drink was the standing case. The public never heard municipalisation or free trade in liquor; the only choice was Balfour’s Licensing Bill (endowment of brewing and distilling firms out of public funds) versus Asquith’s (irritating restrictions leading logically to ultimate prohibition). Conservatives needed brewers of tied houses; Liberals needed cocoa manufacturers, grocers who sold drink retail, and rich faddists who would prohibit. The people chose between the brewer and the cocoa-manufacturer, and usually preferred the brewer.

The Central Office was not representative of the people nor even of active party members. National Liberal Federation and National Union of Conservative Associations passed resolutions that had the smallest effect until ratified by the Central Office. The Office was the hinge between the Front Bench group and local organisations, themselves largely dominated by the local rich men and too democratic to be trusted with the reality of political power. Its head, nominated by the Governing Group, held the purse-strings of the secret sums and made arrangements with subscribers of which the Leader was conventionally supposed to know nothing.

Free local choice of candidates did not happen. Either the richest local man paid the local organisation and was confirmed by the machine, or an outsider approached Central Office: a rich man put down a subscription and the seat’s safety varied with the amount; a poor man fought hopeless seats as a useful gladiator, preferably via a private secretaryship, especially if a lawyer. The Fortunatus Purse of the Party Funds was placed unreservedly at his disposal if he sacrificed freedom and honour. Independents lacked money; there was no second ballot; electors feared to waste votes. Only three types normally entered: local rich men, outside rich men who had suborned the centre, and poor men willing to be obedient servants of the caucus.

An election was decided first by salaried officials: purchasers first; university speakers; heredity (a Cecil, a Howard, a Churchill, or a Rothschild); poor advocates sent first to seats safe for the other colour. The test was willingness to defend not only the existing brief but any future decision. Locally there was no Westminster collusion: prominent Liberals really differed in type from prominent Conservatives. The official organisation was a few dozen men—never a hundred, often twenty or thirty—who sent one to London to fetch someone on the list. In ninety-nine cases they accepted the nominee. Pledges made under popular excitement were expected to be broken always in the spirit and even, if necessary, in the letter. Nomination required cash equivalent to a well-paid skilled artisan’s yearly income; avowed expenses averaged £1,000 and came in reality to nearly double. Winning by 10 per cent of the electorate was enormous; by 2 per cent was not even close. The winner went to Westminster to vote for anything whatsoever that the machine might order.

Two programmes were drawn up by the politicians, usually after consultation with each other. Framing the question was to go a long way towards framing the answer. Free Trade in the 1840s was forced on the legislature by a popular demand; Tariff Reform began when a Cabinet Minister declared for Protection and professional politicians who had never doubted Free Trade converted overnight. Drink and Education Bills were framed for sectarian and trade subscribers, not for parents. In November 1910 the Front Benches agreed the Osborne Judgment should not be an electoral issue and that payment of members should side-track the demand of the workers; the Labour Party of course gave way. The Unemployed were not allowed to vote on the Right to Work Bill. “Non-controversial measures” meant all violently unpopular proposals for the oppression of the poor which happened in no way to affect the professional politicians (Children’s Bill, Prevention of Crimes Bill). Licensing clauses of the People’s Budget, certainly unpopular, would be continued by Conservatives when by mutual arrangement their turn of office came. Irish-home-rule and Protection, launched against the rules, were turned by digestion into unreal issues. Legislation had passed to that Standing Committee of Professional Politicians called in the House of Commons the Two Front Benches.

Beneficiaries said states were organic; Britain had unbroken institutional continuity; under the Party System England had recently been the wealthiest and almost the strongest nation; leave well alone unless a concrete alternative existed. Every question had a yes and a no; an unchecked Executive was worse; two salaried teams arguing Protection versus Free Trade produced open discussion; the system supplied an alternative government without violence. The last argument rested on ignorance and vanity. Britain no longer had the offensive strength, economic position, or social condition it claimed; continental peoples regarded the English poor with pity and horror. The old Whig/Tory philosophical division was real into the last quarter of the nineteenth century; by 1911 not the memory, not the savour of a real distinction remained. Between wage-earners for Jones and for Smith there was no line, not even the vaguest dotted line. The system could not supply an alternative government for a vital change, because it depended upon the conception that there could not be any vital change. Not one great national issue since the repeal of the Corn Laws had had genuine party debate; real disagreement produced stalemate, because a permanent victory would be the death of the system by which both sets of politicians obtained their bread-and-butter. Because it was no longer a real contest of ideals, it could not register or resolve the conflicts that actually pressed—poverty at home, foreign insult abroad.

Scholarship would say an institution in this condition was past praying for. Yet nothing was appearing that could take the place of Parliament. Most statutes were the work of permanent officials; the Commons, fallen into a universally recognised decay, was still the only instrument for making laws. Franchise extension would only drill twenty thousand where ten thousand were drilled now. Abolishing plural voting would be met by a new cry to rebalance the teams. Payment of official election expenses would leave the independent disadvantaged against the Fortunatus fund; payment of members would not stop larger future bribes. Auditing party funds would spawn a second secret fund. A short, indissoluble Parliament of four years at most would be more to the purpose: a vote of censure would not dissolve the House, so members could vote freely. Even an adumbration of independent voting had forced Campbell-Bannerman to give way on the Trades Disputes Bill. Jowett’s proposal to replace collective Cabinet responsibility with departmental committees would still fail in a packed House without a fixed term. Primaries would be valuable but hard to graft. Balfour’s Referendum was obviously insincere unless joined to Initiative. Machinery failed unless the democracy refused party hacks. The first need was exposure; the second, pledges inside the machine—not vague promises, but a pledge to vote against whatever Government unless scheduled measures passed the Commons by a date. Had members in 1906 been pledged to vote against the Government unless the first Chinese left by 1 March 1906 and the last by 31 December, the politician would have been caught. If the mass would not organise, the law-making institution of England was done with. The body would jerk, perhaps—it would not revive. Until a successor organ appeared, the country faced one of those evil spaces, subject to foreign insult and domestic misfortune, when nations lose grip over their own destinies.

Compared with Germany (the Emperor choosing responsible heads in public view), France (ministries satisfying a fluctuating Chamber, with no automatic dissolution and no standing compact of two Front Benches), and the United States (a President personally choosing heads excluded from congressional party rigidity), Britain vested recruitment in a dozen or so who co-opted continuously. Parliament had no actual check. Today it was impossible to conceive of any appointment, however monstrous, which would lead to remonstrance from the drilled voting machine.

Collusion was not the absence of all conflict. What the opponents preserved was the conditions under which they and their clique should retain the opportunities for large salaries. They prevented division on the sale of honours, connections between antagonists, procedure, private members’ time, salaries, public committees. No one important policy for fifteen years had been allowed to form a clear division, with the exception of Women’s Suffrage—and even that reached law only if the Front Benches permitted; they leaned toward a well-to-do franchise, not adult women’s suffrage. Administration, especially where great financiers and some cosmopolitan banker in Egypt or Ceylon were concerned, was concerted. Notes were tossed across the table; Whips conferred openly to prevent majority decisions.

The daily press was more devoted to the Party System than any part of the State except the professional politicians themselves. Liberal advocacy: Star, Daily News, Manchester Guardian, Westminster Gazette. The other team: Times, Daily Telegraph, Birmingham Daily Post, Pall Mall Gazette. Writers on a “Conservative” paper might be Socialists; a “Free Trade” paper might be owned by a Protectionist. The politicians’ only regular lever was honours to proprietors, now rather a matter of routine. The attitude was not wicked, it was merely stupid. Reformers should attack the parliamentary game, that machine for grinding wind, not expect to purify newspapers first. The Press had long ceased to affect opinion on party; it still affected it by selection of foreign news.

Parliamentary-reform that merely widened the drilled electorate, and social measures such as pensions and the People’s Budget that the two Benches could close or chew into unreal issues, left the oligarchy intact. The Party System was the name of that oligarchy.
