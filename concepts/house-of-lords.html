House of Lords

The House of Lords was the hereditary second chamber of the United Kingdom Parliament: a body of peers whose power to amend or reject Commons bills had long coexisted with the convention that money bills belonged to the Lower House. In the decades after 1865 that balance became the central constitutional problem of British politics whenever a Liberal ministry commanded the Commons but faced a permanent Conservative majority above the bar. The chamber stood as a hereditary check on the elected House—revising, delaying, and, when a Liberal government was in power, destroying measures that the electors had returned a majority to pass. Great Britain remained unique in combining democratic forms with a second chamber of hereditary legislators; the democratic grievance against it was not merely hereditary membership but one-sided use of the veto.

Development

Liberal conflicts before the Edwardian crisis

Conflict between the Houses was not new. Over Irish Church disestablishment in 1869 a compromise was found and the Lords, on the whole, gave way. In 1871, when the Lords proposed to amend the Army Reform Bill abolishing purchase of commissions, Gladstone advised the Crown to cancel the royal warrant that made purchase legal and issue a new warrant ending the sale—so that rejection would only deprive officers of the compensation the bill awarded. The device completely worsted the Lords. In 1884 the Lords refused the rural labourers’ enfranchisement bill unless redistribution accompanied it; Gladstone yielded, and franchise and redistribution passed together as the Third Reform Act.

The most consequential pre-Edwardian collision was over Home Rule. Gladstone’s Second Home Rule Bill of 1893 passed the Commons by 301 to 267—the first Home Rule measure ever to clear the Lower House—and was rejected by the Conservative-dominated Lords by 419 to 41. With no statutory means of overriding the absolute veto, the bill was dead. Home Rule entered a decade of eclipse, and the Upper House became part of the Irish question itself: so long as peers could kill a Commons-passed Irish measure, Nationalist support for any Liberal ministry would demand not only a bill but a remedy for the chamber that had killed its predecessor.

One-sided veto after 1906

After the Liberal landslide of 1906—the election-1906-tariff-reform that returned a majority swollen by Labour and Irish Nationalist support—the Upper House rejected or heavily amended a series of Liberal measures. Education and licensing bills felt the weight of revision; there was no pretence of revising Conservative measures with the same severity. The permanent and overwhelming Conservative majority acted on the assumption that a Liberal ministry did not represent the will of the people—an assumption at variance with democratic theory and with the constitutional practice of the Crown. In June 1907 the Commons resolved, by 432 to 147, that the power of the other House to alter or reject bills must be so restricted by law that within a single Parliament the final decision of the Commons should prevail. That resolution was the seed of the Parliament Bill; between its adoption and the Act of 1911 came the Budget rejection, two general elections, and a failed joint conference.

The Budget of 1909 and the breach of convention

The crisis broke over finance. Parliament opened on 16 February 1909 under King Edward VII with a Speech already warning that old-age provision and the Navy would raise expenditure. Asquith insisted that finance must be the first business: social-reform had to be paid for, and the Old Age Pension Act was only the first instalment. On 29 April David Lloyd George, as Chancellor of the Exchequer, introduced the Finance Bill that became the People’s Budget—a “War Budget” against poverty. It proposed a 20 per cent tax on the unearned increment of land when sold; a halfpenny in the pound on the capital value of undeveloped land; a 10 per cent reversion duty on a landlord’s interest at lease expiry; increased death duties; and a super-tax on incomes above £5,000—proceeds to fund pensions and naval construction amid accelerating Anglo-German rivalry. Balfour declared that the force behind the Budget was “really Socialism.” Austen Chamberlain, moving rejection on second reading, indicated that the Opposition alternative was Tariff Reform.

By October independent Unionist peers were making the case for rejection. Lord Curzon at Leeds insisted that the Lords had only waived, never abandoned, the right to amend a Finance Bill, and had never waived the right of rejection—whether for tacking, as mischievous, or to refer principles to the people. After five months of Commons debate the Finance Bill went up to the Lords. On 30 November 1909, by 350 to 75, the House rejected it—the first rejection of a finance measure since 1860, and a breach of the convention, standing since the seventeenth century, that the Lords did not interfere with money bills. The Earl of Crewe, moving second reading, faced a resolution from the Marquess of Lansdowne asserting the Lords’ right to reject a Money Bill; Lansdowne argued that Commons privileges were now interpreted strictly, that “tacking” had increased, and that the Budget would merely be reserved for the approval of the people. The Lord Chancellor (Lord Loreburn) replied that rejection was “legal, but not constitutional”: supply was the exclusive function of the Commons, and rejection was a direct invasion of their privilege and the powers of the Crown. The conflict, he warned, could only end ultimately in one way.

Two elections and the Constitutional Conference

The Lords’ rejection forced a dissolution. The January–February 1910 election turned on the constitutional struggle opened by rejection of the Finance Bill, on Tariff Reform versus Free Trade, and on Home Rule. The result was a hung Parliament—Liberals 275, Unionists 273, Labour 40, Irish Nationalists 82—leaving the ministry dependent on Irish and Labour votes, with a united majority of about 124. The Finance Bill was passed promptly by the new Commons and accepted by the Lords in April 1910—the Peers having, as they claimed, referred it to the people and now bowed to the electoral result on that measure alone.

The constitutional question—the Lords’ veto—now became the central issue. Opposition speakers declared that there had never been a deadlock between the House of Lords and the country, and that Veto resolutions would reduce the Upper House to legislative impotence. The Attorney-General (Sir W. Robson) replied that if the Lords controlled finance they could control the Administration and force dissolutions; they would have unlimited power without responsibility; Tariff Reform, once imposed, could never be repealed if the Lords could stand in the way. The death of Edward VII in May cast a temporary hush over party warfare. On 17 June the Constitutional Conference opened—an attempt by leading men from both sides to compose the differences between the Houses without a further appeal to the constituencies. An influential section of Unionist Peers held that if they were to be deprived of their powers it should be done by the constituencies and not by a conference. Rumours later attributed the final disruption to Lords Lansdowne and Cawdor and to the numerical strength of majorities required on bills sent back after rejection. The Conference continued through summer and early autumn without public disclosure; its failure was welcomed by advanced Liberals, Labour, and the main body of the Nationalists.

On 18 November Asquith insisted that Dissolution was best got over at once: Liberal legislation did not get a fair chance, and the Lords only forced a Dissolution when Liberals were in office. Lloyd George, addressing 5,000 men at Mile End on 21 November, said the crisis had been precipitated by rejection of the Budget: the Government needed money for defence and social reforms by taxing great incomes, while the Lords demanded that the burden fall on the bread and meat of the people. The December 1910 election left the balance of parties substantially as before—a united majority of about 126. Unionist speakers insisted that the Parliament Bill was a dodge to smuggle through Home Rule. The Liberal–Irish–Labour coalition now had a renewed mandate to reform the House of Lords.

The Parliament Act, 1911

King George V opened his first Parliament on 6 February 1911 with a Speech promising proposals “for settling the relations between the two Houses of Parliament, with the object of securing the more effective working of the Constitution.” The Parliament Bill embodied the 1907 resolution. It declared that a Money Bill sent up by the Commons, if not passed unamended by the Lords within a month, should receive the Royal Assent without them; that any other bill passed by the Commons in three successive sessions should become law in the third session without the Lords’ assent; and that the maximum duration of a Parliament should fall from seven years to five. After two days’ debate, leave was given on 22 February by 351 to 227 to introduce the Bill.

In the Lords, Committee began on 28 June. The Earl of Cromer moved to transfer the decision on money bills from the Speaker to a Joint Committee; supported by Lansdowne and the Archbishop of Canterbury, it was carried by 183 to 44, but the Government refused it. Unionist peers divided between those who counselled surrender and the “die-hards” or “Halsburyites,” led by the Earl of Halsbury and numbering between 100 and 150 peers determined to reject or emasculate the Bill. When the Lords returned the measure with unacceptable amendments, Asquith announced—as in 1832—guarantees from the Crown that peers would be created in numbers sufficient to pass it. Lord Midleton and the Earl of Rosebery pressed for a clear statement as to the “guarantees.” To avoid some three or four hundred Liberal creations, Lansdowne—following the Duke of Wellington’s example—advised Conservative peers to refrain from opposition. On 10 August 1911 the Bill passed the Lords by 131 to 114; some 300 Unionist peers stayed away rather than vote against their leadership or join the die-hards. The Royal Assent followed on 18 August. Thereafter the Lords might delay ordinary legislation for two sessions, but not destroy it if the Commons persisted; money bills they could delay for a month only.

Peerage inflation and the permanent majority

The size of the House made packing difficult: in 1831 the creation of forty peerages might have sufficed against Tory opposition to the Reform Bill; by 1911 some four hundred were said to be needed for a Liberal working majority. Every Prime Minister from Melbourne onward added with generous hand—Melbourne forty-four (1835–41); Disraeli twenty-three across his ministries besides his own earldom; Gladstone fifty in four premierships; Campbell-Bannerman twenty in less than two years (1905–7); Asquith thirteen in his first year. Yet only a small minority of Liberal peers retained their allegiance: many whose titles were not more than six years old already voted with the Conservatives. Social popularity of titles endured even among those who denounced hereditary legislation.

Effect on Home Rule and the constitution

The Parliament Act permanently altered the British constitutional-monarchy. No Act of Parliament had been required to abolish the veto of the Crown on statutes; democratic public opinion had not proved strong enough to end the Lords’ veto on Liberal Commons bills, and therefore statute was required. Conservatives after 1911 preferred to reform the composition of the Upper House and make it a stronger and more representative assembly; Liberals preferred that its power of veto stay curtailed. The Act cleared the path for Home Rule, Welsh Church disestablishment, and other Liberal measures—including National Insurance—that the unreformed Lords would have blocked. The absolute veto that had killed the 1893 bill became a suspensory veto of two sessions. That change—itself won with Irish votes in the majorities of 124–126—made a Home Rule statute possible over the head of the Upper House and bound the Liberals, dependent on the Irish Party, to deliver it. The Third Home Rule Bill, introduced by Asquith on 11 April 1912, passed the Commons in each of the sessions 1912, 1913, and 1914 under the Parliament Act provisions; the real drama then unfolded outside Parliament, as Ulster Unionist resistance, the Covenant, the Ulster Volunteer Force, and mutual arming drove the irish-home-rule-crisis toward civil conflict until European war suspended the unfinished settlement. Finance, once the exclusive preserve of the Commons, had been the weapon that broke the hereditary veto—and the instrument by which social reform and free-trade finance were secured against a second chamber that had never accepted either.

Belloc and Cecil Chesterton, in The Party System, refused to read that struggle as a genuine feud between Houses. The two Front Benches were “not two but one”; their essential aim was maintenance of the Party System. Agreement between them was “the normal method of governing the country,” and the 1910 Constitutional Conference was “only one of the hundred conferences” of leaders, Whips, and official paymasters. The Conference, they wrote, “did not fail. It did exactly what it was intended to do. It saved for a moment the life of the moribund Party System.” They predicted that after “ridiculous sham demagogy” from the Treasury Bench and “sham indignation” from relatives across the table, Lords’ powers would be altered so that the Upper House could not stop measures concerted between the two Front Benches, yet could stop end-of-Parliament “swing of the pendulum” bills: the Second Chamber turned into “a machine subservient to the bi-party system.” In the Commons the same group allotted the time-table: five-sixths or more of time at the Government’s disposal under Standing Orders; Opposition protest “merely ceremonial”; no opposed private member’s Bill becoming law without ministerial facilities. The reconstituted Lords, they added, would drop the country squires “who are in one sense really representative of England” and keep purchasers of peerages, professional politicians, and bishops—new elements “nominees of the machine, or, as the pretty phrase goes, ‘of the Crown.’”
