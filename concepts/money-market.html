The Money Market

The money market—named by Walter Bagehot after the street where it lived, Lombard Street—was the concrete machinery of short-term credit, bank reserves, and the Bank of England’s central position in Victorian and Edwardian London. It was not an abstract force but a set of institutions: the Bank of England, joint-stock and private banks, bill brokers, the separation of the Bank’s Issue and Banking departments under Peel’s Act of 1844, and the national habit of concentrating ultimate cash in Threadneedle Street. “Money will not manage itself,” Bagehot wrote.

Development

Bagehot opened Lombard Street (1873) to dispel the notion that the Money Market could be spoken of only in impalpable terms. It was as real as anything else, and could be described in plain words. He deliberately set aside the long controversy over Peel’s Act of 1844—the “cast iron” separation of the Issue Department from the Banking Department—except for its experienced effects. Half the supposed difficulty of the subject, he held, had arisen from abstract disputes about that statute. A quarter-century of material progress and almost marvellous banking development had intervened since 1844; he therefore sought to isolate the “Post-Peel” agencies rather than re-argue the note issue.

The briefest and truest description of Lombard Street was that it was by far the greatest combination of economical power and economical delicacy the world had seen. Money was economical power; England was the greatest moneyed country, with more immediately disposable and ready cash than any other. Known bank deposits published at the turn of 1872–73 illustrated the scale: London about £120,000,000; Paris £13,000,000; New York £40,000,000; the German Empire £8,000,000—and London’s unpublished private deposits were greater still. Concentration of money in banks, not mere national wealth, made the English market so rich: a million in a single banker’s hands was a great power; the same sum scattered through a nation was no power at all. Civilised governments could borrow in Lombard Street at a price; English bankers advanced on foreign stocks “with a margin.” Domestically, trade was carried on upon borrowed capital to an extent few foreigners imagined. A merchant with £10,000 of his own who borrowed £40,000 by discounts could undersell the rich man trading only on his own capital. Modern English business thus placed a steady bounty on trading with borrowed capital; the rough, “democratic” structure of commerce—new men created by bank discounts—kept English trade awake.

Lombard Street was the great go-between between quiet saving districts and active employing districts. Savings from agricultural counties such as Somersetshire and Hampshire lodged in local banks, travelled to London bankers or bill brokers, and were employed in discounting the bills of Yorkshire and Lancashire. English capital ran as surely toward profitable trades as water finds its level: when the iron trade slackened, iron bills thinned; when corn became profitable, corn bills multiplied and were discounted.

The objects grouped about Lombard Street were the Bank of England, the private banks, the joint-stock banks, and the bill brokers. In continental language the Street was an organisation of credit; the cardinal question was soundness—whether the promises to pay would be kept. For banking purposes the practical test was legal tender on demand: gold and silver coin (silver for small amounts only) and Bank of England notes. The attainable note issue was limited by the Act of 1844, which divided the Bank into two halves. The Issue Department issued notes only: by the late-1860s specimen return Bagehot printed (week ending 29 December 1869), notes issued stood at £33,288,640, of which £15,000,000 rested on government debt and other securities and £18,288,640 on gold coin and bullion. The Banking Department conducted ordinary banking with proprietors’ capital, the “rest,” public and private deposits, and a reserve of notes and coin—on that day liabilities of about £27,235,000 against a cash reserve of £11,297,000.

A crucial distinction ran through the system. Cash in the till for daily business was stock-in-trade, not the true reserve. The safety-fund was the special reserve held against extraordinary and unfrequent demands. Broadly, no bank held any considerable sum in hard cash or legal tender above daily needs except the Banking Department of the Bank of England. All London banks kept their principal reserve on deposit there. Bill brokers did much the same: they lent most of their money and deposited the remnant with the Bank or a London banker. Country bankers retained only the minimum cash for local business and sent spare money to London; Scotch and Irish practice was similar. You always came back to the Bank of England at last. The banking reserve of the Banking Department—some £10,000,000 on an average of years when Bagehot wrote, formerly much less—was therefore all that was held against the liabilities of Lombard Street, of all London, and of England, Ireland, and Scotland.

That single-reserve system was both strength and danger. In quiet times it economised capital; in crisis a run on the system became a run on one institution. Three times Peel’s Act had been suspended because the Banking Department was empty: before the breach the reserve had fallen to about £1,994,000 in 1847, £1,462,000 in 1857, and £3,000,000 in 1866. The danger was not merely artificial. In 1825, when only coin was legal tender and the Bank was undivided, the reserve had been reduced to £1,027,000 and the Bank was within an ace of stopping payment. Since the Franco-German war London had become to a much larger extent the banker of Europe; with specie payments at the Bank of France suspended, international cash payments centred more and more on Threadneedle Street. Foreign deposits were delicate: after the panic of 1866, and especially after foreigners confounded suspension of Peel’s Act with suspension of cash payments, large foreign money had been withdrawn. On the wisdom of the directors of that one joint-stock company, Bagehot insisted, it depended whether England should be solvent or insolvent.

Yet the Bank had never by corporate act distinctly acknowledged the public duty of keeping the ultimate reserve. Some directors denied it. Mr. Hankey wrote that the Banking Department did not differ, as far as he was aware, from any well-conducted London bank—though published figures showed it holding 30 to 50 per cent of liabilities in notes and coin while other banks kept only a bare till minimum. No resolution of Parliament, no report of a committee, no remembered speech of a responsible statesman had enforced the duty; authorities from Sir Robert Peel to Mr. Lowe had often taught that the Banking Department was only a joint-stock bank looking to the proprietors’ dividend. Two pressures pulled the Court opposite ways: national and City opinion, taught by panics, demanded a large reserve; shareholders pressed to diminish idle cash. Directors were rich City merchants, not trained bankers; three times since 1844 the Banking Department had needed assistance, yet popular faith held that the Bank could not fail and that the Government stood close behind it.

The liability structure of Lombard Street had grown with startling speed. In 1844 the liabilities of the four great London joint-stock banks had been about £10,637,000; by Bagehot’s day they exceeded £60,000,000. The collapse of Overend, Gurney, and Co.—a house that ten years earlier had stood next the Bank of England in the City—showed that long-established credit was no guarantee. When Overends failed in 1866, the Bank did not flinch: it made advances that would hardly be credited, advanced half its reserves before any Treasury authority to exceed the statutory note issue could arrive, and refused no legitimate application backed by adequate security—an admission that the Bank kept the sole real banking reserve of the country.

Two kinds of drain required opposite remedies, often at once. An unfavourable foreign exchange emptied the Bank till; the holders of the reserve ought at the very beginning of such a drain to raise the rate of interest, so as to stop further diminution and to replenish the reserve by imports of bullion. A rise in the discount rate acted at once on trade: prices fell, imports diminished, exports increased, and bullion was more likely to return. Between resumption of cash payments in 1819 and 1857 the Bank’s management of foreign drains had been, in Bagehot’s judgment, a miserable history; the panic of 1857 first taught the directors sound principles, though policy remained defective. A domestic drain—internal discredit—was different. The instinct was to hoard the reserve; the right course was to lend freely. Discredit meant an opinion that you had no money; to dissipate it you must employ money for the public benefit so that the public may know you have it. The time for accumulation was before the panic. Holders of the cash reserve must advance most freely for the liabilities of others—merchants, minor bankers, “this man and that man”—whenever the security was good. One failure made many; the way to stop derivative failures was to arrest the primary failure. In 1825 Mr. Harman for the Bank had described the classical treatment: the Bank lent by every possible means and in modes never adopted before—took stock on security, purchased and advanced on Exchequer bills, discounted outright, advanced on bills of exchange to an immense amount, and was “not on some occasions over-nice.” After a day or two the City was calm. When foreign drain and internal panic coincided, the rule was clear: raise the rate as high as necessary for the foreign drain; at that raised rate lend freely. Very large loans at very high rates were the best remedy for the worst malady of the money market.

Historically the monarchical form of Lombard Street was an accident of growth, not deliberate design. The Bank of England was founded in 1694 as a Whig finance company to lend to a government desperate for money; its charter privileges made it the only joint-stock bank permitted in England until 1826, and within the London radius until 1833. Bill brokers evolved from agents into principals who borrowed from banks at call and re-lent by discounting commercial bills. Joint-stock and private banks found it convenient to keep balances at the Bank; the practice ossified—“not so much a matter of law or of deliberate arrangement as the result of a growth which no one planned and no one could now reverse.”

The Chancellor of the Exchequer stood in a special relation to the market. In theory banking was a trade and government should let the Money Market take care of itself; but a modern government was the richest corporation in the country. If it deposited its immense income at a bank, it became interested in that bank’s welfare: it could not pay the interest on the National Debt, salaries, or miscellaneous expenses if the bank failed when the money was wanted. Public deposits—Exchequer, Savings Banks, Commissioners of the National Debt, dividend accounts—fluctuated with revenue: taxes coming in reduced the banking reserve; government payments returned money to the market. The published “Public Deposits” mixed the home Budget account with others, including the Secretary for India in Council and Chancery and Savings Bank balances. In peace the government account was calculable; in war it became among the most uncertain of all accounts.

Bagehot’s categories—reserve, legal tender, the Bank as holder of last resort, the money market as a concrete street of firms—remained those in which Englishmen understood financial crisis down to 1914. When the European crisis of late July 1914 broke upon London, the machinery he had described was tested at once. On 31 July the London Stock Exchange was closed and the Bank rate raised to 8 per cent; on 1 August the rate advanced to 10 per cent, the National Penny Bank suspended payment, and a partial moratorium was decreed; a moratorium of a month was established for certain bills of exchange. The fiscal state that free trade and later social expenditure had shaped still rested on the credit of Lombard Street: the People’s Budget struggle of 1909–11 had shown how fiercely the financing of social-reform and naval power could divide the constitution, yet the short-term market remained the daily barometer of confidence. On the outbreak of war the Commons cheered a Vote of Credit of £100,000,000 for naval and military operations, food supplies, trade, distress, and war expenses.

Exchequer cash at the Banks

Bagehot’s “public deposits” were the market’s view of the same balances that financial-administration treated as Exchequer cash. The 1917 Willoughby report, written from interviews and papers collected in London in July–August 1914, described the public side that touched Threadneedle Street without confusing it with the banking reserve. The Consolidated Fund stood to the credit of the Exchequer—the public treasury—at the Bank of England and the Bank of Ireland; Scotch banks received and remitted in rotation. Since 1834 revenues had been paid into those two banks to the Exchequer account. The banks were “mere bankers of the Government”: they received and paid to officers charged with settling obligations, and they took the place of the old Teller. They did not hold the market’s last legal-tender reserve in Bagehot’s sense; they kept the Crown’s cash chest.

Legislative control required a guardian of issues. The Exchequer and Audit Departments Act 1866 consolidated that guardianship with audit in one officer: Comptroller General of the Receipt and Issue of Her Majesty’s Exchequer and Auditor General of Public Accounts—the Comptroller and Auditor General. Funds at the two banks were subject to Parliament. The Treasury first obtained “credits” on the Exchequer. For Consolidated Fund services two Lords of the Treasury requisitioned the C&AG, who if satisfied granted credit by warrant. For Supply a royal order had to precede the requisition, because supply was granted to the Sovereign, who was under no legal duty to use it until he signified that wish. Credit did not move cash. Issues went only to “principal accountants”: the Comptroller of the National Debt and the chief cashiers of the two banks (debt service); the accountants-general of the revenue departments; and the Paymaster-General, general paymaster for military and civil services. Principal accountants requisitioned the Treasury, which ordered the banks to transfer from credits to their accounts. The C&AG did not intervene at that second stage; he had already granted the credit.

The Paymaster-General was banker, not accounting officer. He paid orders drawn by departmental accounting officers, satisfied as to form and supporting documents. He kept a separate book account for each civil vote and consolidated Army and Navy accounts, but one cash balance, and could pay any in-form order so long as the general balance sufficed. Pay offices had been consolidated between 1830 and 1856 to reduce idle balances; the 1856–57 Committee on Public Monies endorsed the single-balance system. A Treasury minute of 21 February 1885 (the Chancellor answering W. H. Smith) listed his sources: extra receipts as appropriations in aid; the Treasury Chest Fund (expenditure abroad); the Civil Contingencies Fund; departmental deposit funds; and daily Consolidated Fund issues. He exhausted outside cash before drawing the Fund, so daily Exchequer-issue returns did not equal expenditure except at prescribed adjustments—monthly, fairly accurate quarterly, and as close as possible at 31 March to the C&AG’s audited expenditure nine or ten months later.

Parliament therefore received two sets of figures: Exchequer receipts and issues, and audited receipts and expenditure. The Finance Accounts remained a compact cash statement of Exchequer income and issues and of year-end resources and liabilities at the two banks. For 1913–14 they showed tax and non-tax income of about £198.2 million against Consolidated Fund and Supply issues of about £197.5 million; Part II opened with balances at the Bank of England and the Bank of Ireland. Those balances were public cash awaiting authorised issue, not the Banking Department’s reserve against Lombard Street’s liabilities. Bagehot’s Chancellor mattered because government payments and tax inflows moved the market reserve; Willoughby’s Comptroller, credits, and Paymaster-General mattered because no shilling left the Exchequer accounts at the two banks until statute, royal order, and warrant had opened the chest.
