Command of the Sea

Command of the sea was the strategic doctrine on which British defence and empire rested: the capacity, in war, to destroy or securely blockade an enemy fleet so that the national frontier, for the purpose of that war and that purpose only, was carried to the enemy’s coast. It was not a claim of peacetime sovereignty over the high seas beyond the gunshot of the shore—such claims England had once made, and other states had at times acquiesced in for the suppression of piracy—but a military condition that made invasion of Britain impossible and invasion of the enemy possible, and that kept open the oceanic routes of trade and empire. Halford Mackinder put the office of sea power in a single image: the navy was Britain’s shield and the army her spear; the final office of command was freedom to choose the point of land attack.

Development

Mackinder’s definition and the nineteenth-century margin

In Britain and the British Seas Mackinder stated the doctrine in geographical terms. A Power has command of the sea against another with which it is at war when it has destroyed the enemy’s fleet or securely blockaded it. Had Britain obtained that command in a war with France, the British frontier would be carried to the French coast and the Channel added to the sphere within which the commanding Power prepared attack; France would be liable to invasion, England safe from it. Had France obtained command, the conditions would reverse. Neutral rights would still limit even complete command against the enemy; but the strategic contrast was absolute.

Viewed from that standpoint, the defence of Britain resolved into three problems: first, retention of the command of the sea—or rather of the power of taking that command when occasion demanded it; second, the defence of Great Britain should command be temporarily lost; third, the separate defence of Ireland in the same contingency, for under such a condition the prompt reinforcement of the army in Ireland would not be practicable. Practically, owing to the limited fuel radius of modern fleets, the regions most threatened were those about the continental angle and the Channel entries; the position of the Metropolis magnified that danger. The chief bases—Chatham, Sheerness, Portsmouth, Devonport—lay within Metropolitan England, opposite the Rhine mouths, Cherbourg, and Brest; harbours of refuge stood at Portland, Dover, and Harwich; Pembroke and Queenstown covered the ocean roads into the St. George’s and Bristol Channels. Mackinder left the chapter as written in 1902, noting only that the growth of the German Fleet and the increased coal-storage of larger battleships opened Britain to invasion, should she lose command, at all points and not merely on either hand of Dover—so that none but a great and efficient army would suffice against a dominant sea-power basing itself on a peninsula such as Cornwall or Fife.

For most of the nineteenth century the Royal Navy’s margin over any likely combination of rivals made the doctrine almost a habit. The Naval Defence Act of 1889 enshrined the two-power standard: the British fleet should equal the combined strength of the next two navies. Against France and Russia that standard rested on a fleet of some fifty pre-dreadnought battleships. Langer’s account of the “new navalism” placed British maritime supremacy as the condition that made England and the Empire almost unassailable while other powers, under Mahan’s teaching on high-seas battle-fleets, turned to naval programmes of their own. Imperial expansion and the scramble for markets sharpened the stake; command of the sea kept the British world-system open.

Tirpitz, risk theory, and the Navy Laws

The German challenge after 1897 turned habit into race. Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, appointed Secretary of State for the Navy in June 1897, returned from the East Asian cruiser squadron convinced that Germany’s economic expansion, commercial rivalry with Britain, and lack of overseas bases made a cruiser war of little use: the only decisive factor would be a battle-fleet in the North Sea. His “risk theory” (Risiko-Gedanke) held that Germany need not equal Britain ship for ship; she needed a fleet powerful enough that even a British victory would cost Britain so heavily as to risk her global position—“a maritime fighting force of such strength that it could not be overcome without much ado even by one of the larger naval forces.” Germany’s position athwart British trade routes would magnify the threat, forcing Britain to recall squadrons from the Mediterranean and elsewhere; rather than pay that price, Tirpitz believed, Britain would accommodate German ambitions.

Tirpitz organised a publicity bureau at the Ministry of Marine, arranged the translation of Mahan’s The Influence of Sea Power upon History, and collaborated with the Pan-German League and the newly formed Navy League (Flottenverein). The First Navy Law, passed by the Reichstag on 28 March 1898, authorised eleven battleships, five first-class cruisers, and seventeen smaller cruisers by 1905—presented as a minimum to lift the German fleet above a quantité négligeable, but revolutionary in legislating construction years ahead of annual budgets. The Second Navy Law of 14 June 1900, riding the nationalist surge of the South African War, doubled authorised strength to thirty-eight battleships, twenty large cruisers, and thirty-eight smaller cruisers—a permanent “risk fleet” that would make attack by even the strongest naval power “a risky undertaking for the aggressor.” Novellen in 1906, 1908, and 1912 accelerated size, pace, or capital-ship numbers. By 1908 the German Navy League claimed 1,018,590 members and a monthly journal, Die Flotte, of 375,000 circulation; nearly seven hundred lectures and exhibitions in seventy-eight towns drove public agitation. Tirpitz later told the Reichstag, as the Annual Register recorded, that the Navy Laws had supplied the only possible method of creating, with the funds at their disposal, the sea power they had created.

Fisher, Dreadnought, and redistribution

Britain answered under Sir John Fisher, First Sea Lord from October 1904. He scrapped scores of obsolescent vessels that “had no fighting value,” introduced a nucleus-crew system that kept reserve ships manned at about forty per cent of complement ready for instant mobilisation, and concentrated British naval power in home waters, shifting the centre of gravity from the Mediterranean toward the North Sea. By 1907 the preponderant might of the British Navy lay in and around the North Sea—the Home Fleet with sixteen fully commissioned battleships and eight in reserve, the Atlantic Fleet relocated toward Dover.

Fisher’s most dramatic stroke was HMS Dreadnought—the first all-big-gun battleship—laid down in October 1905 and commissioned in December 1906 after little more than a year. Armed with ten 12-inch guns against the four heavy guns of a typical pre-dreadnought, turbine-powered and capable of 21 knots, she rendered every existing battleship obsolete at a stroke. The gamble nullified Britain’s overwhelming pre-dreadnought superiority, but Fisher calculated that British industrial capacity would outbuild any rival in the new class. For a few years he was proved right: in 1906–07 Britain’s naval superiority still far exceeded the requirements of the two-power standard. Germany’s Novelle of 1906 was modest; that of 1908, after the second Moroccan crisis and the Daily Telegraph affair, accelerated replacement and authorised the first German dreadnoughts—Nassau, Westfalen, Rheinland, and the large cruiser Blücher among them. The Navy League pressed for a seventeenth battleship and for ships equal in power to those of other nations.

The 1909 scare, conversations, and the approach to war

The Anglo-German naval race entered its most acute phase in 1908–09. Early in 1908 Berlin revamped goals toward a grand total of fifty-eight battleships instead of the thirty-eight of 1900; Admiralty intelligence then reported secret acceleration of dreadnought construction. Estimates predicted Germany with seventeen—or even twenty-one—dreadnoughts by 1912 against Britain’s eighteen; the two-power standard “would be swept away, and possibly Britain with it.” Fisher and the Board demanded six new dreadnoughts for 1909–10 instead of the usual four. The radical wing of the Liberal government—David Lloyd George as Chancellor and Winston Churchill as President of the Board of Trade—resisted, arguing that the intelligence was exaggerated and the cost unaffordable. For months the government was racked by a major crisis; Fisher leaked to the Conservative press; the opposition and public took up the slogan “We want eight and we won’t wait!” Asquith’s government finally capitulated. As Churchill later recorded in The World Crisis: the Admiralty had demanded six; the economists offered four; “we finally compromised on eight”—four laid down at once, four “contingent” later in the year. The 1909 estimates were the largest in British history to that point; the margin was reaffirmed in practice if not in name.

Naval conversations with France, the Haldane mission to Germany, and intermittent “naval holiday” proposals—Churchill’s later advocacy among them—failed to arrest the competition. By 1914 the High Seas Fleet forced concentration of British strength in the North Sea; the doctrine of command had become a two-power struggle with one principal rival. When the July crisis broke, the Fleet that had been concentrated for a test mobilisation was held at war stations. On Churchill’s instruction Prince Louis of Battenberg told the ships not to disperse; a public communiqué under the heading “British Naval Measures” announced that the First Fleet at Portland would not disperse for manœuvre leave. On 30 July the Fleet passed the Dover Straits by night to its war stations. At eleven on the night of 4 August—midnight German time—the war telegram “Commence hostilities against Germany” flashed to ships and establishments under the White Ensign worldwide. The outbreak of the Great War found British command of the sea already disposed for the North Sea struggle that the race had made inevitable.

Dogger Bank, Jutland, blockade, and armistice

In war the doctrine was tested by fleet action and by distant blockade. On 24 January 1915, about the Dogger Bank, Vice-Admiral Sir David Beatty’s battle-cruiser force brought Rear-Admiral Hipper’s German battle cruisers to action—the first engagement in which Dreadnought-type capital ships fought one another with heavy guns at long range. The armoured cruiser Blücher was sunk; the German battle cruisers, damaged and on fire, escaped; Beatty’s flagship Lion was crippled. Signalling confusion after Lion was disabled turned the squadron onto Blücher and let Hipper’s heavy ships get clear—an incomplete victory that nonetheless, as Corbett judged, demonstrated the efficacy of the new Grand Fleet distribution after the Yorkshire coast raid. Churchill told the House that Dogger Bank had established the superiority of British guns and shooting.

On 31 May and the night following, off Jutland, the Grand Fleet under Admiral Sir John Jellicoe and the High Seas Fleet under Vice-Admiral Scheer met in the greatest naval battle since Trafalgar. Beatty and Hipper opened the action; the main fleets closed in evening mist; Scheer, finding himself face to face with the whole Grand Fleet, extricated himself by practised battle-turns under destroyer smoke and forced a passage home. No second Trafalgar followed. Yet when Sir Julian Corbett summed the purely strategical result, Scheer had failed to cut off and overwhelm part of the British advanced forces, and Jellicoe had increased the Grand Fleet’s margin of superiority as a combative force, a defence against invasion, and an instrument of blockade. British control of the North Sea remained unbroken. Command need not mean the annihilation of every enemy ship; it meant that the enemy could not use the sea for invasion or for breaking the economic cordon, while Britain could move armies and sustain the island and the empire. The Battle of Jutland left the strategic condition of command intact.

Through 1917–18 the same condition underwrote the land war. Newbolt’s naval history insisted that the final collapse of Central Europe was mainly the fruit of command of the sea and economic strangulation, of which the Allied counter-offensive was the last expression. When the Armistice of 11 November 1918 was signed, its naval conditions consummated that grip: surrender of all submarines, fifty destroyers, eight light cruisers, six battle-cruisers, and ten battleships; the High Seas Fleet transferred into Allied custody—surface ships to the Forth and then Scapa Flow, submarines off Harwich. The terms were tantamount to complete surrender at sea as on land. The cost of the long doctrine had been fiscal and diplomatic: dreadnought estimates, German enmity, subordination of other stations to the North Sea, and the political crises of 1909 and after. Yet for a state whose food, raw materials, and imperial communications all moved by water, no alternative strategy presented itself. From Mackinder’s shield and spear through Tirpitz’s risk fleet, Fisher’s redistribution, the race to 1914, and the tests of Dogger Bank and Jutland to the Armistice naval clauses, command of the sea remained the condition of British survival as a world power.
