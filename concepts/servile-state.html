The Servile State

The Servile State is the arrangement of society in which so considerable a number of families and individuals are constrained by positive law to labour for the advantage of other families and individuals as to stamp the whole community with the mark of such labour. It is the stable condition toward which industrial capitalism, with the means of production owned by a few and the mass proletarian, is tending: not Collectivism, and not the restoration of widely distributed property, but compulsory labour of non-owners for owners, in exchange for sufficiency and security. The epigraph of the thesis is exact: if the institution of property is not restored, the institution of slavery cannot be escaped; there is no third course.

Development

Definitions

Man lives by transforming his environment. That conscious transformation is the production of wealth; without wealth he cannot exist; to control its production is to control life. Labour is human energy applied to nature; land is material and natural force; capital is wealth reserved for future production. The means of production are land and capital combined. A man master only of his labour, without useful control of land or capital, is proletarian; a considerable class of such men is a proletariat. Property is the legal vesting of control of land and of wealth made from land in a person or corporation, to use or to withhold. Private property is distinguished because the owner may exercise control to his own advantage, not as a trustee for society.

A society in which political officers hold the means of production in trust is Collectivist (Socialist in that special sense). A society in which private ownership is confined to a number of free citizens not large enough to determine the social mass, the rest proletarian, is Capitalist. Its two marks are political freedom of the citizen and restriction of ownership to a section markedly less than the whole. Labour is servile when it is undertaken not in fulfilment of contract but under the compulsion of positive law, attaching to the status of the labourer and performed for the benefit of others under no such compulsion. The free man can refuse his labour and use that refusal as a bargain; the slave has no such instrument. Servile does not mean oppressive: a man guaranteed against insecurity and insufficiency, with subsistence in old age, might be better off than a free man lacking those things. The test is withdrawal of free choice to labour or not, here or there, and compulsion by law to labour for others who do not fall under the same compulsion. Ownership of the slave by a particular master is not essential. A week’s contract of service is not slavery; a life-contract at bare subsistence is.

Pagan origin and Christian dissolution

European civilisation sprang from a purely servile conception of production. Two thousand years ago and upward, slavery was the fundamental institution of Mediterranean city-states, Celtic tribes, and German hordes—everywhere present, nowhere disputed. The Greek had a Greek slave, the Latin a Latin slave; human equality was recognised between master and slave; poverty made the slave, and the slave bred the slave. There were no martyrs for freedom against slavery. Slavery was of the stuff of Europe until the Faith.

The Church included no attack upon the institution as immoral; emancipation was a good work, as among pagans. Over close upon a thousand years the villa transformed the slave. Custom limited dues; surplus was left as an implied bargain; sale of men became exceptional. By the ninth century the estate divided into domain, land in villenage, and common waste with remembered rights. The servus became serf, then nearly a peasant: bound in theory, in practice inheriting a holding, buying, selling, building to his own profit. Town industry organised in guilds of private owners of capital, designed to check competition, safeguard the division of property, and prevent a proletariat. The Middle Ages conceived the Distributive State: the normal family should own. Collectivism did not replace slavery; common land was guarded by personal proprietors.

English failure

That consummation was destroyed, more particularly in Britain, not by machinery but by the deliberate action of men. The Industrial System proceeded from Capitalism; it was not machinery that lost freedom but the loss of a free mind. At the close of the Middle Ages England was economically free. Monastic lands—well over a fifth of agricultural communities, the Church lord of something over a quarter—were seized intending to vest them in the Crown and passed instead to an already wealthy section. Had the Crown kept them, the State might have used economic power against the wealthier classes. Large owners added a new fifth to their third and became at a blow owners of half the land. Clerical landlords had been customary; the new owners were strictly competitive. Howards, Cavendishes, Cecils, Russells, and fifty other new families rose upon the ruins of religion. By 1630–40 an oligarchy of large owners overshadowed a dwindled monarchy. By 1700 more than half the English were dispossessed: England had already become capitalist. That, and not the later so-called Industrial Revolution, accounts for the social condition of the present. Newcomen, Watt, Hargreaves, Darby, and Kay’s shuttle came upon a people already largely without economic freedom; capitalisation had to come from the class that monopolised accumulations. Common rights were destroyed of set purpose. There is not an example of so-called social-reform which is not directed to the further entrenchment of a society in which a few shall own and the majority live at a wage under them.

Instability of capitalism

Capitalism is a transitory phase between two stable states. As it grows perfect it grows unstable. First, law still presupposes free citizens—property as normal, contract as free, punishment as the sanction—while property as experience is unknown to nineteen out of twenty. The vast bulk of free contracts are leonine: one party may take or leave, the other faces starvation. Most men fear the loss of employment more than legal punishment; the true master of the Englishman is the Capitalist. Second, capitalism destroys security. Labour of the non-owner fetches nothing in sickness or despair. A perfect capitalist state cannot exist: left completely free there would be so heavy a mortality from starvation as would dry up labour. It must keep the masses alive by non-capitalist methods—Elizabeth’s Poor Law, the Poor Law of 1834, and a hundred others. Competitive anarchy makes employment precarious; understandings and secret political forces restrict competition. England is no longer possessed of the absolute political freedom true capitalism demands.

Three solutions; why Collectivism slides

There are but three social arrangements that can replace capitalism: slavery, socialism, and property. To solve capitalism one must get rid of restricted ownership, or of freedom, or of both. The only alternative to freedom is legal compulsion to work. Leaving freedom untouched, property must go into the hands of many (the Distributive State) or of none (Collectivism: vesting as a trust in political officers). Direct re-establishment of slavery would be a true solution and is the probable goal, but surviving Christian tradition forbids open advocacy.

The capitalist state breeds a Collectivist theory which in action produces the Servile State. Restoration of property works against the grain: confiscation and redistribution raise insoluble injustices; slow building of small savings must reverse the economy of interest; the proletarian mass thinks in terms of employment, not property. Collectivism works with the grain. Buying out a railway or municipalising gas does not disturb any fundamental thing; creating many small shareholders would leave owners free to sell again to a few. Collectivism promises employment, pensions, promotion, security vastly increased because the State guarantees it. To owners it is an enemy with whom they can treat: a business proposition. The State’s greater credit can seemingly “buy out”—an illusion: it is impossible by any jugglery to buy out the universality of the means of production without confiscation. If you desire to confiscate, you must confiscate. Actual “Socialist” measures fund new official salaries or hand proceeds to another set of capitalists: death duties become palings under an Inebriates Bill, or pass to Prudential shareholders under the Insurance Act.

Reformers, the mass, and the beginning of compulsion

Three interests make for the change: the Socialist (the confiscator who cannot confiscate, and the lover of tables and a vast bureaucracy who fathers servile regulation without trenching on privilege); the Practical Man, who cannot define first principles and gloats on every new detail in the building up of the Servile State; and the proletarian mass—about nineteen-twentieths in England, excluding Ireland, where reaction against capitalism is already successful. The generation of the Education Acts has grown up hopelessly proletarian. Offered a life-contract of service at the usual full wage, very few would refuse. That is the key. What the mass most fears is not the Courts but the sack. After the long terrors of a freedom unaccompanied by property, they see, at the expense of a mere legal freedom, the prospect of having enough and not losing it. The Servile State is, in industrial England, no longer a menace but something in actual existence; the corner-stone is already laid.

Factory laws that bind the citizen as citizen are not an origin. The first servile legislation on the statute book is the present form of Employers’ Liability: it admits status—the worker as inferior claiming protection from a superior. Later measures fall into three categories: relief of proletarian insecurity through the employing class or the proletariat under compulsion; compelling the employer to give not less than a certain minimum; compelling a man lacking the means of production to labour though he has made no contract.

The Insurance Act follows in every particular the lines of a Servile State. Its criterion is employment: compulsion if exchanging services for goods below a certain amount, or being a vulgar fellow working with his hands. It excludes the educated and therefore powerful classes and those earning enough to be economically free. The duty of seeing the law obeyed is put not upon the proletariat but upon the Capitalist class. The future historian will fix upon that point as the cardinal landmark: two categories, the lower man compelled to registration and a tax, the upper man the instrument in enforcing that registration and collecting that tax. Registration of the proletariat—name, address, movement, illness, supposed malingering, drink, domestic habits, employment—is very nearly complete, imposed by the wealthier classes who are the actual gatherers of the poll tax. Labour exchanges will register every proletarian as a worker, his tendencies to rebellion against capital, whether and when he has refused service. Insurance sums are not in the hands of a society he can control: refuse work at twenty-five shillings and he shall not have a right to the money he has been compelled to put aside. No man, once so registered and known, can escape.

Minimum wage as a statutory sum had not entered the laws as of September 1912, but mining had legal sanction for a hypothetical minimum arrived at by local boards—wages boards in one great industry, shortly in more. Parliamentary debate on the miners did not turn on their owning the mines or the State owning them, but on the minimum wage at a satisfactory level. Both parties were agreed upon that fundamental change. A sliding scale according to prices and profits is easy and natural: capital gets guarantee against disturbance; labour sufficiency and security. The proletarian loses the power to refuse his labour and thus to aim at putting himself in possession of the means of production. Two and a half millions guaranteed against unemployment at a weekly sum related to estimated earnings is a short step to statutory sums in employment. A minimum wage involves compulsory labour: courts must constrain either party to a ratified bargain; habit over years makes it compulsory labour. The man who cannot produce the minimum must be made to work; the labour colony—a prison so called because euphemism is necessary to every transition—will crown the edifice. Compulsory arbitration is the obvious bludgeon, twice brought forward as a Bill; no civilised European state has yet succumbed.

Municipalisation and nationalisation proceed by loan, not confiscation. Interest is continued surplus values; sinking fund the instalments. Capitalism is a winner and not a loser by this form of sham Socialism. Translation of the means of production from private owners to public officials is nowhere being attempted.

The Old Age Pensions Act belongs to the same body of organised reform that labour-representation pressed and that ran parallel to the great-labour-unrest-1911-1914: security without property, regulation without confiscation. The community left to itself settles upon that servile basis which was its foundation before the Christian faith, from which that faith slowly weaned it, and to which in the decay of that faith it naturally returns. Mere capitalist anarchy cannot endure. Theoretical Collectivism, now so plainly failing, will not inform a living society. Societies that broke with Christian continuity in the sixteenth century—roughly North Germany and Great Britain—tend to the re-establishment of a servile status. Ireland has decided for a free peasantry; France has contemptuously rejected registration and insurance of artisans as a separate category. The tide making for the Servile State in Prussia and in England may be modified, checked, perhaps defeated in war, by those freer societies upon its flank.
