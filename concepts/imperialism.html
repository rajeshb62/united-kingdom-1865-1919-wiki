Imperialism

Imperialism, in the late-Victorian and Edwardian sense used by observers of the “scramble,” was the deliberate extension of European—and especially British—political control, commercial privilege, and strategic position beyond the older settled empire into Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. It was not merely the inheritance of colonies, but a movement: a concentrated outburst of annexation, partition, and protectorate-making that remade the map between roughly 1880 and 1914. Langer treated it as a world-policy phenomenon in which rivalry overseas became, in the last years of the century, “every whit as important, and perhaps more important, than the shifting trends of purely European diplomacy.”

Development

Drivers of the late-Victorian outburst

Britain already ruled the largest overseas empire when the new phase began. India, the self-governing Dominions, and scattered crown colonies formed a system whose defence rested on sea power and the routes to the East. The opening of the Suez Canal and the occupation of Egypt after 1882 bound that system to the Mediterranean and the Nile. With the Franco-Russian grouping complete by 1894, the old European issues fell into something like deadlock; that fact, together with growing economic pressure and intensifying competition for markets, stimulated and facilitated the overseas expansion Langer called the “tremendous, almost epic outburst of European interest in affairs that lay beyond the frontiers of Europe.” Industrial rivals sought markets and raw materials; prestige and ideology supplied language for expansion; and because so much of the globe was already an English preserve, Britain tended to become the object of attack for both continental groupings even as she advanced claims to regions still unappropriated. Maritime supremacy made England and the Empire almost unassailable; navalism elsewhere became bound up with designs hostile to the British world-system.

Contemporaries framed the movement in more than economic terms. Imperial federation, the literature of action, the popular press, evolutionary theories of society, and a democratic suffrage that cheered Jubilees and colonial wars all entered the climate in which Rhodes, Chamberlain, and Liberal Imperialists such as Rosebery spoke of markets, mission, and special fitness for empire. “Little Englander” became the term of abuse for those who put domestic reform or pure free trade above imperial consolidation. Rudyard Kipling gave the age its most resonant verse of imperial duty; the same democracy that filled music-halls with imperial song also, in time, returned free-trade Parliaments and paid for social reform from a free-trade Exchequer.

The economic measure and taproot

J. A. Hobson, writing in 1902 as the South African War closed, treated the same outburst as a measurable policy rather than a destiny. Quibbles about the word, he held, were best resolved by the last thirty years of annexation and asserted sway. Britain had added some 4,754,000 square miles and an estimated 88,000,000 people in a generation; taking Giffen’s empire (with Egypt and the Sudan) at about 13,000,000 square miles and 400–420 million inhabitants, a third of the area and a fourth of the population had been acquired since 1870. The full impetus came only in the mid-eighties: from about 1884 the method of wholesale partition assigned great African tracts, and a compiled list for 1884–1900—New Guinea, Nigeria, Bechuanaland, Upper Burma, British East Africa, Rhodesia, Uganda, Ashantee, Wei-hai-wei, the Sudan, the Transvaal and Orange River Colony among them—ran to some 3,711,957 square miles and 57 million people. The novelty was not British peculiarity but competing empires: France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, Spain, Russia, and, after 1898, the United States. The new lands were tropical or sub-tropical, thinly settled by whites, and held by protectorate, charter, or paramountcy rather than genuine colonisation. Imperialism was nationalism overflowing its banks to absorb reluctant and unassimilable peoples; colonialism in the best sense—migration carrying self-government, as in Canada and Australasia—was a genuine expansion of nationality. The scramble recast European policy and produced a sliding diplomatic vocabulary of hinterland, sphere, suzerainty, protectorate, lease, and concession.

The commercial case failed on the figures. National income was about £1,700,000,000; 1898 trade at a liberal 5 per cent. profit yielded a little over £38,000,000, about one forty-fifth of income. There was no natural necessity for external markets: with everything produced a corresponding power to consume was created. In the three decades of conscious struggle for markets, external trade had not kept pace with internal trade; value of external trade per head had actually shrunk. Trade did not follow the flag: the share with British possessions was virtually the same in 1855–59 and 1895–98, while colonial trade among themselves grew faster than trade with the United Kingdom. The most profitable commerce remained with rival industrial nations; recent tropical acquisitions were poor and precarious markets. Nor was the new empire an outlet for surplus population. Net emigration was modest; the great majority of outward passengers went to the United States; no substantial settlement of Britons was taking place on areas acquired since 1870 except the Transvaal and Orange River Colony. A few soldiers, officials, engineers, missionaries, and overseers held temporary posts. The new empire was even more barren for settlement than for profitable trade.

If the policy was a business blunder for the nation, it was rational for sectional interests that usurped control of national resources. Direct beneficiaries included armament firms drawing a peacetime bill of some £60,000,000, export manufacturers, shipping claiming subsidies, the services, and colonial posts that James Mill had called outdoor relief for the upper classes. By far the most important factor was investment. Identified foreign income nearly doubled from 1884 to 1900; by the century’s end foreign and colonial holdings could not be set below £2,000,000,000. Interest on investments enormously exceeded profits on ordinary external trade and grew far faster: modern foreign policy was primarily a struggle for profitable markets of investment. Britain was becoming a nation living upon tribute from abroad. More dangerous still were the financiers for whom stocks were material for speculation—banking, broking, loan-floating, company-promoting: parasites upon patriotism, carried on the side of Imperialism by public debts and fluctuating values.

Logical Imperialism repudiated Free Trade and rested on Protection. The markets forced were small, unprofitable, and subject to diminishing returns; the open door was an advantage, not a necessity. The economic taproot lay deeper than any particular annexation. Productive power had outstripped consumption; over-saving—rents, monopoly profits, and other unearned or excessive income—flooded production because consuming power was mal-distributed. Imperialism was the endeavour of the great controllers of industry to broaden the channel for surplus wealth by seeking foreign markets and foreign investments they could not use at home. Divert that excess to wages or to the community as taxes, and there would be no need to fight for foreign markets; the home market was capable of indefinite expansion. Imperialist finance used government machinery to secure those private gains: ordinary expenditure rose sharply from the late eighties; roughly three-quarters of revenue went to naval and military charges and military debts. The classes who formed the economic core extracted these sums to improve investments and open new fields, while the people paid, preferably without knowing they paid—hence the bias toward indirect taxation, cheap drink, and “Mafficking.”

Politically the new empire enlarged the unfree proportion of British subjects. Of some 367 million outside the British Isles, not more than ten million had any real self-government; where British government was real it did not carry freedom, and where it carried a measure of freedom it was not real. India and Egypt were in essential features Crown colonies; government of the vast majority rested on the will of imperial officials, not the consent of the governed. The scientific defence—Pearson, Demolins, Kidd, Giddings—rested on “social efficiency” as capacity to beat other races, a natural-history struggle dressed as a mission of civilisation. Moral and sentimental factors furnished protective colour: a genuine wish to spread Christianity and diminish cruelty was utilised by selfish forces; Egypt occupied for military and financial reasons was afterwards said to have been entered for the fellah. Aggressive Imperialism was virtually confined to those weaker and incapable of effective resistance. Intensive tightening of control over “lower races” in Africa and Asia was as important as extensive growth; if interference were ever justified it must be for the world and the inhabitants, internationally sanctioned, and not destructive of self-development. Asia was the great test: a century of able Indian administration had not materially warded off chronic famine, and China threatened partition of concessions rather than trusteeship. Imperial federation of the self-governing colonies collided with the tropical policy: those colonies would not share the cost of ruling unassimilable millions for City investments; a white federation that excluded the coloured majority would not be the Empire, one that included it would not be free. The outcome, if the policy were not destiny, required repudiation of the claim that any subject venturing person or property abroad could call the nation’s force to his aid, and a genuine democracy directing external policy. To term Imperialism a national policy was, on this showing, an impudent falsehood; every tropical enlargement was a distinct enfeeblement of true British nationalism.

The scramble for Africa

The Scramble for Africa was the most visible theatre of the movement. The Berlin Conference of 1884–85, convened by Bismarck after Leopold II’s claims in the Congo and coastal rivalries, did not itself divide the continent. Fourteen nations produced the General Act of Berlin: free trade in the Congo Basin, freedom of navigation on the Congo and Niger, and above all the principle of effective occupation—a power could keep a claim only by administration, military presence, or treaties with local rulers. Once that rule was set, powers raced to plant flags and posts. Bilateral bargains followed: Anglo-German spheres in East Africa (1886), Anglo-French lines in West Africa (1889), and the Heligoland–Zanzibar Treaty of 1890, by which Salisbury ceded Heligoland for a clearer British sphere north of a line from Lake Victoria toward Lake Tanganyika, securing Uganda against Karl Peters’s German treaties and the threat, as The Times put it in April 1890, of Germans on the Nile.

British advance ran along three great axes. In East Africa the Imperial British East Africa Company (chartered 1888 under Sir William MacKinnon), Stanley’s relief of Emin Pasha, and the race for Uganda preceded the protectorate of 1894 and the Uganda Railway (1896–1901). In West Africa the Royal Niger Company under Sir George Goldie secured the lower Niger and Benue against French and German rivals; its charter was revoked in 1899 and the territories became the Protectorates of Northern and Southern Nigeria (unified 1914). Ashanti was annexed after the wars of 1873–74 and 1895–96; the Benin Expedition of 1897 followed the massacre of a British mission. Further south, Harry Johnston, subsidised by Rhodes, planted posts between Lakes Nyasa and Tanganyika; Bechuanaland, made a protectorate in 1885, kept open the “road to the north” between the Transvaal and German South-West Africa. An ultimatum to Portugal in January 1890 broke the “rose-coloured map” that would have linked Angola to Mozambique.

Egypt, the Sudan, and Fashoda

The Egyptian and Sudan campaigns were the strategic hinge of British African imperialism. Dual Control of Egyptian finance by Britain and France, the nationalist cry “Egypt for the Egyptians,” and the rise of Ahmed Arabi produced the Alexandria crisis of June–July 1882. After the French fleet withdrew, Sir Beauchamp Seymour bombarded the forts (11 July); John Bright resigned from Gladstone’s Cabinet; Sir Garnet Wolseley crushed Arabi at Tel-el-Kebir. Though Granville had written that Her Majesty’s Government had “a strong objection to the occupation by themselves of Egypt,” the troops stayed. McCarthy put the irony plainly: English statesmanship had first meant only to secure the Canal and the highway to India—and then occupied alone. Langer judged the occupation the “initial impetus to British imperialism in the last years of the century.” After France and Russia blocked the projected Anglo-Turkish convention of 1887, “the chances of evacuation rapidly diminished.”

From Egypt Britain was drawn into the Sudan. Muhammad Ahmad, the Mahdi, annihilated Hicks’s Egyptian army (1883) and in January 1885 took Khartoum two days before Wolseley’s relief; General Charles Gordon died on the palace steps. The Ministry survived votes of censure only to fall on Childers’s budget in June 1885. For more than a decade the Sudan remained under the Khalifa Abdallahi. Sir Evelyn Baring (Lord Cromer) denied any serious idea of reconquest; yet fear for Egypt’s water supply—Milner in England in Egypt (1892) called regular Nile water a question of life so long as the upper reaches were uncontrolled—drove policy back toward the river. After Italy’s disaster at Adwa (1896), Salisbury authorised an advance from Wadi Halfa toward Dongola, “to plant the foot of Egypt farther up the Nile.” Sir Herbert Kitchener, Sirdar of the Egyptian army, built railway across desert, destroyed Mahmoud at the Atbara, and on 2 September 1898 at Omdurman broke a Dervish host of over 60,000 with Maxim guns and Lee-Metfords—some 11,000 dead against fewer than fifty British and Egyptian killed. Gordon was “avenged”; the Sudan became an Anglo-Egyptian condominium (1899). The same Nile thrust met a French expedition at Fashoda: the crisis of 1898 brought Britain and France to the edge of war before French withdrawal cleared the way for later colonial bargain.

Rhodes, Cape-to-Cairo, and the Boer War

Cecil Rhodes was the most energetic empire-builder of the southern advance—“the great imperialist” ready to take over anything in central Africa between the Zambezi and the White Nile. The Cape-to-Cairo vision of continuous British territory and railway from Egypt to the Cape, first aired by Sir Edwin Arnold in 1876, became his life’s work. The British South Africa Company received a Royal Charter in autumn 1889 with “almost unlimited rights and powers of government” north of the Transvaal and west of the Portuguese, without fixed northern limit. Southern and Northern Rhodesia took his name. The Chartered Company wrote “a new volume of history with a big pen, sometimes… dipped in very red ink,” conquering Ndebele and Shona while Rhodes, Cape Premier (1890–96) and master of De Beers and the Gold Fields, combined mining capital with territorial ambition.

Gold on the Rand filled the Transvaal with Uitlanders who outnumbered Boers yet were denied the franchise by President Paul Kruger. “Sooner or later,” McCarthy wrote, “the influence of President Kruger and the influence of Mr. Cecil Rhodes must come into collision.” On 29 December 1895 Dr. Leander Starr Jameson rode from Bechuanaland into the Transvaal; the High Commissioner ordered him stopped; he pressed on, was defeated, and was sent to England for trial. A Parliamentary Committee found that Rhodes, controlling Chartered Company, De Beers, and Gold Fields, “directed and controlled the combination which rendered such a proceeding as the Jameson Raid possible.” The Kaiser’s telegram to Kruger inflamed Anglo-German feeling; Rhodes fell as Cape Premier; race feeling was embittered.

The road to the South African War ran through Sir Alfred Milner’s High Commissionership, the failed Bloemfontein Conference of May 1899, and Chamberlain’s pressure for a five-year Uitlander franchise. On 9 October 1899 the Transvaal issued an ultimatum; war followed on 12 October. Black Week (Stormberg, Magersfontein, Colenso, 10–17 December 1899) shattered expectations of a short campaign. Lord Roberts and Kitchener reorganised; Cronje surrendered at Paardeberg (27 February 1900); Bloemfontein and Pretoria fell; both republics were annexed. Guerrilla war under Botha, De Wet, and de la Rey then stretched across country “nearly equal to the area of Continental Europe.” Blockhouses, farm clearances, and concentration camps—denounced by Emily Hobhouse and by Campbell-Bannerman as methods of “barbarism”—became the political cost of reconquest. The Treaty of Vereeniging (May 1902) ended the war. Chamberlain had staked imperial paramountcy in South Africa; the conflict exposed continental hostility, strained isolation, and left reconstruction and Union (1910) as unfinished imperial business. Gandhi’s South African years belonged to the same settler empire the war had secured.

Free-trade empire and tariff preference

Imperialism was also a domestic and fiscal politics. For most of the Victorian age the commercial face of empire was free trade: open imports, cheap food, and the belief that British industry needed no protective wall. The self-governing Colonies, however, offered preference to British goods and asked reciprocity; Germany and the United States rose as protected competitors. Colonial federation and closer commercial union became Chamberlain’s programme after his years at the Colonial Office (1895–1903). Preferential tariffs were offered as the economic cement of empire—the alternative to a “Little England” dependent on the mercy of rivals.

On 15 May 1903, at Birmingham, Chamberlain opened the campaign for Tariff Reform and imperial preference: recover freedom to negotiate and, if necessary, retaliate; bind the Empire by duties that favoured colonial produce; fund social reform from new revenue if need be. The Unionist party split among Chamberlainite reformers, Balfourite moderates, and Unionist Free Traders. The general election of 1906 returned an overwhelming Free-Trade majority; Campbell-Bannerman and Asquith kept Cobdenite finance as the basis of Liberal social legislation. Yet Tariff Reform remained the chief Unionist imperial programme through the People’s Budget and the Lords crisis—the commercial argument of Empire against the free-trade Exchequer that still paid for navy and pensions. Free-trade empire and preference empire thus competed inside the same imperial state.

Sea power, isolation, and pre-war diplomacy

Strategic geography governed what annexation could hold. The Cape route, the Canal, coaling stations, and command of the sea made a scattered empire defensible; Mackinder’s Imperial Britain was a maritime system whose heart was the British Isles and whose unity depended on oceanic communication. The Naval Defence Act of 1889 had enshrined the two-power standard. Tirpitz’s Navy Laws after 1897 and Fisher’s redistribution and Dreadnought revolution turned that standard into the Anglo-German naval race—the fiscal underside of late imperialism, in which South African war feeling, Navy League agitation, and the 1909 dreadnought scare (“We want eight and we won’t wait!”) bound empire, taxes, and German enmity together.

Diplomatically, the Boer War shattered complacency about “splendid isolation.” Chamberlain told Baron Eckardstein that if alliance with Germany proved impossible, England would have to reach an understanding with France and Russia even at far-reaching cost. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (30 January 1902) was the first formal breach; Lansdowne discarded “musty formulas or old-fashioned superstitions.” The Entente Cordiale of 8 April 1904 was “essentially colonial in inspiration and results” yet possessed European significance: France recognised the British position in Egypt; Britain recognised French special interests in Morocco; Newfoundland, Siam, Madagascar, and the New Hebrides were settled. Diplomatic support in Morocco pulled Britain into continental crises at Tangier and Algeciras; staff talks followed. Colonial bargains had become the road out of isolation and into the alignment that faced Germany in 1914.

By 1914 the map of Africa was almost entirely coloured in European claims. The British share was vast—from Egypt and the Sudan through East and West Africa to the Cape and the Rhodesias—but so were the costs and the enmities. Imperialism had enlarged the empire, entangled Britain in continental rivalries through colonial bargains, forced naval concentration in the North Sea, and left Ireland and the Dominions as unfinished problems of self-government within a system still styled “Empire.”
