Labour Representation

Labour representation meant the election of working men—trade-union secretaries, municipal leaders, miners’ agents—to the House of Commons, and the organisation of those members into a distinct parliamentary force. It was the political counterpart of franchise reform: once the workman had the vote, he sought men of his own class to sit for industrial seats and to press legislation that would ease working-class life. Enfranchisement to secure representation for the redress of grievances guided English advance toward democracy; middle class and working class alike used the vote to obtain seats, and the return of a Labour party of some forty members marked the political arrival of organised labour within the constitution.

Development

The Acts of 1867 and 1884 made the entry of the workman into Parliament inevitable. The Reform Bill of 1867 enfranchised all male householders in boroughs and male lodgers paying £10 a year for unfurnished rooms, raising the electorate from about a million to two million and giving the town workman the vote as 1832 had given it to the middle-class man. Gladstone’s third Reform Act of 1884 extended household suffrage to the country districts, enfranchising the agricultural labourer; the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885 created single-member equal electoral districts on a basis of roughly 54,000 inhabitants and fixed the membership of the Commons at six hundred and seventy. With that double extension the change in the personnel of the House became marked. The richer middle class had entered in force after 1832; after 1867 and 1884 the workman followed.

Miners were the first to send Labour representatives to Westminster, and their members came to outnumber those of any other trade. From 1892 industrial constituencies—chiefly in Yorkshire, Lancashire, South Wales, and the mining districts—steadily elected and re-elected working-class representatives, trade-union secretaries and officers for the most part. Parallel to that electoral growth ran the organisation of independent labour politics. The Independent Labour Party, formed in 1892, professed Socialism yet aimed rather at returning labour members to Parliament and local councils than at converting the working class to dogmatic social democracy. Behind it lay the Socialist revival of the mid-1880s: the Social Democratic Federation founded by H. M. Hyndman in 1884, which never won trade-union confidence yet trained many future Labour leaders; and the Fabian Society of the same year, which educated opinion toward State interference in the conditions of life and labour. The Independent Labour Party and its offspring, the Labour Party in the Commons, became the effective parliamentary instrument.

The formation of a National Labour Representation Committee in 1900 gathered these representatives into a separate organisation. After 1906 they sat as the Labour Party. The general election of January–February 1906, climax of the Tariff Reform crisis and the Liberal landslide, returned Labour as a distinct parliamentary force rather than a mere wing of Liberalism. The Annual Register recorded fifty-three Labour members among the Ministerialist majority of five hundred and thirteen—twenty-nine of them from the Labour Representation Committee, sitting as an independent party—beside three hundred and seventy-seven Liberals and eighty-three Irish Nationalists, against one hundred and fifty-seven Unionists. When the new Parliament assembled on 13 February, J. W. Lowther was re-elected Speaker on the motion of Sir Edward Grey, seconded by Keir Hardie, who thus marked Labour’s arrival as a separate force. The King’s Speech promised legislation on Trade Disputes, Workmen’s Compensation, Merchant Shipping, and Education. At subsequent general elections some forty Labour members returned to Westminster; by the years just before the war more than forty workmen sat in the Commons.

Those members had almost all served apprenticeship in municipal and trade-union office. Northumberland, Durham, Yorkshire, Lancashire, Stafford, South Wales, Glasgow, Dundee, Leicester, Norwich, and London all returned Labour members. Preference fell on men who had proved honesty and capacity as municipal councillors or union leaders: G. N. Barnes, for ten years general secretary of the Amalgamated Society of Engineers; J. R. Clynes, long district secretary of the Gas Workers’ and General Labourers’ Union; Will Thorne, general secretary of that union since 1889 and a West Ham councillor for more than sixteen years; George Lansbury and Will Crooks, known for work on the London County Council, borough councils, and boards of guardians. All the miners’ representatives were tried and experienced men. In the House they were grave, business-like, and undemonstrative—respecting the rules and dignity of the assembly because public business required order and because they were there to get legislation for their constituents. When Victor Grayson, in youthful passion against the misery of the poor, made a scene and was expelled, Labour members disapproved: disorder alienated sympathy, was not helpful in debate, and was not popular with the electors.

Belloc and Cecil Chesterton, writing the party-system as this wiki names Clayton on the rise of democracy, treated that expulsion as something larger than a breach of manners. The two Front Benches were, they said, one Governing Group—a self-elected corporation filling vacancies by co-option—and the “kindly tolerance” politicians congratulated themselves upon was extended only to those who played the game. It was not extended to Parnell, and it was not extended to Mr Victor Grayson. Labour’s absorption, in their account, was not caused by the Tone of the House but by a definite compact with the Executive: places, advantage in moving motions, and ultimately, perhaps, Cabinet rank as the price of compromise. The real grip was secret Party Funds, the control of elections, and the choice of candidates. An independent member who asked a question or moved a motion affecting the machine was reminded that his ability to fight his seat again depended upon a secret Caucus and the money that kept it going. Once pledged to do nothing that might injure the party, a candidate could cheerfully pledge himself to almost anything else, knowing that if the measure was inconvenient to the Front Benches he would have no chance of voting on it, or his vote would be rendered harmless by the subsequent shelving of the question. Only three types normally entered Parliament: local rich men who dominated the local organisation; rich men from outside who had suborned the central organisation; and comparatively poor men willing, for a seat and the chances of material gain, to become the obedient servants of the caucus.

The Old Age Pensions Bill of 1908 was their chosen farce of representation. They first describe a supposed Distress Relief Bill whose Committee stage is consumed in verbal amendments—seven lines of the bill “debated” in a day, nothing changed—until the Front Benches closure the rest before the vital clause is reached and “the people of the West Riding are left without relief.” Substitute, they write, destitute people over seventy for South Lancashire, the Old Age Pensions Bill for the Distress Relief Bill, and destitute old people between sixty-five and seventy for the West Riding, “and you have an exact and literal account of what took place” in Committee in the summer of 1908. The one thing that most mattered to the destitute was dealt with “precisely as a despotic monarchy would deal with it,” without a despot’s moral right: “the procedure of the House had been used simply to cheat the people.”

F. W. Jowett, Labour M.P., they treat as an outsider proposing to smash that machinery. In the penny pamphlet What is the Use of Parliament? he would abolish the Ministry with its collective responsibility and substitute departmental committees of the House, like those of local councils, on which all parties would sit and to which the permanent officials would be responsible. The Minister would remain as chairman and might be outvoted; neither that nor a reversal by the House would produce a “crisis.” Belloc and Chesterton called the exposure lucid and the examples typical, and allowed that if freely and honestly carried out, with duration of Parliament fixed by law so that Front Benches could not force a dissolution, the scheme would smash the Party System and give a free deliberative assembly its machinery. As things stood they saw little chance of honest working: committees packed with partisans; independent members kept off the particular committees where they might be dangerous. Grayson had told the world how he applied to sit on a Committee of Social Reform and was immediately appointed to the Irish Linen Marks Bill—“no insignificant indication” of what Jowett’s plan would become in a House still dominated by the Party System.

The House itself proved more orderly and industrious than a century earlier. It asked of a new member not birth or wealth but awareness of the honour of membership, modesty of bearing, and readiness to catch the tone of the chamber. A labourer or the son of a belted earl was indifferent so long as parliamentary manners were good. Labour’s presence increased rather than diminished that good behaviour and compelled a volume of social legislation—Workmen’s Compensation, old-age pensions, the Trade Disputes Act, and kindred measures—that members of fifty years earlier would have thought impossible. Once in Parliament or on city councils, Labour men became absorbed in practical legislation and administration; the return of so many of them damped the fires of scientific Socialism by placing the ablest working-class leaders in positions of authority. Social Reform took the place of social democracy as the conscious programme of democratic politics.

Three working-class leaders stood out in contemporary politics: John Burns, Keir Hardie, and J. Ramsay MacDonald. Two of an earlier generation—Thomas Burt and Henry Broadhurst—had held under-secretaryships in the Liberal ministry of 1892–5; Burns was the first trade unionist to sit in the Cabinet. He might have taken an under-secretaryship then, but decided that if he was fit for that place he was fit for the Cabinet. At the close of 1905 Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman offered him the Local Government Board; at forty-seven he accepted, and the workman first took his place in the Cabinet. His robust egoism was largely class pride: whatever John Burns did, that the working class did in the person of their representative. He had polled as a Social Democratic candidate at West Nottingham in 1885; been charged with seditious conspiracy after an unemployed riot in 1886 and acquitted; suffered six weeks’ imprisonment with Cunninghame Graham for free speech in Trafalgar Square in 1887; led the London dockers with Mann and Tillett in 1889; and been returned by Battersea to the London County Council in 1889 and to the Commons in 1892. The Liberal Party offered a wider sphere than the Socialists; political isolation was barren; and Burns passed from the trade-union movement to the Treasury Bench. Socialist convictions of early manhood mellowed under responsibility, yet he still held that the working class must work out its own salvation rather than leave affairs to a benevolent bureaucracy.

Hardie, a Lanarkshire miner and active trade unionist, embodied independent Labour politics. To him, more than to any other man, the existence of the Independent Labour Party and of the Parliamentary Labour Party—the latter consisting of the Independent Labour Party and the trade unions—was due. Political independence of an organised working class was the one great idea of his public life. Both he and Burns entered the House in 1892. From 1895 to 1900 Hardie was out of Parliament, rejected by South West Ham; nothing turned him from independence. Merthyr Tydvil re-elected him in 1900 at the very time he was denouncing the South African War. He would never take office in a Liberal ministry; sturdy republicanism kept him from court functions and the dinner parties of the rich. Visionary, with a religious temperament that treated political work as a solemn calling, he recruited the Independent Labour Party considerably from Nonconformists with Puritan traditions, and lived to see an independent Labour Party of forty members and to serve as its accredited leader.

MacDonald, elected leader of the Labour Party and its secretary since its formation, was for the working class but, though born of labouring people and educated in a Scotch board school, had long ceased to be of them. Never a workman and never associated with a trade union, he went from school teaching to journalism and a political private secretaryship, settled into the habits of the ruling middle class, and by marriage removed fear of poverty. He entered the Commons only in 1906, at forty; as leader of the Labour Party he was already a distinguished parliamentary figure. Native prudence had restrained him from pioneering, but once Hardie’s Independent Labour Party promised an effectual means of political work he joined it. He studied politics as others study art or science; by indefatigable industry he became controlling power first in the Independent Labour Party and then in the National Labour Party. Contemporaries expected him as a Cabinet Minister—even as Prime Minister—in a Liberal-Labour Government: a “safe” man without taint of fanaticism. Burns and Hardie remained, in their own and the public eye, representatives of the working class; MacDonald adapted himself to the life and manners of the wealthier professional classes and moved without constraint in the social world of high politics.

David Lloyd George, though of the industrious Nonconformist middle class rather than the workshop, illustrated the same opening of the highest offices to men neither wealthy nor of distinguished family. He entered Parliament in 1890 at twenty-seven; with Burns he entered the Cabinet at the Liberal revival of 1905, and government by aristocracy was ended. From the Board of Trade he passed to the Exchequer; the Budget of 1909 and National Insurance made his name the most loudly spoken in the country. The Cabinet had ceased to be an aristocratic committee.

Labour representation was tested again in the years of the great labour unrest of 1911–1914. On the Address in 1912 Ramsay MacDonald moved a Labour amendment ascribing industrial trouble to insufficient wages amid expanding national wealth and rising living costs: of some 7,300,000 wage-earners, he said, sixty per cent had incomes of less than thirty shillings a week; a minimum wage, begun experimentally by the Trade Boards Act of 1910, must go on. J. R. Clynes seconded. When the national coal stoppage of March 1912 forced Asquith’s government to legislate a district minimum wage, Labour members spoke for the miners’ claim that a minimum for abnormal places must have legal sanction. In the railway crisis of August 1911 Arthur Henderson sat on the Special Commission that investigated the 1907 conciliation scheme; MacDonald attacked the Home Office for a “reckless display of force” even as settlement came through Board of Trade channels. Parliamentary Labour thus stood between the industrial army outside and the Liberal ministry inside—pressing social claims, criticising the use of troops, yet remaining a constitutional force within the House.

War completed the transformation. In May–June 1915, when shells, drink, and the Admiralty rupture made pure party government impossible, Asquith reconstructed his administration as the First Coalition. Unionists and Labour sat on the Treasury Bench with Liberals; both Houses reassembled on 3 June for the first sitting of the Coalition, and the old party geography of the chamber was visibly altered. Labour accepted a place in national government for the duration of the emergency. The franchise settlement that followed closed the long sequence of parliamentary reform. The Representation of the People Act 1918, shaped by a Speaker’s Conference and carried through the critical stages of 1917, wrote women into the electorate at thirty, secured the vote of soldiers and sailors, redistributed seats toward industrial and urban centres, and produced the greatest single extension of the parliamentary franchise in British history. By 26 February 1918, when the Labour Party met in resumed Annual Conference at the Central Hall, Westminster, the party adopted a new constitution transforming itself from a federation of societies into a national democratic political organisation open to every worker who laboured “by hand or by brain.” The change marked the determination of the party leaders to seize the opportunity of the widened electorate and the enfranchisement of women. The first general election under the new register returned Labour candidates with nearly two and a half million votes. The electoral foundation of modern labour representation was completed at last by statute.
