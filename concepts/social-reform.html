Social Reform

Social reform was the programme of state action—pensions, insurance, labour regulation, and progressive taxation—by which late-Victorian and Edwardian governments sought to mitigate poverty without the common ownership demanded by Socialism. In the years after 1906 it became the central domestic work of the Liberal ministries, financed by Free-Trade revenue, opposed by the house-of-lords, and pressed forward under the pressure of labour-representation and industrial unrest.

Development

In the late seventies and early eighties republicanism still claimed many ardent working-class Radicals; Charles Bradlaugh was its chief exponent, and both Joseph Chamberlain and Sir Charles Dilke had been suspected of republican leanings. But the rise of Socialism in the eighties diverted republican enthusiasm into social channels. Robert Owen’s utopian Socialism had flourished and died, as Chartism flourished and died. Marx’s “scientific” Socialism claimed to emancipate the workers from wage slavery and insisted that the change was inevitable. On the Continent Social Democracy gained enormous working-class support. In England the Social Democratic Federation (later the Social Democratic Party), founded in 1884 by H. M. Hyndman, never won the sympathy of the trade unions nor the confidence of the working-class electorate; yet John Burns and many another Labour leader passed through its ranks, and a social conscience was made sensitive to the miseries of the poor. The Fabian Society, also founded in 1884, made no proposal for a separate Socialist party; its influence showed mainly in statistical knowledge and State interference in the conditions of life and labour. The Independent Labour Party (1892) professed Socialism yet aimed rather at returning labour members to Parliament and local councils than at converting the working class to dogmatic social democracy. Often frankly opportunist, the ILP and its offspring, the Labour Party in the Commons, followed the national custom of attacking and redressing evident evils—with considerable success. That success was the institutional foundation of modern labour-representation.

While Socialists made social reform the chief concern of all politicians, the idea of a social democracy steadily receded. Socialist workmen in Parliament found themselves absorbed in practical legislation, with neither leisure nor outlet for revolutionary propaganda. The return of so many Labour members damped down the fires of Socialism by placing the ablest leaders in positions of authority. John Burns might be denounced as a renegade by Socialist critics, but a working-class electorate returned him; Cunninghame Graham and Victor Grayson might be applauded for consistency, but working-class constituencies were loth to return such representatives. As Socialism became a dim and remote possibility of the future, Social Reform took its place. In Britain, where practical politics had always prevailed over theory, the old Liberal doctrines of laissez faire, unrestricted competition, and the personal liberty of the subject were as dead as the Stuart doctrine of divine right. Hostility to State interference and compulsory social legislation melted away. Lord Cromer and Harold Cox still repeated the ancient watch-words of Victorian Liberalism—and were regarded as strange survivals, but no popular echo followed. Pensions for the aged, better provision for the sick and infirm, the well-being of children, national health, some cure for destitution, and some remedy for unemployment—these were the matters a Liberal Government was concerned about. Conservatives were no less sincere; legislative proposals for social reform were treated as non-party questions, and Tariff Reform was advocated mainly as a social reform, a cure for industrial evils and unemployment. Thus the debate over free-trade-and-tariff-reform was itself framed, on the Unionist side, as a competing path of social betterment.

The two ideals stood in clear contrast. Socialism proposed the abolition of poverty and the common ownership of land and the means of production, distribution, and exchange. Social Reform proposed to mitigate the hardships of life for the multitude and, while leaving land and capital in private hands, to compel by taxation provision for the wants of the people. Its aim was the abolition of destitution by State assistance to voluntary effort and the gradual raising of the standard of life—not the removal of the cause of poverty. It promised a living wage, decent housing, insurance against unemployment, and security in old age, and left national or private ownership to posterity. Alongside both ideals ran Henry George’s Single Tax: Progress and Poverty (1879) became the bible of land reformers as Marx’s Capital was (or had been) of Socialists. George’s visits in 1881, 1884, and 1889 strengthened the movement for taxing land values; Liberals were won over—if not to twenty shillings in the pound, at least to a tax on land values—and principal municipalities petitioned Parliament in its favour.

The Liberal landslide of 1906—the election-1906-tariff-reform that broke Tariff Reform at the polls and returned a majority swollen by Labour and Irish Nationalist support—put the condition of the people at the centre of the parliamentary programme. Free-Trade finance was to carry social legislation without food taxes. Under Campbell-Bannerman, and then under Asquith, the statute book received Workmen’s Compensation, the Trade Disputes Act, regulation of sweated trades, and the first great social-security statute of the ministry.

The Old Age Pensions Act of 1908 created a non-contributory state pension for persons over seventy, paid from the Exchequer and subject to tests of means, character, and poor-relief history. On 15 January 1908 Asquith, still Chancellor, promised at Lancaster that a scheme would be begun. Lord Cromer, five days earlier at Glasgow, had condemned any such scheme as developing “demoralising dependence on the State.” The Speech from the Throne on 29 January promised proposals “for making better provision for old age”; Lansdowne condemned “the assumption of the colossal liability of old-age pensions.” After Campbell-Bannerman’s death in April, Asquith became Prime Minister and David Lloyd George succeeded him at the Exchequer. Moving the second reading on 16–17 June, Lloyd George said contributory schemes had been abandoned by most reformers, including Chamberlain; they would exclude women; and the failure of friendly societies’ superannuation schemes had shown the contributory plan impracticable. Of 1,262,632 persons over seventy, 572,000 were estimated pensionable; annual cost £7,500,000, with £6,000,000 for the first year—“the most liberal scheme yet proposed in any old country.” The Bill imposed a qualified character test and disqualified persons who had received poor relief (other than medical relief) since 1 January 1907. Friendly societies regretted the absence of thrift encouragement; labour organisations treated the measure as only an instalment. Harold Cox condemned expenditure by “arbitrary standards of age, income and character”; O’Grady for Labour declared the Bill “hopelessly inadequate.” Haldane replied that it embodied the poor-law principle since Elizabeth—that the prosperous must relieve the necessitous. By early 1909 some 490,000 had established claims, with about 130,000 more probable. Asquith insisted that Free-Trade finance would “stand the strain of social reform,” and that the Act was “only the first instalment.”

That instalment forced the financial crisis of 1909. Parliament opened under King Edward VII with a Speech warning that old-age provision and the Navy would raise expenditure. Asquith told the Commons that finance must be the first business: social reform had to be paid for. On 29 April Lloyd George introduced the Finance Bill that became the People’s Budget—a “War Budget” against poverty. It proposed a 20 per cent tax on the unearned increment of land when sold; a halfpenny in the pound on the capital value of undeveloped land; a 10 per cent reversion duty on a landlord’s interest at lease expiry; increased death duties; and a super-tax on incomes above £5,000—proceeds to fund pensions and naval construction amid accelerating Anglo-German rivalry. Balfour declared the force behind the Budget “really Socialism.” Austen Chamberlain, moving rejection, indicated that the Opposition alternative was Tariff Reform. The Lord Advocate answered that land owed its value to the community and that the Budget was “a great and democratic Budget.” After five months of Commons debate the Lords, on 30 November 1909, rejected the Bill by 350 to 75—a breach of the convention that the Upper House did not interfere with money bills. The constitutional crisis that followed—two general elections in 1910, a broken Constitutional Conference, and the Parliament Act of 1911 stripping the Lords of absolute veto—joined the financing of pensions and the Navy to social reform, free trade against Tariff Reform, and the Irish demand that Home Rule no longer be blocked by an hereditary chamber. At Mile End in November 1910 Lloyd George put the issue in popular form: the Government needed money for defence and social reforms by taxing great incomes, while the Lords demanded that the burden fall on the bread and meat of the people. The People’s Budget struggle made progressive taxation for social expenditure inseparable from the remaking of the constitution.

Once the Parliament Act had broken the absolute veto, the Government could press social measures the Lords could no longer kill outright. When King George V opened his first Parliament, the Speech named, as the only Bills specifically promised, removal of the pauper disqualification for old-age pensions and “insurance against sickness, invalidity, and (in special trades) unemployment.” Asquith had already, at Glasgow in April 1909, sketched labour exchanges and unemployment insurance as “the next stage of social reform after old-age pensions and debt reduction.” Lloyd George’s visit to Germany in August 1908 to study workmen’s insurance had given him a continental model of contributory provision that the non-contributory pension scheme had deliberately rejected. The National Insurance Act of 1911 established compulsory insurance against sickness and invalidity for the mass of employed persons, and against unemployment in selected trades. Contributions were shared among worker, employer, and the State; benefits ran largely through approved friendly societies, with a provisional Post Office scheme for those outside. Separate Commissioners covered Ireland, Scotland, and Wales. Sick pay was set at 10s. per week for men and 7s. 6d. for women for twenty-six weeks; maternity benefit extended to the wife of an insured person; persons earning less than 9s. a week paid no contribution. Labour opposition was not to insurance in principle but to the Bill’s treatment of casuals, sweated workers, and “poor lives.” Keir Hardie’s proposal to give maternity benefit to unmarried mothers was defeated 207 to 95; yet when the great strikes of August estranged Labour and some advanced Liberals, the Government had “gained in popularity, apparently, by the Insurance Bill.” “Joy Day,” 15 July 1912, was the day the Act came into force: of 13,000,000 persons compulsorily insurable, 9,500,000 had already joined approved societies. Medical resistance over panel terms continued; Bonar Law answered “Certainly” when asked whether he would repeal it if he became Prime Minister—then qualified the admission in the press.

Social reform did not quiet the workshops. The great-labour-unrest-1911-1914 of seamen, dockers, railwaymen, miners, and transport workers ran parallel to the Insurance Act, the aftermath of the People’s Budget, and the deepening Irish crisis. Bonar Law, at Leeds in autumn 1911, blamed the Budget campaign of 1909 for intensifying working-class unrest, of which the labour disturbances were “the fruit,” and pointed to a rise of nearly 10 per cent. in the cost of living without a corresponding rise in wages; the Government’s method, he said, was to take from the rich by taxation and give to the poor in doles. The coal strike of March 1912 forced a district minimum-wage statute; Ramsay Macdonald, on the Address of 1912, ascribed labour troubles to insufficient wages amid expanding national wealth—of 7,300,000 wage-earners, 60 per cent. under 30s. a week. Social reform had fixed pensions and insurance on the statute book; it had not abolished the industrial conflict that Socialism had once promised to end by reconstruction.

Belloc, writing while the same statutes were still new, refused that middle ground. In The Servile State he argued that social reform which registered the proletariat, erected wages boards, mapped men through labour exchanges, and levied insurance contributions did not restore the Institution of Property and did not confiscate the means of production for the community. It established security and sufficiency without ownership: the wage-earner kept his legal freedom only so long as he remained unregistered and uncompelled; once the State defined him by employment, collected a poll-tax through his master, and accumulated a dossier of work refused, he slid toward status and compulsory labour. Collectivism, Belloc held, was the ideal reformers named; the servile-state was the third thing their statutes actually produced—owners confirmed, the mass constrained, and the path of confiscation made more remote with every “positive economic reform.” The national-insurance-1911 was his cardinal instance: compulsion attached to the employed, not to the citizen as citizen; the Capitalist class enforced registration and gathered the tax.

Clayton, writing while these measures were still new, listed old-age pensions with Workmen’s Compensation and the Trade Disputes Act among the labour legislation of the previous twenty years that demonstrated “the value of parliamentary representation to the working class.” Social reform thus occupied the middle ground between laissez faire and collectivism. It assumed free trade and a solvent Exchequer; it used the newly widened franchise and the presence of Labour members to compel legislation that the Commons of fifty years earlier would have thought impossible; and it left private property standing while taxing it more heavily for public ends. Socialism proposed the abolition of poverty by reconstruction; social reform proposed its reduction by statute. The years 1906–14 were the high season of the second path—interrupted by war before a full reckoning with either ideal could be made.
