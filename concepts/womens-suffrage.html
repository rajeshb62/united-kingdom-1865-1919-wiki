Women’s Suffrage

Women’s suffrage was the claim that women should share the parliamentary franchise on terms that already qualified men—or on closely related occupation and household qualifications—and thereby sit, through their chosen representatives, within the constitution that taxed them, bound them by statute, and increasingly admitted them to local government. It was the last great sex disability in a century of parliamentary-reform that had already opened Parliament and public life to Catholics, Jews, Nonconformists, freethinkers, and working men. Enfranchisement and representation were, in the language of the movement itself, the two chosen instruments of democratic government.

Development

Medieval exemption and modern exclusion

Medieval practice had excused women from parliamentary attendance and from juries without treating their powers and privileges as landowners as shortened by sex. The modern exclusion was judicial and statutory. The Reform Act of 1832, by writing “male person” into the parliamentary franchise, fixed in statute what custom had left ambiguous. Thereafter the path to the vote ran only through Parliament—or through the courts, which in 1868 closed the judicial route.

Mill’s amendment, 1867

During the discussions on the Reform Bill of 1867, John Stuart Mill introduced an amendment to admit women who were registered occupiers as well as men to the franchise—in other words, to make the qualification one of occupation only, without reference to sex. The majority of the House were at first disposed to regard the proposition as something merely droll, and to deal with it only in the spirit of pleasantry; but the debate proved a very interesting, grave, and able discussion, and it was the opening of a momentous chapter of political controversy. Mill got seventy-three members to follow him into the lobby; 196 voted the other way. The amendment that women should not be excluded from the franchise was defeated. The borough franchise that emerged from the Act established household suffrage for male householders and £10 male lodgers, and left sex as an express barrier. From that time supporters of women’s suffrage were always present in the Commons. Henry Fawcett, the radical Liberal who married Millicent Garrett Fawcett, was remembered as “a most ardent advocate of women’s claims to the legislative suffrage.”

Chorlton v. Lings and the local-government franchise

In 1868 the test case Chorlton v. Lings was brought. The judges decided that women householders were not to be registered as electors; it was left to Parliament to pass a Women’s Enfranchisement Bill. The parliamentary door closed; the municipal door opened almost at once. Since 1869 women who were householders enjoyed the municipal franchise, and as Poor Law guardians and members of school boards they were freely elected to sit side by side with men. Local government became the practical school of female citizenship while the national franchise remained closed. In 1907 Parliament declared women eligible for membership on county and borough councils, and for the chairmanship of county councils and the mayoralty of boroughs. Women entered the councils of great cities—Manchester and Liverpool among them—and were chosen as mayors in several towns. The contrast sharpened the grievance: women ratepayers and councillors still stood outside the House that levied the taxes and made the laws they administered.

Constitutional campaign, bills, and Conciliation

A Women’s Suffrage Bill introduced in 1870 passed its second reading in the Commons by 124 to 91 but was later blocked by the government. During a debate on women’s suffrage in 1871, Gladstone stated that if the ballot were once introduced there would be no harm done by allowing women to vote—an admission that the secret ballot would alter the conditions of the controversy, removing the duty of going up to the open poll. Another bill reached a second reading in 1884, during the passage of the Third Reform Act, but Gladstone refused to include women’s suffrage in the government’s franchise measure, arguing that the question was not ripe for settlement. The Act of 1884, which extended household suffrage to the counties, confined the vote to male householders.

Throughout the 1880s and 1890s the constitutional wing grew. In 1897 the many local and regional women’s suffrage societies were federated into the National Union of Women’s Suffrage Societies (NUWSS) under the presidency of Millicent Garrett Fawcett. The NUWSS pursued parliamentary lobbying, petitioning, public meetings, and the education of opinion. By the turn of the century the organisation represented some 300,000 women; successive governments still declined to bring forward a franchise bill. Only the refusal of the Government to give time for discussion of the Bill in Committee prevented a Woman’s Enfranchisement measure, which on several occasions received a second reading, from passing the Commons.

Women were not asking for adult suffrage, but were willing to receive enfranchisement on the terms that qualified men as electors. The Conciliation Bill—so called because members of every political party agreed to make it their Bill—would have placed on the roll of electors rather more than a million women voters. The demand rested on the old constitutional ground of the Parliamentarians of the seventeenth century—that those who are directly taxed by Government must have some political control of the public expenditure—and it was supported by A. J. Balfour, then leader of the Conservative Party, on the ground that government can only be carried on in England by consent of the governed. Campbell-Bannerman stated that personally he warmly supported the enfranchisement of women: they were taxed without direct representation, and had to obey laws they did not make. The agitation for “Votes for Women” became one of the most conspicuous public movements of the age—parallel, in the minds of its advocates, to the earlier certainty of middle-class men and workmen that Parliament would not understand popular grievances until they chose their own representatives. Enfranchisement and representation were the instruments women sought; the same instruments underlay the rise of labour-representation for working men.

The militant movement

Frustration with the slow pace of constitutional methods produced a schism that became the suffragette-movement. In 1903 Emmeline Pankhurst and her daughter Christabel founded the Women’s Social and Political Union (WSPU) in Manchester, adopting the motto “Deeds, not words.” The first militant phase began in 1905 when Christabel Pankhurst and Annie Kenney disrupted a Liberal meeting in Manchester, courting arrest for publicity. The Annual Register for 1906 recorded that the agitation by the extreme supporters of women’s suffrage, popularly known as the “suffragettes,” had caused a good deal of disturbance both in Liberal meetings and in the House of Commons itself. In June 1906 a deputation attempted to force its way to the Prime Minister’s house in Cavendish Square; one leader assaulted a police sergeant and was sentenced after refusing to recognise courts “on the ground that laws were made exclusively by men.”

In 1907 nearly thirty women’s suffrage societies, representing some 300,000 women, held a conference under Mrs. Fawcett and passed resolutions that neither sex nor marriage should disqualify for the franchise. The WSPU escalated: in 1908 suffragettes chained themselves to the railings of 10 Downing Street; by 1909 prisoners in Holloway broke windows, assaulted wardresses, and adopted the “hunger strike.” The government’s response—forcible feeding—provoked intense public controversy. After 1910, when a promised Conciliation Bill that would have enfranchised women householders was abandoned by the government of Asquith, the WSPU answered with systematic attacks on property—letterboxes set alight, windows smashed, unoccupied houses burned (Ascot, Clevedon, Kew among them). In April 1913 the Prisoners (Temporary Discharge for Ill-Health) Bill—the “Cat and Mouse Act”—allowed hunger-striking prisoners to be released on licence when health was endangered and rearrested when recovered; it did nothing to moderate the agitation. On 4 June 1913 Emily Wilding Davison ran onto the course at the Epsom Derby, was struck by the King’s horse Anmer, and died four days later. By the spring of 1914 militancy had reached its peak—churches burned, paintings slashed at the National Gallery and Royal Academy, a deputation toward Buckingham Palace leading to the arrest of sixty-six women. WSPU receipts for the year ending February 1914 amounted to nearly £37,000.

War, suspension, and the Reform Act of 1918

The outbreak-of-the-great-war transformed the situation. On 10 August 1914 the government announced a general amnesty for suffragist prisoners. Both the non-militant and the militant groups of the suffrage societies provisionally abandoned their agitation and organised themselves for the relief of women and children sufferers by the war. Admirable work was done by the National Union of Women’s Suffrage Societies and by the Women’s Emergency Corps; Christabel Pankhurst, on her return from Paris, spoke at meetings designed to encourage recruiting for the “new Army.” Women filled factory jobs—particularly in munitions—served as nurses, drove ambulances, worked on the land, and replaced men in civilian occupations. By 1918 they had demonstrated capacity for labour previously considered the exclusive domain of men.

The need for a new franchise became practical before it became comprehensive. Soldiers and sailors abroad could not be left off any wartime register; from that narrow necessity the path led to a Speaker’s Conference on electoral reform and a full reconstruction of the franchise. Many members declared that the experiences of the conflict had shown that the conscious co-operation of women in the activities of the nation was a source of strength and not of weakness. On 19–20 June 1917 the Commons fixed the women’s age-limit at thirty; Sir George Cave opposed lowering it, and the women’s clause was carried by 214 to 17. The age of thirty was the price of carrying the principle: it kept the women’s electorate short of a majority of the whole, and held together a wartime coalition of suffragists and cautious converts that a more sweeping clause might have broken.

The Representation of the People Act 1918—the largest single extension of the franchise in British history—received the Royal Assent on 6 February 1918, at the close of the third war session. Its main women’s provisions were exact. Men qualified at twenty-one years of age with six months’ residence or occupation of business premises. Women qualified at thirty years of age, and either as a local-government elector or as the wife of one. The qualification for the local-government franchise was six months’ ownership or tenancy of land or premises; lodgers in furnished rooms were not qualified. University franchise extended to a man or woman within those age-limits who had taken a degree (in England and Wales), and a woman was also qualified to vote for a university that did not admit women to degrees if she had fulfilled the conditions for the admission of a man to a degree. Naval and military voters were to be registered for the constituencies for which they would have been qualified but for their service; male voters who had served in the war were qualified at nineteen. Dual voting was limited to two constituencies at a general election; the receipt of poor relief ceased to be a disqualification.

In the House of Lords the clause which enfranchised women was passed on 10 January 1918 by 134 votes to 71—a majority of sixty-three. A particularly powerful plea for woman suffrage was made by Lord Selborne, who declared that the enfranchisement of women would bring strength and fresh power to the country and the Empire. The Archbishop of Canterbury also strongly supported the measure.

The scale of the Act was unprecedented. While the Reform Act of 1832 enfranchised 500,000 new voters, that of 1867 one million, and that of 1884 two million, the Act of 1918 enfranchised eight million new voters—six million being women, of whom five million were married, and two million being men. Full adult equality of age with men still lay in the future, but the sex bar on the parliamentary franchise had been broken. Later in 1918 the Parliament (Qualification of Women) Act, passed on 23 October, removed the bar on women standing for election to the Commons (carried by 274 to 25). Under the Coalition that Lloyd George led into the post-armistice election, the duty of removing remaining legal inequalities between men and women entered the programme of reconstruction; the franchise Act had opened the gate.
