The Scramble for Africa and Partition (1880–1914)

The Scramble for Africa was the rapid invasion, occupation, colonisation, and partition of the African continent by European powers during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. What Langer calls the “tremendous, almost epic outburst of European interest in affairs that lay beyond the frontiers of Europe” transformed the political map of Africa with extraordinary speed. The British Empire, already the largest colonial power, emerged from the Scramble with vast new acquisitions stretching from Egypt in the north to the Cape in the south — a continuous belt of territory that imperialists dreamed would one day run uninterrupted from Cairo to the Cape of Good Hope.

The partition was driven by a confluence of forces: the strategic imperative to secure the sea route to India through the Suez Canal; the economic pressure of intensifying European industrial competition and the search for new markets; the psychological and ideological appeals of imperial expansion; and the deadlock in European continental politics after the formation of the Franco-Russian Alliance, which redirected the energies of the great powers toward overseas competition. As Langer observed, “the rivalry and competition between the great powers in Africa, in the Far East and in the Near East were, during the last years of the dying century, every whit as important, and perhaps more important, than the shifting trends of purely European diplomacy.”

The Berlin Conference and the Rules of Partition

The Berlin Conference of 1884–1885, convened by the German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck, established the framework within which the European powers partitioned Africa. The conference was called in response to the intense rivalries that had erupted following King Leopold II of Belgium’s claims in the Congo Basin and the expansionist activities of other European powers along the African coasts.

Fourteen nations participated, though no African representatives were invited. The conference produced the General Act of Berlin, which established several key principles for the colonisation of Africa. It declared the Congo Basin a free trade zone and guaranteed freedom of navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers. Most consequentially, it laid down the “principle of effective occupation”: a European power could only claim African territory if it could demonstrate actual control through administration, military presence, or treaties with local chiefs. This doctrine — known as the doctrine of hinterland — became the legal architecture of partition. The conference recognised the International Association of the Congo (Leopold II’s private enterprise) as the legitimate authority over the Congo region, effectively creating the Congo Free State as a personal possession of the Belgian king.

Although the Berlin Conference was ostensibly about free trade and humanitarian principles — including the suppression of the slave trade — its practical effect was to accelerate the partition by defining the rules of the game. Once the principle of effective occupation was established, the European powers raced to plant flags, sign treaties with African rulers, and establish administrative posts to secure their claims. The conference did not divide Africa itself, but it set the diplomatic and legal framework within which the division would be carried out over the following decades.

The Berlin Act was followed by a series of bilateral treaties between the European powers that delimited colonial boundaries. Among the most important were the Anglo-German Agreement of 1886 defining spheres in East Africa, the Anglo-French Convention of 1889 delimiting West African boundaries, and the Anglo-German Heligoland-Zanzibar Treaty of 1890, which fundamentally shaped the British position in East Africa.

The British Advance: East and West Africa

Egypt and the Sudan

The British occupation of Egypt in 1882 was the decisive event that drew Britain deep into African affairs. Though the Gladstone government had repeatedly declared its opposition to a permanent occupation, the suppression of the Arabi revolt at Tel-el-Kebir was followed not by withdrawal but by an indefinite stay. Langer observes that the occupation of Egypt was the “initial impetus to British imperialism in the last years of the century.” Gladstone intended to withdraw; even Salisbury expected to make some adjustment. But after 1887, when France and Russia blocked the projected Anglo-Turkish convention, “the chances of evacuation rapidly diminished.”

From Egypt, Britain was drawn into the Sudan. The Mahdist revolt under Muhammad Ahmad swept the Sudan in the 1880s, culminating in the fall of Khartoum and the death of General Gordon in January 1885. For over a decade thereafter, the Sudan was abandoned to Mahdist rule while British attention was occupied elsewhere. The reconquest began in 1896 under General Herbert Kitchener, whose methodical campaign up the Nile — accompanied by a railway built across the desert — culminated in the decisive victory at Omdurman in September 1898. The Sudan was restored as an Anglo-Egyptian condominium in 1899, formally under joint sovereignty but in practice under British control.

East Africa: Kenya and Uganda

In East Africa, British expansion was driven by a combination of missionary activity, commercial enterprise, and strategic rivalry with Germany. The Imperial British East Africa Company (IBEA), chartered in 1888 under the leadership of Sir William MacKinnon, was the vehicle for British claims in the region. Henry Morton Stanley’s “Relief Expedition” to Emin Pasha in 1887–1889 further stimulated British interest in the interior.

The German presence in East Africa posed a direct challenge. The German explorer Karl Peters, acting under the auspices of the German East Africa Company, had penetrated inland and by March 1890 had reached Uganda and concluded a treaty of protection with the King. An expedition sent out by the IBEA arrived in Uganda only a month later. “This race for Uganda and the threat of Peters to cut the British off from the interior and from the Nile created a sensation in England,” Langer records. The Times on 3 April 1890 called attention to the danger of having the Germans on the Nile.

The rivalry was resolved by the Anglo-German Agreement of 1890 (the Heligoland-Zanzibar Treaty), which assigned Uganda and the territory north of a line running from the southern tip of Lake Victoria to the northern tip of Lake Tanganyika to the British sphere. Britain subsequently decided to retain Uganda as a protectorate (declared in 1894), and Kenya — then known as the East Africa Protectorate — was gradually brought under British administration. The Uganda Railway, begun in 1896 and completed in 1901, linked the East African coast with Lake Victoria and cemented British control over the interior.

Nyasaland and Central Africa

Further south, British influence was extended over the territories around Lake Nyasa. The British African Lakes Company had been active in the region since the 1870s, combating the Arab slave trade and establishing missionary stations. In 1889, Cecil Rhodes began subsidising the company and sent Harry Johnston — whom he had indoctrinated with his imperial vision — to Nyasaland to set up posts between Lake Nyasa and Lake Tanganyika, territory to which the Germans were also laying claim. By 1890, the British Central Africa Protectorate (later Nyasaland) was established.

West Africa: Nigeria, the Gold Coast, and the Niger Basin

In West Africa, British expansion followed the Niger River inland and extended British authority over the territories that would become Nigeria and the Gold Coast. The Royal Niger Company, chartered in 1886 under Sir George Goldie, was the instrument of British penetration of the Niger Basin. Through a combination of treaties with local rulers and military force, the company secured British claims to the lower Niger and the Benue Valley, outmanoeuvring French and German rivals. The Royal Niger Company’s charter was revoked in 1899, and the British government took over direct administration of the territories, which were amalgamated into the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria and the Protectorate of Southern Nigeria in 1900. In 1914, these were unified into the Colony and Protectorate of Nigeria.

The Gold Coast (modern Ghana) had been a British possession since the early nineteenth century, but British authority was contested by the powerful Ashanti Confederacy. After several Anglo-Ashanti wars — the most important being the war of 1873–1874 and the final conflict of 1895–1896 — the British annexed Ashanti in 1901 and incorporated it into the Gold Coast Colony.

The Benin Expedition of 1897, following the massacre of a British mission to the Kingdom of Benin, resulted in the capture of Benin City and the extension of British authority over the Niger Delta region. The famous Benin bronzes, seized as booty, were carried to Europe.

The Cape-to-Cairo Dream and Rhodesia

Cecil John Rhodes was the most energetic and visionary of the British empire-builders in Africa. As Langer describes him, he was “the great imperialist” who “was ready at once to take over anything in central Africa between the Zambezi and the White Nile.” His driving ambition was encapsulated in the slogan “Cape-to-Cairo” — the vision of an uninterrupted belt of British territory stretching the entire length of the African continent, from the Cape of Good Hope to Egypt, linked by railway and telegraph.

The idea appears to have been first put forward in 1876 by Sir Edwin Arnold. Rhodes made it his life’s work. As Langer records, “This group, of which Cecil Rhodes was the leading spirit, was wedded to the idea which later became familiar through the slogan Cape-to-Cairo, and which envisaged the ultimate connexion of British territory in south Africa with the new possessions in central Africa and Egypt.”

The British South Africa Company

Rhodes’s instrument for northern expansion was the British South Africa Company (the “Chartered Company”), which was granted a Royal Charter in the autumn of 1889. The charter gave the company “almost unlimited rights and powers of government in the huge area north of the Transvaal and west of the Portuguese possessions, without fixing any northern limit.” Rhodes used the company to acquire vast tracts of land, establish settlements, and impose British administration over the territories that would become Southern and Northern Rhodesia — named after him in what Justin McCarthy called “perhaps the most striking personal tribute ever paid to a British imperial figure during his own lifetime.”

The company’s operations were conducted with a military arm. It fought several wars of conquest against the Ndebele (Matabele) and the Shona peoples. McCarthy observed that “for a long time, all but unknown to the vast majority of Englishmen at home, the Chartered Company was writing out a new volume of history with a big pen, sometimes, it must be allowed, dipped in very red ink.”

Bechuanaland and the British Ultimatum to Portugal (1890)

The road to the north passed through Bechuanaland. As a member of the Cape ministry in 1884, Rhodes had urged the establishment of a British protectorate over Bechuanaland — the territory lying between the Transvaal and the newly proclaimed German colony of South-West Africa — to keep open “the road to trade and territorial expansion towards the north.” This protectorate was established in 1885, preserving the corridor northward.

British expansion also brought conflict with Portugal, which claimed a vast swathe of territory linking Angola on the west coast with Mozambique on the east — the so-called “rose-coloured map” that would have blocked British access from south to central Africa. In January 1890, the British government delivered an ultimatum to Portugal demanding the withdrawal of Portuguese forces from the disputed territory between the Anglo-Portuguese spheres in Central Africa — territory that would connect the British position in Nyasaland with the Chartered Company’s claims in the interior. Confronted by British naval power, Portugal was forced to capitulate, relinquishing its claims to the disputed region. The 1890 ultimatum was a landmark in British imperial supremacy in Africa.

The Rhodesian Settlement

Rhodes’s vision extended to the railway. The Cape-to-Cairo railway and telegraph line was to be the physical embodiment of British dominion. Rhodes planned to buy the Portuguese end of the Delagoa Bay railway line and even the whole of Portuguese territory south of the Zambezi, but these projects proved too ambitious. Nonetheless, the railway was pushed northwards from the Cape through Bechuanaland, with Mafeking as its temporary terminus.

Rhodes’s personal dominance of southern African affairs was checked by the Jameson Raid of 1895, which forced his resignation as Prime Minister of Cape Colony. But his territorial legacy endured: Rhodesia remained under Chartered Company administration until the 1920s, and the Cape-to-Cairo idea, though never fully realised, shaped British strategic thinking for a generation.

Anglo-German Agreements and Rivalries

Germany’s emergence as a colonial power in the 1880s introduced a new dimension to the Scramble for Africa. Bismarck, initially sceptical of colonial acquisition, had authorised the proclamation of German protectorates in South-West Africa (1884), Togoland and the Cameroons (1884), and German East Africa (1885). These acquisitions brought Germany into direct rivalry with British expansion, particularly in East Africa and South Africa.

The Heligoland-Zanzibar Treaty (1890)

The most important Anglo-German colonial agreement was the Heligoland-Zanzibar Treaty of 1 July 1890. The negotiations were driven by the acute rivalries that had developed in East Africa. Langer describes how “the rivalries of the English and Germans had become so acute that Bismarck had already taken up the question of a settlement.” By April 1890, the agitation on both sides had reached such dimensions that it threatened to impair good relations.

Lord Salisbury, the British Prime Minister, took the initiative. He concluded that the growing tension with Germany was dangerous and “would force us to change our systems of alliance in Europe.” He therefore decided “to offer the Germans the island of Heligoland in return for concessions in Africa.” He spoke of the island as “a mere sandbar which would eventually be washed away.” The naval authorities agreed that it could not be defended without heavy fortification. Some members of the cabinet objected, but Salisbury finally induced them to agree.

On the German side, the suggestion regarding Heligoland was received with great enthusiasm. Emperor William II and the new Chancellor Caprivi considered the island of great strategic importance — as a coaling station and protective outpost at the mouths of the German rivers, and to prevent its use by the French fleet in time of war.

Under the treaty, Germany recognised a British protectorate over Zanzibar (which Britain had long regarded as crucial to the suppression of the slave trade) and gave up claims to territory in East Africa north of a line from the southern tip of Lake Victoria to the northern tip of Lake Tanganyika. This ceded to Britain what would become Kenya and Uganda. Germany also renounced claims to the coast north of the Tana River. In return, Britain ceded the small North Sea island of Heligoland — of immense strategic value for the defence of the German coast — and recognised German claims to territory in East Africa south of the agreed line.

The agreement was well received in both countries: “in England because of the extensive gains made in Africa, in Germany because of the sentimental interest in Heligoland, which was inhabited by Germans,” Langer records. Politically, the treaty “checked the growing tension between the two nations.” But it was not without wider consequences: the apparent rapprochement of England and Germany “represented a danger for France as it did for Russia,” and contributed to the urgency with which the Franco-Russian Alliance was pursued.

French Objections and German Ambiguity

The French protested loudly against the Anglo-German Treaty, arguing that “the assumption by Britain of a protectorate over Zanzibar was a violation of an earlier treaty.” They demanded compensation either in Egypt or in Tunis. The British government was in a difficult position but refused to give way.

Anglo-German relations remained ambivalent. The Heligoland Treaty had eased tensions, but German policy in South Africa — particularly the encouragement of the Transvaal Republic and the Kruger Telegram after the Jameson Raid — kept the two powers at odds. In the later 1890s, Joseph Chamberlain’s abortive attempts to negotiate an Anglo-German alliance foundered on mutual suspicion, and the two powers drifted toward the naval rivalry that would define their relationship in the new century.

The Fashoda Crisis and Anglo-French Rivalry

The most dramatic crisis of the Scramble for Africa was the confrontation between Britain and France at Fashoda in 1898, which brought the two countries to the brink of war and resolved the long struggle for control of the Upper Nile.

The Struggle for the Nile

The Fashoda crisis was the culmination of a decade-long rivalry for the Nile Valley. Langer calls it “the great and dramatic contest that ended in 1898 with the Fashoda crisis.” The British position in Egypt, occupied since 1882, required control of the Nile waters. The French, smarting from their exclusion from Egypt, sought to establish a position on the Upper Nile that would enable them to challenge British dominance.

King Leopold II of Belgium, through the Congo Free State, also harboured ambitions on the Upper Nile. The Anglo-Congolese Treaty of May 1894 provided for the lease to Leopold of a considerable area along the west bank of the Upper Nile, extending from Lake Albert to Fashoda. This treaty aroused immediate protests from France and Germany and was ultimately abandoned.

The Marchand Mission

The French government decided upon a direct challenge. Captain Jean-Baptiste Marchand was dispatched with a small force of Senegalese troops to march across Africa from the French Congo to the Upper Nile. His instructions, dated February 1896, were to establish a French presence at Fashoda. The mission, supported by French activity in Abyssinia, was intended to force a reckoning with Britain over the Nile.

Marchand’s heroic journey across Africa took over two years. He reached Fashoda on 10 July 1898, raised the French flag, and established a small fort. Unknown to Marchand, however, the strategic situation had been transformed by Kitchener’s annihilation of the Mahdist army at Omdurman on 2 September 1898. Kitchener was now master of the Sudan.

The Confrontation

Kitchener, informed of Marchand’s presence, proceeded up the Nile with a flotilla of gunboats. He reached Fashoda on 19 September 1898. The two commanders met — Marchand with his small band of exhausted men, Kitchener with the overwhelming force of the Anglo-Egyptian army. The confrontation was conducted with impeccable courtesy but absolute firmness. Kitchener insisted that Marchand must evacuate; Marchand insisted that he had his orders from Paris.

For the next two months, the two governments argued the case. The British position, grounded in the principle of effective occupation and the Anglo-Egyptian claim to the Sudan (derived from Egypt’s historical sovereignty), was unyielding. The French public was outraged, but the French navy was no match for the Royal Navy. The French foreign minister, Théophile Delcassé, who had replaced the more combative Hanotaux, recognised that France could not fight. On 3 November 1898, the French government ordered Marchand to withdraw.

Consequences

The Fashoda crisis was a decisive moment in European diplomacy. It demonstrated British naval and imperial supremacy and confirmed the British position in the Nile Valley. The Sudan became an Anglo-Egyptian condominium under effective British control.

But Fashoda had the paradoxical effect of clearing the ground for a new relationship between Britain and France. The two powers, having faced the prospect of war and recoiled, began to resolve their colonial differences. The Anglo-French Agreement of 21 March 1899 delimited spheres of influence in Central Africa east of the Niger watershed. More significantly, the experience of the crisis contributed to the climate in which the Entente Cordiale of 1904 could be concluded.

The Entente Cordiale — a series of agreements signed on 8 April 1904 — resolved a range of colonial disputes between Britain and France. The most important provision concerned Egypt and Morocco: France recognised the British position in Egypt, and Britain recognised the French position in Morocco. The exclusion of Morocco from the British sphere was a notable feature of the Entente, reflecting the new spirit of compromise. The 1904 agreement, though not a military alliance, laid the foundation for Anglo-French co-operation in the decade before the Great War.

The Boer War and the Consolidation of British Africa

The Scramble for Africa reached its violent culmination in southern Africa with the Boer War of 1899–1902. The collision between British imperialism and Boer independence, personified in the rivalry between Cecil Rhodes and President Paul Kruger, resulted in the conquest and annexation of the Transvaal and Orange Free State. The war — the largest and most costly imperial conflict Britain had fought since the Napoleonic Wars — absorbed enormous military resources, provoked intense international criticism, and generated bitter domestic controversy in Britain.

The peace settlement under the Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902) incorporated the Boer republics into the British Empire, but with generous terms that promised eventual self-government. The Union of South Africa, established in 1910, brought together the Cape Colony, Natal, the Transvaal, and the Orange Free State under a single dominion government, completing the British consolidation of southern Africa.

Legacy

By 1914, the Scramble for Africa was essentially complete. The continent had been divided among the European powers with almost no regard for African political boundaries, ethnic distribution, or cultural coherence. The British Empire’s African possessions formed a vast arc from Egypt and the Sudan in the north-east, through Uganda, Kenya, and Nyasaland in the east, to Rhodesia, Bechuanaland, and South Africa in the south, and across to Nigeria, the Gold Coast, and Sierra Leone in the west.

The partition left a lasting legacy of arbitrary borders, colonial economic structures, and ethnic divisions that would shape the post-colonial history of Africa. The Berlin Conference’s principle of effective occupation — requiring demonstrable control rather than mere claim — had driven a frenzied expansion that transformed the political map of an entire continent in the space of a single generation. As Langer concluded, imperialism in this period was driven by the fact that “much of the globe was an English preserve and the British were not backward in advancing claims to such regions as were still unappropriated” — and the same could be said of the other European powers. The race for Africa was the most spectacular example of the imperial rivalries that characterised the closing decades of the nineteenth century.
