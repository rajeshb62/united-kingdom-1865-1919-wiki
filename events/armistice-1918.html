The Armistice (1918)

The armistice that ended the Great War on the Western Front was signed at 5 a.m. on Monday, 11 November 1918, and came into force at 11 a.m. French time the same day. It closed the continuous Allied advance that had run from Foch’s counter-stroke of 18 July and Rawlinson’s breakthrough of 8 August — the Allied counter-offensive that reversed the German Spring Offensive — and imposed terms which the Annual Register judged tantamount to complete surrender. Germany was defeated and surrendered; the surrender was not quite unconditional, being framed on President Wilson’s Fourteen Points with two Allied qualifications, but the German Government did not negotiate as equals. At home the signal was the sound of maroons over London; for the British army the last act of the war was the Canadian entry into Mons and, as the Register records, shots fired by King Edward’s Horse a few minutes before eleven. The Coalition under Lloyd George turned at once to demobilisation, Rhine occupation, and the December “coupon” election under the franchise of the Representation of the People Act.

Approach to Surrender

The capitulation of Bulgaria and Turkey, and the surrender of the Austro-Hungarian Government together with the dissolution of the Dual Monarchy, caused the German Government — now led by Prince Max of Baden — to decide to accept a peace on the lines of President Wilson’s Fourteen Points and to seek an armistice on that basis. The German Government inquired of the American Government whether the European Allies also agreed to accept the Fourteen Points as a basis of peace. On 4 November it was announced on behalf of Great Britain, France, and Italy that those Powers accepted the Fourteen Points subject to a reservation on the so-called “freedom of the seas” and to a second reservation on exacting reparation from Germany for damage done in invaded territory. With these two qualifications, therefore, President Wilson’s dicta were formally accepted by all parties as the basis of the future treaty of peace. This point having been settled, it only remained to arrange the armistice.

The terms were decided by the Associated Governments and communicated to Marshal Foch and Admiral Sir Rosslyn Wemyss, who were authorised to convey them to the Germans. The Register is explicit that the Allies could have enforced unconditional surrender within a few months, probably within a few weeks, but did not deem it necessary to incur the extra sacrifice of life, since the terms Germany was already willing to accept — the Wilson policy with the two qualifications — provided the foundation for a just and enduring peace.

The German Delegation and Foch’s Terms

A German delegation, headed by Herr Erzberger and General von Gündell, approached the French lines on 7 November. They were conducted through the French lines, and on the following day they formally applied for an armistice from Marshal Foch. The latter read out the terms which had been drawn up, and the Germans were given seventy-two hours in which to accept or reject them — the Chronicle of Events recording that the conditions were to be accepted by 11 a.m. on 11 November. Herr Erzberger declared himself astonished at their severity and asked to be allowed to communicate with the German headquarters, which were then at Spa. The member of the delegation used as courier, Captain Helldorf, was delayed on his journey to Spa by a misunderstanding on the part of the German High Command regarding arrangements for a cease-fire along the route he was to travel; hence the armistice was not actually signed until 5 a.m. on Monday, 11 November. It came into force at 11 a.m. (French time) on that day.

Before that hour Canadian troops captured the town of Mons, so that, from the British point of view, the war may be said to have ended at the spot where it began. In the meantime it was announced on 9 November that the Emperor William had abdicated. Prince Max of Baden issued a decree stating that the Kaiser had abdicated; Herr Ebert became Imperial Chancellor in place of Prince Max. On most parts of the front fighting continued during the six hours’ interval between the signing of the armistice and the moment at which it came into operation. It was said that the last shots of the war were fired by a troop of King Edward’s Horse, who were proceeding to capture a field-gun a few minutes before 11 o’clock. They shot two of the gun crew and were about to rush the gun when the officer in charge, watch in hand, stopped the operation — for it was 11 o’clock.

Terms: Military, Territorial, Naval

The terms of the armistice, given in full in the Register’s Public Documents, stipulated for the immediate evacuation of the invaded territories — Belgium, France, Luxemburg, and (the Register notes) Alsace-Lorraine — and for the surrender by the Germans of 5,000 guns (2,500 heavy and 2,500 field), 30,000 machine-guns, 3,000 flame-throwers, and 2,000 aeroplanes. Further, all Germany west of the Rhine was to be evacuated in thirty-one days, and the principal bridge-heads at Mainz, Coblenz, and Cologne were to be occupied by the Associated Powers. The naval conditions included the surrender of all submarines, fifty destroyers, eight light cruisers, six battle-cruisers, and ten battleships. The duration of the armistice was to be thirty-six days, but provision was made for possible (and probable) extension.

It will be seen that the terms imposed were tantamount to complete surrender. The victory was complete. Germany was defeated and surrendered. It was true, however, that the surrender was not quite unconditional. The German Government did not in any sense enter into negotiations as equals — they surrendered; but they surrendered not unconditionally, but to certain terms: President Wilson’s Fourteen Points, with two qualifications. Newbolt’s naval history underlines the same point from the sea: the naval clauses stripped Germany of the means of renewing submarine or surface war and transferred the High Seas Fleet into Allied custody pending the peace treaty — the consummation of command of the sea that had constrained Central Europe throughout the war.

11 November in London: Maroons and the Lord Mayor’s Banquet

At 11 in the morning on Monday, 11 November, London was stirred by the sound of maroons. It was the long-expected signal that Germany had signed the armistice terms. The announcement had been awaited all the previous week, and even as late as Saturday evening (9 November) at the Lord Mayor’s banquet Lloyd George, one of the principal speakers, commenced his speech by saying he had no news for them. The Chronicle records that at that same banquet the Prime Minister announced the abdication of the Kaiser. The Lord Mayor’s Show on 9 November had itself been a pageant of the nation’s war effort — naval and military detachments, Women’s Auxiliary Corps, tanks, captured guns, colonial and Allied troops — celebrated in accordance with time-honoured custom while the German plenipotentiaries were already inside the French lines.

The Register’s English History chapter “Peace” opens on the maroons and the public recognition that the long struggle was over. The same hours saw the last naval loss of the war recorded in the Chronicle: H.M.S. Britannia torpedoed in the western entrance to Gibraltar Straits on 9 November, thirty-nine officers and 673 men being saved. On the front, Maubeuge had fallen to the Guards and the 62nd Division on 8 November; Sedan had been occupied by the Americans on the 6th; Valenciennes by the British on the 3rd. Hostilities ceased on all fronts at 11 a.m. on the 11th.

Preceding Armistices: Bulgaria, Turkey, Austria-Hungary

The German armistice was the last of a chain. Bulgaria, after the Salonika offensive of mid-September and the French entry into Uskub on 29 September, applied to General Franchet d’Esperey; the armistice was granted on severe terms — immediate evacuation of Greek and Serbian territory, demobilisation, surrender of arms, munitions, vehicles, and horses, restitution of Greek Fourth Army Corps material, and the treatment as prisoners of Bulgarian troops of the Eleventh German Army north and west of Uskub. King Ferdinand abdicated and retired to Germany; the Crown Prince Boris became King.

Turkey followed after the fall of Bulgaria. In mid-October Taalat and Enver resigned; Izzet Pasha became Grand Vizier and immediately entered into negotiations for an armistice, utilising General Townshend (the hero of Kut) as intermediary in the preliminary negotiations. Vice-Admiral Calthorpe acted for the Allied Powers. The armistice was signed at Mudros on 30 October and came into operation the following day; its terms provided for the opening of the Dardanelles and the occupation of the Bosporus forts. Calthorpe was appointed British Commissioner at Constantinople.

Austria-Hungary signed an armistice with General Diaz on 3 November; it came into operation at 3 p.m. on 4 November. Italian troops landed at Trieste on the 3rd. Between 24 October and 4 November the Allied armies on the Italian front — including Lord Cavan’s mixed Italian–British Tenth Army on the Piave — had taken 300,000 prisoners and 5,000 guns. The Dual Monarchy dissolved as it surrendered.

Aftermath into 1919

The conditions of the armistice were carried out in a quiet and orderly manner. The British occupied Cologne and Bonn; the Americans were stationed in Coblenz; the French took over Mainz. The British occupied the furthest points — the Cologne bridge-head — on 12 December, the river being formally crossed that day; on the following day the Americans crossed the Rhine at Coblenz.

The German warships were surrendered in batches, the first ships leaving the German ports for that purpose on 20 November and the remainder following during the next few days. The submarines surrendered off Harwich. The surface warships surrendered in the Forth and were then sent to Scapa Flow, where they were to remain until the treaty of peace decided their final fate — the naval counterpart, in Newbolt’s account, of the military occupation of the Rhine bridge-heads.

After the termination of hostilities certain provisional statistics of casualties were published. The total British (including Dominion and Indian) military casualties were given provisionally as 3,050,000. The total of “killed” reported up to 10 November was 658,704, but many soldiers reported “missing” were no doubt killed. The Admiralty report of total casualties was stated to be complete: officers and men together, 39,766 (dead 33,361; wounded 5,183; missing 47; prisoners 1,175). These totals included a certain number of merchant seamen serving temporarily in a naval capacity. In addition, no fewer than 14,661 officers and men of the mercantile marine lost their lives through enemy action whilst following their ordinary vocations, and 3,295 were captured and held as prisoners of war. A provisional estimate of German casualties published in December stated that the dead numbered 1,600,000 and the total casualties 6,385,000, with wounded given as over 4,000,000.

Politically the Armistice opened the path to the Peace Conference and to the domestic settlement of 1919. The Coalition manifesto and the December election, fought on the enlarged register of the Representation of the People Act, translated military victory into a parliamentary majority pledged to “make Germany pay” and to reconstruct at home. The thirty-six-day armistice was extended as expected; the formal treaty of peace, and the fate of the fleet at Scapa, belonged to the following year. For Britain the war that had opened in 1914 closed under Haig’s armies on the old Mons ground, under Foch’s supreme command, and under a Coalition whose naval and economic grip — command of the sea — the Register and Newbolt alike treat as the condition of the land surrender of 11 November.
