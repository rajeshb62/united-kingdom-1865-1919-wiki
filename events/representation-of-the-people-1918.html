The Representation of the People Act 1918

The Representation of the People Act 1918 was the greatest single extension of the parliamentary franchise in British history. Shaped by a Speaker’s Conference on electoral reform, carried through the Commons in the critical stages of June and the autumn of 1917, and in force as statute in the early months of 1918, it wrote women into the electorate at the age of thirty, secured the vote of soldiers and sailors from the place of their home, redistributed seats in Great Britain toward the industrial and urban centres, and left Irish redistribution as the chief unresolved anomaly of the settlement. It closed the long Victorian and Edwardian sequence of parliamentary-reform that had run from 1832 through the Second and Third Reform Acts, and it did so under the pressure of a war that had made the old register, and the old exclusions, impossible to defend.

Wartime Register and the Problem of the Absent Voter

The need for a new franchise had become practical before it became comprehensive. In the summer of 1916 the question was agitated whether a General Election could or should be held during the war, and whether a new register could be prepared that would include the soldiers and sailors fighting abroad. The Government determined to appoint a select committee to inquire whether it was practicable and desirable to prepare such a register, with adequate representation of those engaged in the war and in war work, and to conduct an election upon it during the war.

On 19 July 1916 Herbert Samuel moved the appointment of that committee in the Commons. He laid special emphasis on the difficulties of the task — above all on how soldiers at the front would vote while under bombardment — and asked whether there was justification for embarking on an expenditure of £300,000 and the labour of 20,000 persons except in case of overwhelming necessity. Sir Edward Carson took him sharply to task for attacking the very proposal he was supposed to be advocating. The country must have a register, Carson insisted, since a General Election might be necessary at any moment; he branded the proposed Committee as a farce and called on the House to refuse it and compel the Government to do its own business. Many members took the same view. asquith-herbert-henry, hastily sent for, consulted with Samuel and other Ministers and acquiesced in the obvious feeling of the House that the motion should be withdrawn. The Government would reconsider the matter.

From that failure of a narrow, wartime-register expedient the path led toward a full reconstruction of the franchise. The instrument chosen was not a party bill but a Speaker’s Conference on Electoral Reform — a device of all-party composition under an impartial chairman that could produce a compromise acceptable enough to pass in wartime.

The Speaker’s Conference and the Bill of 1917

By the summer of 1917 the Franchise and Representation of the People measure, already shaped by the Speaker’s Conference, was advancing through its critical stages in the Commons. The war itself had altered the argument. Many members declared that the experiences of the conflict had shown that the conscious co-operation of women in the activities of the nation was a source of strength and not of weakness. Sir John Simon insisted that the industrial problem would be more than before a women’s problem, and that its solution could not, in any justice, be attempted by a Parliament with an exclusive male electorate.

The militant and constitutional campaigns for women’s suffrage had suspended their peacetime methods after the outbreak-of-the-great-war; the Bill now before the House was the parliamentary harvest of that suspension as much as of the long pre-war agitation for womens-suffrage. At the end of the sitting Sir Frederick Banbury’s amendment — the principal anti-suffrage challenge at that stage — was heavily defeated.

Women at Thirty: The Clause of 20 June

On the following day, 20 June 1917, the House considered the qualifications which women parliamentary electors must possess. The only substantial point that remained to be decided was the age-limit. The Bill had fixed it at thirty years. Many of the anti-suffragists tried to produce dissension among the suffragists, and an ultimate wrecking of the principle, by proposals to remove or lower the age-limit. Sir George Cave, who was in charge of the Bill, opposed the alteration and declared roundly that if any such amendment were accepted he could not be responsible for the further conduct of the measure. The amendment was rejected by an overwhelming vote. In the end the clause enfranchising women was added to the Bill by a vote of 214 members as against 17.

The age of thirty was thus not an accident of drafting but the price of carrying the principle. It kept the women’s electorate short of a majority of the whole, and it held together a wartime coalition of suffragists and cautious converts that a more sweeping clause might have broken. Women local-government electors and women who met a minimum property or marital qualification within the Bill’s scheme entered the parliamentary nation; the younger women who filled the munitions factories and the transport services would wait for a later Act.

Soldiers, Sailors, Paupers, and Conscience

The House then proceeded to a series of Government amendments securing the franchise to soldiers and sailors. Every soldier and sailor on active service and on full pay was to have the right to vote for the place of his home. The debate was resumed on the 25th, when it was proposed that the voting age for soldiers and sailors who had seen active service should be nineteen, on the argument that if a man was old enough to fight he was old enough to vote. The feeling in the House was so strong that the Government promised to reconsider the matter.

On the following day two further proposals of interest were made. The first was that the pauper disqualification should be removed, which the Government accepted — a quiet but significant breach in the old Victorian association of the franchise with rate-paying independence. The second was a proposal to disfranchise conscientious objectors. Mr. M’Neill, who moved it, declared that the objectors had sheltered themselves from the perils and sacrifices accepted by others, and that after the war they would enjoy immunities and liberties preserved to them by other men’s efforts: were they then to exercise the franchise when the peril was over? Lord Hugh Cecil made an eloquent speech for liberty of conscience, arguing that since conscientious objectors perversely but sincerely adhered to a higher law above the safety of the republic, it belonged to the credit of the country to respect their convictions; he hoped for the honour of the House and the nation that the amendment would be rejected. Sir John Simon also spoke against the proposal. The wartime franchise was thus drawn wide for the fighting man and, in the matter of conscience, refused the path of punitive exclusion.

Redistribution and the Irish Difficulty

Parliament reassembled on 16 October 1917. The main business of the autumn session was the Franchise Bill and a new Vote of Credit; progress was also expected with the Education Bill, so that franchise reform and the reconstruction of popular schooling moved forward together as complementary pieces of wartime social-reform. The Boundary Commissioners had already put forward their plans for redistribution. They proposed to add thirty-one members to the number representing Great Britain, and their scheme provided for a new distribution of political power by which the industrial and urban elements would gain at the expense of the agricultural and rural. Greater London was to have 101 members; Lancashire, sixty-six; Birmingham and its environs, forty-one; Glasgow and its environs, thirty-two. The map that had still carried the weight of the older county and small-borough system after 1885 was to be redrawn toward the cities that had made the nation’s war production.

When the Commons began its discussion of the Reform Bill in the autumn, it was confronted with an Irish difficulty. The Bill, in accordance with the recommendations of the Speaker’s Conference, applied the new franchises to Ireland but confined redistribution to Great Britain. Sir John Lonsdale, the Ulster Unionist leader, pointed out the resultant dilemma. The Bill, he declared, was based on a party compromise involving the accepted principles of “one man one vote” and “one vote one value,” but without redistribution in Ireland the second principle could not come into operation. He suggested a rearrangement of the Irish constituencies that would not prejudice any settlement which might result from the Irish Convention. John Redmond urged that a compromise made by the Speaker’s Conference ought not to be disturbed. Sir George Cave, for the Government, admitted that a strong case had been made out for consideration, and that if the House so desired the matter would receive further attention.

The Irish anomaly was not accidental. It stood at the intersection of the franchise settlement and the still-unresolved Home Rule question, which lloyd-george-david had in May offered to treat either by a limited application of the Home Rule Act with exclusion of north-east Ulster or by a Convention of Irishmen of both parties. Redistribution in Ireland would have prejudged representation under any future Irish settlement; leaving it untouched preserved the Speaker’s Conference bargain at the cost of “one vote one value” across the whole United Kingdom.

The Act in Force and the New Electorate

The year 1917, which had opened with unbroken determination to prosecute the war to victory, closed with the great Representation of the People measure still the central domestic business of Parliament — women already written into the franchise at the age of thirty, soldiers and sailors secured their vote from the place of their home, and Irish representation left as the chief unresolved anomaly of redistribution. Early in 1918 the measure stood as the Representation of the People Act. By 26 February, when the Labour Party met in resumed Annual Conference at the Central Hall, Westminster, the party adopted a new constitution transforming itself from a federation of societies into a national democratic political organisation open to every worker who laboured “by hand or by brain.” The change marked the determination of the party leaders to seize the opportunity given by the widening of the electorate and the enfranchisement of women under the Act, and to make the Labour movement the most powerful political force in the country — the electoral foundation of modern labour-representation completed at last by statute.

The Coalition manifesto issued as the nation turned from war toward reconstruction after the armistice-1918 declared it the duty of the new Government to remove all existing inequalities of the law as between men and women. The franchise Act had opened the gate; equality in the wider law remained a programme. The same manifesto treated housing, education, and the prevention of degrading standards of employment as conditions of social harmony which the Coalition would earnestly endeavour to promote — the new electorate was assumed to demand a new social agenda. In 1919 a Women’s Emancipation Bill appeared among the session’s measures, while the first General Election under the new register returned Labour candidates with nearly 2,500,000 votes — a measure of how far the enlarged electorate had already shifted the balance of party competition.

Place in the Sequence of Reform

The long sequence of parliamentary-reform that the Act completed ran in a single line through the century. The Reform Act of 1832 had transferred power from the landed aristocracy to the middle class while leaving the working class voteless. Disraeli’s “leap in the dark” of 1867 enfranchised male householders in the boroughs and raised the electorate from about one to two millions. Gladstone’s Act of 1884 gave household suffrage to the country districts and enfranchised the agricultural labourer, and the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885 followed on a basis of single-member equal electoral districts of some 54,000 inhabitants — with the enduring controversy over plural voting and “one man, one vote” still unresolved. Local government had already moved further: household suffrage in municipal elections, and the eligibility of women for town councils, school boards, and boards of guardians, had made the exclusion of women from the parliamentary franchise look increasingly anomalous even before the war.

The Act of 1918 completed that sequence under conditions none of the earlier reformers had imagined. It brought women within the parliamentary nation at thirty, gave the fighting services a vote that could be cast from the place of home, pushed redistribution toward the industrial cities, and left the Irish difficulty — as so often in the politics of irish-home-rule — only half resolved. The war that had compelled the Military Service Acts in 1916 compelled democracy in 1918.

Chronology

  • 1916, 19 July: Samuel moves a select committee on a wartime electoral register including soldiers; motion withdrawn after House opposition led by Carson.
  • 1917, May: Lloyd George’s Irish offer refers to a Conference “on the lines of the Speaker’s Conference on Electoral Reform.”
  • 1917, June: Critical Commons stages of the Franchise Bill; Banbury amendment heavily defeated.
  • 1917, 20 June: Women’s age-limit fixed at 30; clause enfranchising women carried by 214 to 17.
  • 1917, 25–26 June: Soldiers’ and sailors’ franchise; proposal of voting age 19 for men who had seen active service; removal of pauper disqualification accepted; debate on disfranchising conscientious objectors.
  • 1917, 16 October: Autumn session; Franchise Bill principal business; Boundary Commissioners’ scheme (+31 seats for Great Britain; London 101, Lancashire 66, Birmingham 41, Glasgow 32).
  • 1917, autumn: Irish difficulty — new franchises applied to Ireland, redistribution confined to Great Britain; Lonsdale, Redmond, and Cave.
  • 1917, end of year: Women at thirty, soldiers’ and sailors’ votes, and Irish redistribution unresolved, as the measure’s outstanding features.
  • 1918, early: Representation of the People Act in force.
  • 1918, 26 February: Labour Party adopts new constitution to exploit the widened electorate and women’s enfranchisement.
  • 1918–19: Coalition programme pledges removal of legal inequalities between men and women; first election under the new register; Women’s Emancipation Bill in the 1919 session.
