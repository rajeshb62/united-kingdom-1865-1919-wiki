The National Insurance Act 1911

The National Insurance Act of 1911 was the second great pillar of Liberal social-reform, following the non-contributory old-age-pensions-1908 and the financial and constitutional settlement of the peoples-budget-1911. Introduced by lloyd-george-david as Chancellor of the Exchequer while the Parliament Bill still occupied the centre of the session, it established compulsory insurance against sickness and invalidity for the mass of employed persons, and insurance against unemployment in selected trades. Contributions were shared among worker, employer, and the State; benefits were delivered largely through approved friendly societies and, for those outside societies, through a provisional Post Office scheme. The Act became law in the autumn of 1911 and came into force on “Joy Day,” 15 July 1912, amid medical resistance, Unionist obstruction, and the concurrent drama of the great-labour-unrest-1911-1914.

From Pensions to Insurance

Asquith had already, at Glasgow in April 1909, sketched labour exchanges, the reduction of boy labour, and unemployment insurance as “the next stage of social reform after old-age pensions and debt reduction.” Lloyd-George’s visit to Germany in August 1908 to study workmen’s insurance had given the Chancellor a continental model of contributory provision that the non-contributory pension scheme of 1908 had deliberately rejected. The constitutional crisis of 1909–11 delayed legislation; once the Parliament Act had broken the absolute veto of the house-of-lords, the Government could press social measures that the Upper House could no longer kill outright.

When george-v opened his first Parliament, the Speech from the Throne promised, as the only Bills specifically named, measures removing the pauper disqualification for old-age pensions and providing “insurance against sickness, invalidity, and (in special trades) unemployment.” The National Insurance Bill was brought forward as the principal social measure of the session. From the outset its details were closely scrutinised by friendly societies, the Labour party, and employers. Concurrently the Parliament Bill ground through Committee; the Coronation of 22 June suspended party strife for a few days; and industrial unrest on the docks, railways, and in transport trades rose toward the August climax.

Structure of the Bill: Health and Unemployment

The Bill had two main parts. The health (or national health insurance) part covered sickness, invalidity, maternity, and — through sanatorium benefit — consumption. The unemployment part applied only to special trades. Administration was to rest on approved societies — predominantly the great friendly societies — with a residual “Post Office” contributor class for those who did not join a society. Separate Commissioners were provided for Ireland, Scotland, and Wales. Local Health Committees, later renamed Insurance Committees, were given enlarged numbers and powers.

Committee stage was, in the Register’s phrase, “kaleidoscopic,” though most of the hundreds of amendments were verbal or consequential. The chief substantive changes fixed the shape of the Act as it left Parliament. Sick pay was set at 10s. per week for men and 7s. 6d. for women, and was to continue for twenty-six weeks instead of the original thirteen. Maternity benefit was extended to the wife of an insured person even if she herself was insured. Sanatorium benefit for consumption might be extended to the dependants of the insured consumptive. Married women might come in during their husband’s lifetime as voluntary contributors with reduced payments, receiving sickness and disablement benefit. Soldiers, sailors, naval men, and mercantile seamen might come in under special provisions; so might fishermen co-partners under regulations adapted to local custom.

Where it was the custom for the employer to pay full wages during sickness — especially for agricultural labourers and clerks — contributions of both employer and employed might be reduced, and sickness benefit likewise, if the employer agreed to pay wages for six weeks of illness. Persons earning less than 9s. a week paid no contribution; those between 9s. and 12s. paid 1d. per week less than under the original Bill — a provision of particular importance to women outworkers and to Scottish rural and industrial populations. The Post Office part of the Bill was made distinctly provisional. For Ireland the employer’s contribution was reduced to 2½d. a week; migratory labourers and outworkers whose work was done in leisure time were exempted; medical benefit was withdrawn in view of the existing dispensary system; and the constitution of Health Committees was adapted to Irish conditions.

Labour Criticism and the Weakest Classes

Labour opposition was not to insurance in principle but to the Bill’s treatment of the weakest. Broadly, the measure was thought to do too little for casuals, sweated workers, “poor lives,” and the strugglers generally. On 17 July 1911 attempts were vainly made to extend full medical benefit to persons entitled to maternity benefit; to extend maternity benefit to unmarried mothers (Keir Hardie’s proposal, defeated by 207 to 95); and to make sick pay run from the first day of illness — as the friendly societies desired — instead of from the fourth day as the Bill provided. The next day Lloyd-George appealed to members not to seek popularity by voting away safeguards they knew to be proper. On the day following, Lees Smith (Liberal, Northampton) moved to exempt insured persons who had fallen into arrears through unemployment from having to pay up the employer’s arrears as well as their own before recovering benefits. The amendment was defeated only by 163 to 116, after the Chancellor warned that its success might involve the defeat of the Bill. The arrears clause as amended was carried by 210 to 77. Rejection of these amendments intensified Labour denunciations; at pending bye-elections Unionists made much of the Bill’s defects.

Yet when the great strikes of August had estranged the Labour party and some advanced Liberals from the Ministry, the Register recorded that the Government had “gained in popularity, apparently, by the Insurance Bill.” Firmness against industrial paralysis had mitigated Unionist hostility; the social measure remained a popular asset.

Passage, Misrepresentation, and Medical Resistance

The Bill completed its progress in the autumn of 1911. Hundreds of amendments had been absorbed; separate national Commissioners were in place; Irish medical benefit had been withdrawn; low-wage contribution relief had been written in. The Act received the Royal Assent as the session closed. Implementation, however, was another battle. Through the winter of 1911–12 and the spring of 1912, Unionists attacked the measure in Parliament and misrepresented it in the country; a Liberal Insurance Committee was formed to answer those misrepresentations. In the Address debate of February 1912, Asquith asked whether Mr. Bonar Law, if he became Prime Minister, would repeal the Act. Bonar Law answered “Certainly” — then wrote to the press that he meant repeal only if Unionists took office before the Act became operative; otherwise they would amend it drastically. Ministers treated the admission as a gift.

Medical resistance was the most serious practical obstacle. Doctors objected to the terms of service under the panel system. On Joy Day itself Lloyd-George told a great meeting at the Kennington Theatre that six shillings per member had been set aside for the doctors, who would have an interest in keeping patients well and no bad debts; if they would not come to terms, the £4,000,000 set aside for them would go back to the members, and actuarial report showed that large additional benefits could then be given. The doctors asked for 11s. per member — an extra £4,000,000 a year, or roughly 1d. on the income tax and ¾d. on tea. Sir William Plender’s report showed that in Dundee doctors’ incomes worked out at 4s. 2d. per head, including guinea patients. The Chancellor was not afraid of the “squalid resistance” to paying 3d. a week that opponents encouraged, but he warned that the beginning of law-breaking was full of menace.

Joy Day and the Working of the Act

“Joy Day,” 15 July 1912, was the day the Act came into force. Through hot weather, or through the efforts of opponents, it was not extensively celebrated; but Lloyd-George at Kennington worked the meeting to high enthusiasm. Of 13,000,000 persons compulsorily insurable, 9,500,000 had already joined approved societies; in three weeks or a month, he predicted, there would be 12,000,000, and 500,000 Post Office contributors would be in societies as well. Friendly societies, previously at a standstill and hard put to keep solvent, would start solvent with a margin; those joining were making additional payments for higher benefits. Critics said the Government could not deliver the benefits — “wait and see.” The money for the sanatoria was there, but “the Act was not an Aladdin’s lamp”; already eleven tuberculosis doctors had been appointed for the whole of Wales out of fifty-seven applicants.

By late autumn Lloyd-George was claiming at Kirkcaldy that the Insurance Act was “working like clockwork.” At Port Sunlight, Asquith linked Old Age Pensions and Insurance as benefits secured under the Parliament Act, which Ministers would have betrayed their trust not to use for the declared wishes of the electorate. The Act thus closed the legislative arc opened by the 1906 majority: from non-contributory pensions financed by the People’s Budget, through the curbing of the Lords, to compulsory contributory insurance administered by the voluntary societies of the working class itself.

Aftermath and Significance

National Insurance did not end industrial unrest; the coal strike of March 1912 and the transport strike of the early summer ran alongside the Act’s first months. It did not satisfy Labour critics who wanted more for casuals and sweated workers. It did not immediately reconcile the doctors. But it created a permanent machinery of contributions, approved societies, Insurance Committees, and State subsidy that transformed the relationship between the British State and the wage-earning population. Clayton’s contemporary survey of democracy already treated the labour legislation of the previous twenty years — including pensions and the wider social programme of which insurance was the climax — as proof that parliamentary representation could deliver material gains to the working class.

Belloc, in The Servile State, treated the Act as the registration of the wage-earning class. Compulsion attached to employment below a certain income, or to “a vulgar fellow working with his hands,” not to the citizen as citizen; the educated and the economically free were excluded. The duty of seeing the law obeyed was laid on the Capitalist class, who gathered what he called a Poll Tax and completed the map of name, address, illness, and supposed malingering. Labour exchanges already recorded the worker, his refusals of service, and his “tendencies to rebellion against Capital.” Insurance money, once compelled, was not even in a society he could control: refuse work at a stated wage and “you certainly shall not have a right to the money you have been compelled to put aside.” For Belloc the Act followed “in every particular the lines” of the servile-state — security purchased by a change of status, not by restoration of property.

Politically, the Act was Lloyd-George’s personal monument within the Asquith ministry: a measure he had prepared since the German journey of 1908, defended through the constitutional crisis, forced through Committee against Labour amendments and Unionist hostility, and launched on Joy Day against medical and partisan resistance. It completed, with old-age pensions, the Edwardian experiment in using Free-Trade finance and a reformed parliamentary constitution for social-reform — even as the great-labour-unrest-1911-1914 and the irish-home-rule-crisis tested how far statute could contain the conflicts of an industrial and multi-national kingdom.
