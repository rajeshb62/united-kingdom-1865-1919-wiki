The Second Reform Act (1867)

The Reform Act of 1867 — commonly called the Second Reform Act — was the most dramatic volte-face in nineteenth-century British politics. A Conservative government that had helped defeat a mild Liberal reform bill in 1866 proceeded, in the session of 1867, to carry a measure far more democratic than anything the Liberals had proposed. The bill introduced household suffrage in the boroughs, nearly doubling the electorate, and prompted Lord Derby to describe his own policy as a “leap in the dark.”

The Fall of the Russell-Gladstone Reform Bill (1866)

The immediate background to the Act lay in the failure of Lord Russell’s Liberal government. In 1866, Russell and Mr. Gladstone introduced a Reform Bill of modest scope. Gladstone stumped the country during the Easter recess, declaring at Liverpool that the government had “passed the Rubicon; had broken the bridge and burned the boats behind them.” Yet the measure commanded little public enthusiasm. Conservatives opposed all reform on principle; many Whigs and half-hearted Liberals drifted away. The hero of the anti-reform opposition was Mr. Robert Lowe, a Liberal who attacked the bill with withering sarcasm. Although Lowe had few physical gifts as an orator — a harsh voice, ungainly presence, poor eyesight — his classical allusions and epigrammatic invective delighted the Tory benches. Conservatives made an idol of him, even declaring him the greatest statesman of the day. Mr. Disraeli, by contrast, was thrown into the shade and listened quietly, regarding Lowe’s prominence as a passing episode. The bill was defeated, the Liberal government fell, and Lord Derby formed his third Conservative ministry. (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. LII)

Disraeli’s “Ten Minutes’ Bill”

When Parliament reassembled in February 1867, the Queen’s Speech promised a Reform Bill. Lord Derby was frank about the risks: he called the undertaking a “leap in the dark” — a phrase originally used by Lord Cranbourne (the future Lord Salisbury) as a taunt, which Derby adopted as his own. The Conservative Cabinet had prepared two measures: a “comprehensive” bill and a more modest “second-class” bill held in reserve. On Saturday, 23 February, the Cabinet seemed in harmony on the comprehensive measure. But on Sunday, Lord Cranbourne examined the figures and concluded that the comprehensive bill would amount to household suffrage in many boroughs. He tendered his resignation, threatening to bring others with him. On Monday, 25 February, the Cabinet was hastily summoned. With only ten minutes before Disraeli had to address the House, the comprehensive bill was shelved and the “second-class” bill substituted. Sir John Pakington, who later explained the affair at Droitwich, gave the measure its enduring name: the “Ten Minutes’ Bill.”

The Ten Minutes’ Bill was received with universal discouragement. Disraeli saw at once that it could not pass. On 26 February he withdrew it and announced that the government would bring in a genuine measure on 18 March. At this, General Peel, Lord Carnarvon, and Lord Cranbourne resigned. Cranbourne explained that he objected to a bill that would leave political power divided between rich and poor, destroying the influence of the intervening classes. For the second time in ten years, a Conservative Cabinet had split on the question of reform and the borough franchise. (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. LII; Annual Register, 1903, Obituary of Lord Salisbury)

The March Bill and the Progress of Household Suffrage

The bill introduced on March 18 proposed that in boroughs all who paid rates, or twenty shillings a year in direct taxation, should have the vote, along with various “fancy franchises” for property-holders, savings-bank depositors, and the educated. Mr. Bright compared the scheme to “offering something with one hand and quietly withdrawing it with the other.” The government, however, had resolved to pass some Reform Bill that session at any cost. Lord Derby frankly admitted that he saw no reason why a monopoly of reform should be left to the Liberals, and Disraeli was determined not to go out of office on a Reform Bill a second time.

One by one the checks and balances were abandoned. The dual vote was laughed out of the bill. The voting-paper principle was dropped. The fancy franchises were swept away. A lodger franchise was introduced. At each stage Disraeli would threaten to drop the bill if an amendment were carried — and when the amendment was carried, he would announce that the government had changed its mind and would accept it. The House came to treat this as mere ceremonial.

The crucial struggle turned on the compound householder — the occupier of a small house whose landlord compounded for the rates. The bill as framed excluded compound householders from the franchise. Mr. Gladstone proposed a compromise: a line below which houses should not be rated at all, but every rated occupier should vote regardless of who paid the rates. He and Bright feared carrying the vote too low, to what Bright called “the residuum of the constituency.” But a group of advanced Liberals — known as the “Tea-Room Party” after the meeting where they resolved their course — refused to support Gladstone’s amendment. They believed that if pressed, the government would yield to anything rather than see the bill defeated. They were proved right: the government undertook to abolish the compounding system entirely, place every occupier’s name on the rate-book, and give every occupier the vote — household suffrage pure and simple in the boroughs. “The Tea-Room Party,” McCarthy writes, “had conquered both ways. They had prevailed against Mr. Gladstone, and prevailed over Mr. Disraeli.” (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. LII)

Redistribution and Final Provisions

The bill passed its final stage on 15 August 1867. Its principal provisions were:

  • Borough franchise: All male householders rated for the relief of the poor, and all lodgers resident for one year paying not less than £10 a year rent.
  • County franchise: Persons of property of the clear annual value of £5, and occupiers of lands or tenements paying £12 a year.
  • Redistribution: Eleven small boroughs were disfranchised; thirty-five boroughs with fewer than 10,000 inhabitants were reduced to single-member constituencies. New seats were created for Chelsea, Hackney, Leeds, Liverpool, Manchester, Salford, Glasgow, Birmingham, Dundee, and Merthyr Tydfil. Manchester, Liverpool, Birmingham, and Leeds each received a third member. The University of London was given a representative.
  • Minority representation: In constituencies returning three members, no elector could vote for more than two candidates (the Lord Cairns amendment, carried in the Lords against the government’s wishes). In the City of London, with four members, each elector could vote for only three.
  • Incidental reforms: Parliament was no longer required to dissolve on the death of the sovereign; members holding offices of profit from the Crown no longer needed to vacate their seats on moving from one department to another.

The Irish and Scotch Reform Bills were deferred to the following year. The Scotch Bill established a borough franchise equivalent to England’s and a county franchise based on £5 property or £14 occupation, with some new seats created by disfranchising small English boroughs. The Irish Bill merely reduced the borough franchise from £8 to £4 and did nothing on redistribution. (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. LII; Clayton, The Rise of the Democracy, ch. “Household Suffrage”)

The “Leap in the Dark”

Lord Cranbourne had first used the phrase “leap in the dark” to taunt the government. Lord Derby adopted it as an honest description of his policy. McCarthy observes that while the government acted sagaciously in settling the question decisively, “one is still lost in wonder at the boldness, the audacity with which the Conservative government threw away in succession every principle which they had just been proclaiming essential to Conservatism and put on Radicalism as a garment.” Disraeli, he notes, had said of Peel that he “caught the Whigs bathing and walked away with their clothes”; now Disraeli had “helped to turn the Whigs out of their clothes in order that he might get into them.”

Lord Cranbourne denounced the surrender with great severity from his place in the Commons and anonymously in the Quarterly Review. Yet he did not lose the confidence of his party; Disraeli admired his invective, and they were soon drawn together by common opposition to Gladstone. (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. LII; Annual Register, 1903)

Hyde Park Disturbances

While the bill was passing through its stages, the Reform League convened a public meeting in Hyde Park on 6 May. Mr. Walpole, the Home Secretary, issued a proclamation forbidding it, but the League took legal advice and found the government had no power to prohibit a meeting in advance. The meeting was held peacefully, watched by police and soldiers. However, on an earlier occasion the park railings had been torn down by a crowd that surged in when the gates were closed — the Hyde Park “riot” that convinced the government of the necessity of reform. Walpole, worn out by the thankless troubles of his office, resigned as Home Secretary (though he retained his seat in the Cabinet) and was succeeded by Mr. Gathorne Hardy. (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. LII; Clayton, The Rise of the Democracy, ch. “Household Suffrage”)

Impact on Party Politics

The Act’s passage was a masterstroke of Conservative party strategy — what contemporaries called “dishing the Whigs.” The Conservative party, which had in opposition helped defeat Gladstone’s moderate bill, now carried a more radical one. As the Diamond Jubilee volume of McCarthy’s history observes, this was characteristic of Disraeli’s method: he would carry to success measures that the Liberals had been unable to pass, and thereby claim the credit for reform.

The first general election under the new franchise came in 1868. The results confounded expectations. The working-men’s candidates — Mr. Ernest Jones, Mr. Beales, Mr. Odger, Mr. Bradlaugh — all failed. “Either our new masters were not so powerful as they were expected to prove,” McCarthy writes, “or they were very much like our old masters in their taste for representation.” The new House of Commons had a Liberal majority of about 120, larger than the majority of 60 in the previous Parliament. Mr. Gladstone thus had a commanding position for his programme of reform, beginning with the disestablishment of the Irish Church. (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. “The Irish Church”)

The Act fundamentally altered the composition of the electorate, increasing it from about 1,000,000 to 2,000,000. The town workman was enfranchised, as the middle-class man had been by the Act of 1832. Mr. Lowe, the great opponent of reform, was elected as the first representative of the University of London — a constituency created by the Act he had fought — and is remembered for his remark: “We must now educate our new masters.” (McCarthy, History of Our Own Times, Vol. IV, ch. LII; Clayton, Rise of the Democracy)

Chronology

  • 1866, June: Defeat of Gladstone’s Reform Bill; fall of Russell government; Derby forms his third ministry.
  • 1866, autumn–winter: Widespread reform demonstrations in large towns; Hyde Park railings torn down.
  • 1867, February: Queen’s Speech promises Reform Bill.
  • 1867, 23 February: Cabinet meets in seeming harmony on comprehensive bill.
  • 1867, 25 February: Cabinet crisis; “Ten Minutes’ Bill” introduced.
  • 1867, 26 February: Ten Minutes’ Bill withdrawn; comprehensive bill promised for 18 March.
  • 1867, March: Resignation of Cranbourne, Carnarvon, and General Peel.
  • 1867, 18 March: Disraeli introduces the comprehensive Reform Bill.
  • 1867, May–July: Clause-by-clause fight; abolition of compounding system; household suffrage established.
  • 1867, 6 May: Reform League meeting in Hyde Park.
  • 1867, 15 August: Reform Bill passes its final stage.
  • 1868: Irish and Scotch Reform Bills passed.
  • 1868, November–December: First general election under new franchise; Liberal victory under Gladstone.
