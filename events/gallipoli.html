The Gallipoli Campaign (1915–1916)

The attempt to force the Dardanelles and open a way to Constantinople was the most ambitious combined operation undertaken by the British Empire in the first year and a half of the Great War. Conceived, after the Dogger Bank action, as a naval stroke that might knock the Ottoman Empire out of the conflict, relieve Russia, and fix Bulgaria’s attitude, it became a prolonged campaign on the Gallipoli Peninsula — bombardments in February and March 1915, the landings of 25 April, the battles for Krithia, the Suvla Bay effort in August, and, at the year’s end, an evacuation of extraordinary difficulty executed with almost incredible success. The enterprise tested British command of the sea in a secondary theatre, strained the Liberal ministry into the first Coalition, and closed without the strategic prize for which it had been opened.

Decision: After Dogger Bank

It was on 28 January 1915, four days after the Dogger Bank action, that the final decision to attack the Dardanelles was taken. Although Vice-Admiral Carden had been informed that his plan was accepted, the matter was still far from settled. Events had been tending toward Salonica; Greece’s reply of 27 January required Bulgarian co-operation or, failing that, Roumania’s, and if Bulgarian neutrality could not be assured, two Entente army corps in addition. Under those conditions nothing could be done from Salonica for the present, and opinion solidified in favour of the Dardanelles as the quickest way of fixing Bulgaria’s attitude. Russia was particularly eager after the heavy defeat of the Turks at Ardahan and the annihilation of their Vth Corps at Sarakamuish.

Serious opposition came from the best British naval opinion, with Lord Fisher at its head: a purely naval attack on a strongly fortified base was a departure from established doctrine, and once only had such an attempt succeeded, when Rooke seized Gibraltar by coup de main. The First Lord’s relative-strength memorandum of 27 January argued that superiority was sufficient for a subsidiary operation without prejudicing North Sea command. When the War Council met on the 28th the Board was not in a position to give a firm opinion; all that was clear was the political necessity for action and the decisive advantages success would bring. For Lord Kitchener the Dardanelles was essentially diversionary — the view that appealed to the French, who promised four battleships and a minesweeper flotilla under the British Admiral. Augagneur declared that the plan permitted operations to be broken off at any stage without loss of prestige.

Opening of the Naval Attack

Carden had been pushing preparations without interruption except for weather. All January except three days it had blown a gale from the south-west; February brought gales from the north-east. Since 15 January no active operation had been undertaken; that day the French submarine Saphir, against strict orders, attempted to repeat B 11’s exploit, ran aground inside the Straits and was lost. The magnitude of the force required two additional flag officers: Rear-Admiral Rosslyn Wemyss to take charge of the Allied base at Mudros, and Rear-Admiral de Robeck as second-in-command. Commodore Keyes became Chief of Staff. Carden’s flag flew in Inflexible, still bearing Falkland scars; learning that Queen Elizabeth’s accident greatly reduced her speed and that Goeben was active again, he was allowed to keep Inflexible as the only ship able to bring Goeben to action.

Ships gathered from home and from distant commerce-protection stations freed by the Falklands and the destruction of the Emden, together with four French battleships under Guépratte, bringing the Allied fleet to sixteen capital ships. Command presented difficulty under the Convention of 6 August 1914, which gave France general direction in the Mediterranean; the French were willing that Dardanelles operations should be under the British Admiral but wished to take over the Syrian coast as far south as Jaffa, and on those lines the matter was amicably adjusted.

The first bombardment opened on 19 February against Helles, Orkanie, Kum Kale and Sedd el Bahr — the first test of whether long-range naval gunfire could silence modernised works without an observing station ashore. By the 25th four ships working in pairs ran up to the jaws of the Straits, engaging the outer forts with secondary armament down to 3,000 yards, aiming “to destroy each individual gun by a direct hit.” De Robeck ran with Vengeance and Cornwallis; Guépratte with Suffren and Charlemagne. Agamemnon was hit seven times with armour-piercing shell in ten minutes — three killed, five seriously wounded — yet nothing that could not be put right. Queen Elizabeth got direct hits on Helles, one dismounting a 9.4-inch; by noon not one of the forts was firing. After 3.0 the minesweeping trawlers closed the entrance. The outer defences were practically reduced.

The Narrows, 18 March

While officers on the spot were beginning to realise the full measure of the enterprise, elsewhere the initial operations were taken to promise success. Venizelos was trying to induce the King of Greece to offer his fleet and one infantry division; Bulgaria began to trim her sails; on 4 March Italy made her first approaches for joining the Allies. Russia replied on 3 March that a whole army corps was being prepared at Odessa and Batum within a fortnight or three weeks of the forcing of the Dardanelles. Kitchener’s despatch to Birdwood on 4 March still held that the fleet was to break through alone — expected in the Sea of Marmara by the 20th — with over 60,000 men concentrated at Lemnos by then. Till large-scale military assistance was certain, troops were not to be used in the Gallipoli peninsula at all; the real objective was Constantinople. Birdwood again pointed out little chance of the fleet getting through alone.

By the operation orders completed before Carden had to resign, the fleet had been reorganised in three divisions under de Robeck, Hayes-Sadler, and Guépratte. The General Idea was to silence the defences of the Narrows and of the minefields simultaneously — not expecting destruction of forts at 8,000 yards, but hoping to dominate them sufficiently to prevent interference with sweeping. Dawn of the 18th came with warm southerly breeze and cloudless sky. At 8.15 the signal to carry on flew from the flagship; at 10.30 Agamemnon led the First Division in. The first half-hour gave good promise — little or no reply from the forts, though barrage fire increased. Then the undetected minefield in Eren Keui Bay sank Bouvet, Irresistible and Ocean, crippled Inflexible and Gaulois, and forced the abandonment of the purely naval attack — the change of plan that committed the army to the peninsula.

Landings of 25 April

Since the army had been sent out merely to make good what the navy had won, it had to concentrate in Egypt for reorganisation. Civilian-manned trawlers had proved unfit for minesweeping under fire, so the flotilla was reorganised with naval ratings; beach-gear and a landing flotilla had to be improvised. Air reconnaissances confirmed that damage to the Narrows forts was slight and proved new works covering the vital Kephez minefields. Organisation of the combined attack went forward under Sir Ian Hamilton.

It was not till midday on 23 April — a full five weeks since the attack on the Narrows — that the weather began to give promise of the fine spell essential for the operation. De Robeck gave the word for preliminary movements about sundown. At least 200 vessels — over sixty British transports, over thirty trawlers, and well over ninety ships of war — moved in confined waters without mutual interference. On the 24th Hamilton transferred headquarters from Arcadian to Queen Elizabeth. Shortly after midnight the sea was perfectly smooth; in brilliant moonlight Wemyss transferred the three leading battalions of the main landing to Euryalus, Implacable and Cornwallis without a hitch. By 4.30 a.m. on the 25th the Royal Naval Division was off Xeros Island; Dartmouth and Doris bombarded the Bulair lines while trawlers swept Bakla Bay as though a landing were intended. The feint’s effect on Liman von Sanders was immediate: he moved the VIIth Division toward Bulair and for over twenty-four hours had his post of command near the central fort of the Bulair lines — leaving the Gaba Tepe–Helles landings to develop against the IXth Division’s coast cordon and the incomplete XIXth Division in reserve at Boghali, while Australian and New Zealand troops went ashore at Anzac Cove and the XXIXth Division at the Helles beaches. The River Clyde carried troops into V Beach; Y Beach was seized by the King’s Own Scottish Borderers and Plymouth Marines.

At Anzac on the 26th the troops had dug themselves in; for two or three hours the situation remained critical, but fire from the sea, which prisoners afterwards described as appalling, made organised assault almost impossible. By 9.0 a.m. Queen Elizabeth had knocked out the most annoying battery and the Turkish attack died away exhausted. At the southern beaches progress had been slight. Hunter-Weston reported the XXIXth Division not strong enough without further assistance to take Hill 141 dominating Sedd el Bahr. Y Beach, so cleverly seized and so vital to the hoped-for envelopment, was abandoned after night attacks from Krithia and a serious misunderstanding as to re-embarkation orders — the failure of envelopment; the initial advance was already hardening into trench warfare.

Krithia and the Submarine Threat

When news reached home of how far short the first effort had fallen of the coup de main planned against Achi Baba, it became clear the enterprise could not be carried on without greater military force. Nothing could be moved from France or Home — Egypt was the only source. On 27 April Kitchener told Hamilton that Maxwell would send anything asked for; embarkation of the East Lancashire Territorial Division and over 3,000 Anzac reinforcements commenced on 1 May. Attacks on Krithia and Achi Baba, supported by naval bombardments, marked the spring fighting; the Third Battle of Krithia in early June closed a phase in which the army’s inability to break the Ottoman defence had extended to the squadron itself.

It was not only reduced in strength: the long-expected hour had come when it could no longer claim a full working command of the Aegean. Queen Elizabeth had gone; under the Italian Convention Rear-Admiral Thursby left with four battleships, joining the Italian fleet at Taranto on 27 May. Liman von Sanders combined the first effort of the German submarines with a desperate attack to drive the Anzacs into the sea on 19 May; the supporting ships kept up continuous fire, and though the Turks had never fought with greater determination they had to desist with nothing gained and losses so great that they asked for an armistice to bury the dead. After 19 May the Turkish War Office officially stated that the British defences at Anzac were too strong to enable anything to be effected without heavy artillery. Lieutenant-Commander E. C. Boyle in E 14 and later E 11 carried the war into the Sea of Marmara; the supporting battleships Triumph and Majestic were lost to submarine attack — a sharp reminder that the Aegean was no longer a protected anchorage.

Suvla

After over three months’ campaigning in a strictly limited area a strategical surprise was to be attempted — not at a distance, but at a point within the area, half a day’s march from the enemy’s reserves. In the dark the troops must start and in the dark they must be put ashore, on a beach that it had been impossible to survey adequately, and by a force exceeding in number anything that had been attempted before. A period of moonless nights was essential; the new moon was on 10 August and the attack was fixed for the night of 6–7 August. As a preliminary step from 7,000 to 8,000 Anzac drafts were got ashore during the nights following 31 July, together with forty guns and extra transport, without being detected. Five brigades to reinforce the Anzac Corps were landed secretly from Mudros and hidden in dug-outs constructed by General Birdwood.

The original intention, as agreed between the Admiral and the General, was to land the whole force on the Nibrunesi beach, outside and just south of the bay — so “steep-to” that destroyers could run in with their bows almost touching. So it stood in the operation orders which Hamilton issued to Lieutenant-General Sir F. W. Stopford on 29 July as commander of the new IXth Corps: the primary object was “to secure Suvla Bay as a base for all the forces operating in the northern zone”; if he found this could be done without using the whole of his force, he was to assist Birdwood’s attack by an advance on Biyuk Anafarta. Stopford came to the conclusion that the bay could not be secured without seizing Anafarta Ridge, requiring two brigades inside Suvla Bay. De Robeck and his staff saw serious objections: the old chart was not to be relied on — the eastern shore had apparently silted up and the north shore was foul with reefs. Under these conditions of uncertainty they could not undertake to land two brigades at once. The soldiers still insisted that Karakol ridge at least must be seized at once as part of the covering position. The landing was made; the opportunity of the first days was not seized; the Turks reinforced; and the collapse of the Suvla stroke left the Allied forces holding three separated footholds — Helles, Anzac, and Suvla — without the decisive command of the Peninsula that alone could have opened the Straits.

Doom of Gallipoli

Ever since the failure at Suvla a general conviction had been growing that only two courses were open in the Near East: push on in increased force or abandon the attempt to open the Dardanelles. Committed by the importunity of the French ally to more than had ever been contemplated in the Balkans, the necessary increase of force for Gallipoli was not to be found. On 2 November the Prime Minister announced that henceforward the war would be directed by a new War Council of from three to five members of the Cabinet. The original members of the Council, known officially as the “War Committee,” were Mr. Asquith in the chair; Mr. Balfour (First Lord of the Admiralty); Lord Kitchener (Secretary of State for War); Sir Edward Grey (Foreign Secretary); and Mr. Lloyd George (Minister of Munitions). Their first meeting was on 3 November, to consider the question of evacuating Gallipoli.

In the middle of October Sir Ian Hamilton had returned to England and been succeeded by General Sir C. C. Monro; during the short interregnum Lieutenant-General Sir W. R. Birdwood assumed control. On 31 October, after conferring with the Corps Commanders and visiting each sector, Monro telegraphed a very unfavourable account: the troops were too much exhausted and had lost too many officers to make a further effort with any prospect of success, and he had no hesitation in advising evacuation. Maxwell, responsible for Egypt, strongly urged holding on if possible. Birdwood shrank from the loss of prestige and life that evacuation would probably entail, and was opposed unless a strike could be made elsewhere immediately. General Byng at Suvla thought voluntary withdrawal was possible without much loss; General Davies at Helles agreed with Monro.

Ministers were also confronted with wide divergence on the naval side. The Eastern Mediterranean Naval Staff had been working for months at a detailed plan by which they were confident another attempt to force the Straits would succeed — counting on increased ammunition, monitors, aircraft, and a greatly improved mine-sweeping service. Admiral Wemyss fully concurred; Admiral de Robeck was less sanguine. The moving spirit of the proposed scheme was his Chief of Staff, Commodore Roger Keyes, whom de Robeck sent home to lay the plan before the Admiralty (arrived 28 October): rush the Straits by surprise at dawn with two squadrons under support of a third. The War Committee, under the pressure of Balkan commitments and Monro’s advice, moved toward evacuation rather than a renewed naval coup de main.

Evacuation

The withdrawal was an operation which no one concerned could contemplate without the gravest misgiving. Nothing of the kind on a scale so great or under conditions so formidable had ever been attempted before. From beaches under artillery fire, without any safe natural harbour, some 90,000 men with two hundred guns had to be re-embarked in the face of an undefeated enemy whose trenches were nowhere more than three hundred yards from the British — and in some places only five — at a season of treacherous weather, in confined waters open to submarine attack, the whole work carried out at night. “As a piece of combined work… it must surely stand as a feat of arms unparalleled in the annals of war.”

Secrecy confined the labour of working out the scheme to the few naval and military officers who had to know. A joint staff conference decided that the operation must be conducted in three stages. The first was purely preparatory: the evacuation of all troops, animals and material not required for a defensive winter campaign. That stage was completed by 10 December, when Admiral Wemyss received definite orders to proceed; no fewer than 10,000 sick — frostbite alone numbering 3,700 — had already been re-embarked. The intermediate stage withdrew all men, guns and animals not required for the tactical defence of the positions during the final stage, which it was hoped would take only two nights more. For the final stage nothing but fighting men and medical personnel were to be left, not exceeding what the navy could embark in two nights — mainly a question of small craft. Wemyss held about one-third of the craft in reserve; twenty thousand men at each place was the utmost found possible, for with them must come the last of the guns. The troops would have to rely almost entirely on the ships for artillery support. Everything depended on lulling the enemy to sleep: all through the intermediate stage a show was made of landing animals and stores, and firing after midnight was avoided so as to accustom him to a routine of nocturnal inactivity.

The withdrawal from Suvla Bay and Anzac took place on the night of 18–19 December. It took the Turks completely by surprise; they were not even conscious of the progress of the operation until the whole of it had been concluded. Not a single life was lost; only three men were wounded. Six guns were left behind after being destroyed, and a relatively small quantity of stores. Mr. Asquith, announcing the result in the House of Commons, stated that so successful a result had seemed to him almost incredible. A few days later Sir Charles Monro was appointed to the command of the First Army in France, being succeeded in Gallipoli by Lieutenant-General Sir Archibald Murray. The final withdrawal from Helles followed in early January 1916.

Aftermath

The campaign had failed in its strategic object. The Straits remained closed; Russia was not relieved by that route; the Ottoman Empire was not knocked out. Looking over the year as a whole, the Annual Register observed that in the Dardanelles the event of chief importance was the general recognition of the failure of the expedition; at the end of the year it was gradually becoming apparent that the main theatre of operations in the future was likely to be on the Western front. Political consequences in London had already been severe. The resignations of Fisher and Churchill, coinciding with the formation of the Coalition, closed the first phase of the naval war of 1915. On 15 September Kitchener, speaking in the Lords, still declared that there was abundant evidence of a process of demoralisation among the German-led — “or rather German-driven” — Turks; by November the War Committee was deciding evacuation. Yet the re-embarkation itself stood as a masterpiece of combined staff work and discipline under conditions that had seemed to promise disaster. The Peninsula was left to the Turks; the troops re-embarked for Egypt and other theatres; and the Eastern Mediterranean effort of 1915 passed into the longer war of attrition that would outlast the year opened by the outbreak of the Great War.
