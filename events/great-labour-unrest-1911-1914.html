The Great Labour Unrest (1911–1914)

Between 1911 and the outbreak of the Great War, British industrial life was shaken by a wave of strikes and lock-outs on a scale that contemporaries compared to a labour war. Seamen, dockers, carters, railwaymen, miners, and transport workers struck in successive waves; a national coal stoppage in 1912 forced Parliament to legislate a district minimum wage; London transport closed the early summer of the same year; Dublin produced its own drama under James Larkin. The unrest ran parallel to the passage of the national-insurance-1911 Act, the aftermath of the peoples-budget-1911, and the deepening irish-home-rule-crisis. It tested the Liberal ministry of asquith-herbert-henry, estranged the parliamentary Labour party from the Government’s use of troops, and left employers and the Home Office preparing “strike breakers” and volunteer police reserves against the next round.

Prelude: 1910

The year 1910 was already marked by unsettlement. A cotton lock-out involving 120,000 operatives ended only on 10 October by Board of Trade intervention. In South Wales the coal trade was disturbed by incessant disputes after the Mines (Eight Hours) Act took effect on 1 January; a threatened general strike in March over a new wages agreement was averted, but sectional strikes in the Aberdare and Rhondda valleys cost an estimated £750,000 in wages and £120,000 in strike pay, cut production, and drove orders abroad. Among railwaymen there was much discontent over the interpretation of the 1907 arbitration awards; the Railway Servants’ Congress found a new argument for nationalisation in the working agreements that had virtually established monopolies among the companies.

Shipbuilding was hit still harder. A fourteen-week lock-out of ironworkers employed by the Federation of Shipbuilding Employers — described as a revolt against trade-union discipline after men broke an agreement made by the Boilermakers’ Society — began early in September when the yards were filling with work. Estimated losses reached £800,000 in wages and £100,000 in strike pay. On the Tyne a sectional riveters’ dispute led to a boilermakers’ lock-out that paralysed the district; no worse calamity, the Register noted, had threatened Tyneside since the engineers’ strike of 1897. A Clyde partial strike among subsidiary trades nearly produced a lock-out. Yet for all the stoppages, 1910 was commercially prosperous: Bankers’ Clearing House turnover rose 7.7 per cent., home railway earnings improved, and shipbuilding output increased in tonnage over the previous year once work resumed.

1911: Docks, Transport, and the Railway Crisis

The notes of the Annual Register for 1911 list among the year’s great events the seamen’s and transport workers’ strikes. Industrial unrest culminated in August in a railway crisis of extreme gravity. The Board of Trade invited railway companies and the men’s unions to confer separately on 16 August. Sir Guy Granet, general manager of the Midland, stated afterwards that the Government had promised the companies protection and that they were prepared to give “an efficient, if restricted, service” during a strike. Next day the executives of the men’s unions, who had come up from Liverpool overnight after resolving not to settle unless the Liverpool lock-out were ended, went with the chairman of the Labour party to the Board of Trade. Their grievance, they said, was the companies’ failure to observe the spirit and letter of the Agreement of 1907; the only remedy was a direct meeting with the companies or a strike.

Asquith offered an immediate Royal Commission to investigate the working of the 1907 conciliation scheme and to recommend amendments for prompt settlement of differences. The Government, he said, was absolutely impartial, but its first consideration must be the public interest: it could not allow a general paralysis of the railway system. The men’s representatives disliked his tone, thought the offer a device for shelving the dispute, and, after learning that he was not bringing companies and unions together that day, passed a resolution declaring his statement an unwarrantable threat. The strike began.

It was not then known that the peremptory tone of the Prime Minister and of the Home Secretary owed something to the critical condition of Anglo-German relations after Agadir — a condition of which the public was scarcely aware until November. The London and South-Western was not affected; other lines suffered in varying degrees. The worst stoppages lay in the quadrilateral whose extremities were roughly Newcastle, Liverpool, Coventry, and Hull, and also in South Wales. There were disturbances at Derby and at Llanelly. In London the Great Central, Great Western, and Brighton lines suffered most; St. Paul’s and Cannon Street stations closed, and Marylebone nearly so. Great Western traffic fell 40 per cent. against the corresponding week of 1910; North Staffordshire, 48 per cent. Troops moved into London parks and to Birmingham, Sheffield, and elsewhere; stations and strategic points were occupied. Six thousand men volunteered as special constables in London. Winston Churchill, speaking in the Commons, reported outrages on railway property — a crime punishable by penal servitude — and pledged the Government to secure food and fuel supplies and services vital to the community, because the working classes would be the worst sufferers from famine and a general arrest of industry.

Settlement came through a Special Commission under Sir David Harrel, with Sir Thomas Ratcliffe Ellis (Mining Association), Charles G. Beale of Birmingham, Arthur Henderson (Labour M.P. for Barnard Castle), and John Burnett (sometime Board of Trade Labour Correspondent) as members, and J. J. Wills of the Board of Trade as secretary. The Commission was to investigate the 1907 Conciliation Scheme and report desirable changes. Parliament, reassembling on 22 August, adjourned until 24 October. Ramsay Macdonald thought the settlement might guarantee peace if fairly carried out, but attacked the Home Office for prejudiced bulletins and a “reckless display of force” — a reversion, he said, to medieval ideas of law and order. Austen Chamberlain approved the Government’s action: the right to work was not less sacred than the right to strike. J. H. Thomas, of the railwaymen’s executive, praised the way the companies had met the men once negotiations resumed.

In the days after adjournment industrial peace returned in form. The Railway Commission met on 23 August; Manchester carters settled the same day; the Liverpool dockers’ strike was declared at an end on the evening of the 24th, after a threatened prolongation when tramwaymen were refused indiscriminate reinstatement and some 37,000 men were out or kept out in sympathy. Reinstatement “as and when required,” subject to the general manager’s satisfaction, and advice from labour leaders to return to work, closed the episode. A similar reinstatement dispute on the Great Eastern was compromised on 31 August after Board of Trade intervention. G. B. Askwith, the Board’s principal conciliator, received a knighthood. But there was a general feeling that the labour war was only suspended. Subscriptions were raised for railwaymen who had remained at their posts; suggestions circulated for local corps of “strike breakers” to keep lighting and railway travel running; a Home Office circular of 15 September encouraged volunteer police reserves. In Wales, anti-Jewish riots arising out of the unrest continued at Bargoed on 24 August, ascribed to rent-raising by Jewish houseowners.

Politically the August crisis left the Government stronger with the mass of Unionists and weaker with Labour and advanced Liberals. Bonar Law, at Leeds that autumn, blamed the Budget campaign of 1909 for intensifying working-class unrest, of which the labour disturbances of 1911 were “the fruit,” and pointed to a rise of nearly 10 per cent. in the cost of living without a corresponding rise in wages. The Government’s method, he said, was to take from the rich by taxation and give to the poor in doles; capital and population were going abroad. A fiscal change — Tariff Reform — would tend to raise wages. Scottish industrial centres felt the railway stoppages and Welsh disturbances less violently than the English midland and northern quadrilateral, but felt them nonetheless.

1912: The Coal Strike and the Minimum Wage

The political year 1912 opened under the shadow of industrial unrest, the approaching operation of the National Insurance Act, and the Government’s determination to press Home Rule and Welsh Disestablishment under the Parliament Act. On the Address, Ramsay Macdonald moved a Labour amendment ascribing labour troubles to insufficient wages amid expanding national wealth and rising living costs. Of 7,300,000 wage-earners, he said, 60 per cent. had incomes of less than 30s. a week; aggregate wages had fallen heavily since 1901 while living costs rose; skilled organised labour had been largely supplanted by unskilled, ill-paid labour; and the Government “supinely allowed monopolists, like the coal ring, to put up prices.” A minimum wage, begun experimentally by the Trade Boards Act of 1910, must go on. J. R. Clynes seconded, hoping unrest would continue until justice was done.

Industrial crisis soon overshadowed all else. The coal strike began at Alfreton in Derbyshire on 1 March; railway services were reduced; the King’s projected visits to foreign Courts were cancelled. On 19 March, in a crowded House, Asquith introduced the Coal Mines (Minimum Wage) Bill. The Government, he declared, had resorted to legislation only after all hope of settlement by agreement had disappeared, and was justified only in proposing legislation limited to effecting that settlement. The Bill gave effect to propositions laid before coalowners and miners on 28 February and accepted by the miners and by 65 per cent. of the coalowners. It was temporary — three years. The district minimum would be recoverable by civil process; any contract modifying it would be void; once ascertained it would be paid retrospectively from the date the miner resumed work. Wage rates, conditions, and exceptions would be fixed by district boards — twenty-one districts, possibly identical with existing Conciliation Boards — with employers and workmen equally represented and an independent chairman (or three chairmen) holding a casting vote, appointed by agreement or, failing that, by the Board of Trade. The aged and infirm would be excepted; there would be safeguards for efficiency and regularity; there were no penal provisions on either side. A legislative declaration in favour of a minimum wage would alter the situation; Parliament would then be justified, if heaven forbid, in taking further measures to defend the industries of the country against paralysis and starvation.

Bonar Law disclaimed party advantage but feared a precedent for other industries: the minimum wage had been treated in the Address debate as proper only for sweated trades; now it was applied where wages were above the average. The only difference, he said, was that the miners’ union was strong enough to put pressure on the Government. William Brace (Labour, South Glamorgan) replied that a minimum for abnormal places had been customary for years, but a county court had held it not legally binding and employers had refused legal sanction. Austen Chamberlain called the Bill panic legislation without finality and blamed the Chancellor’s speeches for the trouble. Sir Edward Grey wound up: the House faced a new demand — that the miner be paid a fair wage when he failed to earn it from causes beyond his control. Before intervention the minimum had been accepted by owners in the largest mining area; with a minimum wage there should be a minimum output; safeguards were in the Bill. Carried to its extreme, the strike meant starvation and the complete destruction of national life. A door had been opened for regulation of the minimum wage which could not be closed.

On 26 March, during the Report stage, Asquith announced with profound emotion that a new Conference had proved abortive. The rates of 5s. and 2s. were not unreasonable, but the Conference had refused his suggestion of treating them separately from the hewers’ wage. Having exhausted persuasion, argument, and negotiation, the Government pressed the Bill as the best possible solution. Bonar Law recognised that Ministers had done their best and would put no obstacle in the way; if the men did not return when the Bill passed, the whole resources of society might rightly protect those who desired to work. Macdonald said the miners were law-abiding people. The strike closed on 7 April after the Act’s passage. Cardiff steam coal, which had opened the year at 18s. 3d., risen over 30s. on fear of a national stoppage, and in March realised as much as 45s., began to decline once work resumed.

Transport, Syndicalism, and Concurrent Crises

A strike of London transport workers was declared as the Commons adjourned over Whitsuntide on 22 May 1912. It dominated early summer industrial politics while the Home Rule Bill went into Committee and the Franchise Bill received a second reading. The transport strike ended before the summer recess; the Finance and Appropriation Bills passed; Unionist speeches at Blenheim continued to reverberate. An academic debate on syndicalism (27 March), moved by Ormsby Gore and seconded by Hewins, declared advocacy of an anti-social policy based on class warfare and incitement to mutiny a grave danger to the State. Charles Hobhouse, for the Government, repudiated syndicalism as negativing parliamentary action and destroying national capital, ascribing unrest to the rise in living costs without corresponding wages and to improved mental qualities of workers without corresponding material improvement.

Throughout 1912 the irish-home-rule-crisis and the suffragette-movement occupied the same newspapers as the strikes. Asquith’s Dublin visit, the Ulster Covenant campaign, and Bonar Law’s Blenheim pledge of support for Ulster resistance ran alongside the coal and transport stoppages. The National Insurance Act came into force on Joy Day in the middle of the transport struggle. Trade, nonetheless, was extraordinarily active: practically every industry was employed; Bankers’ Clearing House returns reached record levels; commercial failures fell to the lowest figure in twenty-five years. The boom and the unrest were simultaneous facts.

1913–1914: Dublin, Building Trades, and the Coming of War

In 1913 labour unrest continued, especially noted in Scotland alongside heavy emigration from the Clyde to Canada (some 36,000 from 1 January to 24 May, a 50 per cent. increase on 1912). Dublin produced a separate drama. After the conviction of James Larkin — the prosecution citing his derisive treatment of the King’s name at a forbidden meeting — Liberal papers compared the trial bitterly with the immunity enjoyed by Sir Edward Carson, and even the Unionist Spectator said Larkin had been condemned on a charge of lèse majesté. An Albert Hall meeting on 1 November, for which there were 40,000 applications for admission, was addressed by Ben Tillett, several suffragists, and Bernard Shaw; the majority pledged themselves to heckle every Cabinet Minister until Larkin was released — and the pledge was kept. Municipal elections that day showed a considerable loss of Liberal seats to Labour and Socialist candidates.

In 1914 the arrival at Gravesend (24 February) of the Umgeni with Labour leaders deported from South Africa produced one of the largest Hyde Park demonstrations ever seen in London. Easter conferences of the Independent Labour party and other bodies exhibited “sectional discontent rather than any single organised challenge.” The Whitsuntide recess was overshadowed by the almost certain prospect of a national lock-out in the building trade and by the sinking of the Empress of Ireland. Ministers had lost prestige; of seven bye-elections since the session began they had lost four; they were suffering from Labour and Nationalist pressure and from the cumulative strain of the Ulster and Army crises of the spring.

Then the European storm broke. On 4 August an Order in Council declared it expedient that the Government should take control of the railways of the United Kingdom. The political year of 1914 ended; the war year began. The divisions on which, contemporaries believed, the Germans had staked their hopes closed rapidly. The great labour unrest of 1911–14 was not resolved so much as suspended by a greater emergency.

Aftermath and Significance

The unrest of 1911–14 was not a single organised revolution. It was a series of industry-by-industry explosions — docks and seamen, railways, coal, transport, Dublin general labour, building — driven by the rise in the cost of living, by disputes over the working of conciliation awards, by demands for a legal minimum wage, and by a new readiness of large unions to stop national services. The Liberal Government answered with a mixture of force and statute: troops and special constables in August 1911; the Harrel Commission on railway conciliation; Askwith’s mediation; the Coal Mines (Minimum Wage) Act of 1912. Bonar Law offered Tariff Reform as the alternative to “doles”; Labour members demanded nationalisation of railways and mines and a general minimum wage.

For labour-representation the period was double-edged. Parliamentary Labour had arrived in force in 1906 and could claim the Trade Disputes Act, pensions, and insurance as fruits of representation; yet the same years saw Macdonald and Thomas criticising the Home Office, Clynes hoping unrest would continue until justice was done, and Larkin’s supporters treating Cabinet ministers as enemies to be heckled. The unrest proved that social-reform by statute — old-age-pensions-1908, national-insurance-1911, Trade Boards, labour exchanges — had not removed the wage and recognition conflicts of an industrial society whose real incomes were under pressure. It also proved that when railways and coal stopped, the State would intervene, by soldiers if necessary and by minimum-wage law if possible. The irish-home-rule-crisis and the European war then absorbed the political energy that might have produced a further cycle of industrial confrontation. The labour war of 1911–14 closed not with a settlement but with an armistice imposed by history.
