The Western Front, 1914 (Mons to First Ypres)

After the outbreak of the Great War the first British campaign on the Continent was the passage of a small professional army across the Channel, its collision with the German right wing at Mons, the long retreat to the Marne, the recovery on the Aisne, and the first terrible stand around Ypres that closed the open warfare of 1914 and fixed the trench line for years to come. Parallel with that land drama ran the contest for command-of-the-sea in home waters: the Grand Fleet held its controlling position in the North Sea, while German battle cruisers struck the Yorkshire coast in December and the Admiralty redistributed its forces against further raids. For Great Britain the year on the Western Front was the year of the British Expeditionary Force — the force that, in the contemporary judgment of the Annual Register and in Churchill’s later narrative, played a part out of all proportion to its numbers in denying Germany the six-week victory on which the German design had staked the war.

Passage of the Expeditionary Force

The war found a Government prepared to withstand the enemy and a Parliament and people whose divisions — on which the Germans had staked their hopes — were rapidly closing. Promises of help poured in from all parts of the Empire. Horses and motor-lorries were hastily requisitioned; preparations, perfectly well known in the ports, were actively made for the despatch of the British Expeditionary Force; and most of the papers patriotically suppressed what news of the movement leaked by inadvertence. Then began that system of secrecy as to movements and details, loyally observed by all concerned and rigorously enforced by authority, which was kept up throughout the year by all the belligerents.

The Force crossed mainly by way of Southampton to Havre and Boulogne. Two destroyers and the Eighth Submarine Flotilla watched continuously to attack the German fleet had it interfered. The South-Western Railway Company dealt with the huge traffic. The arrival in France was announced officially in England on 18 August, though French papers had published it ten days earlier on the authority of the French War Office. Disquieting rumours had arisen from the delay; it was officially stated that no casualties had yet occurred among the troops.

At the same time were published a Message from the King and Instructions from Earl Kitchener. The King told the soldiers that they were leaving home to fight for the safety and honour of his Empire; that Belgium, whose country Britain was pledged to defend, had been attacked, and that France was about to be invaded by the same powerful foe. Kitchener’s instructions, to be kept in every soldier’s active-service pay-book, ordered the men to help their French comrades against a common enemy, to set an example of discipline and steadiness under fire, to maintain friendly relations with those they were helping, and to look upon looting as a disgraceful act. “In this new experience you may find temptations, both in wine and women. You must entirely resist both temptations… Do your duty bravely. Fear God. Honour the King.”

Mons and the Retreat

The concentration of the Expeditionary Force in France was completed on 21 August. Hopes raised in England by earlier accounts from France and Belgium gave place to anxiety as the Germans occupied Liège and advanced to Brussels, the French retired in Alsace, and Namur fell suddenly on 25 August. The fall of Namur, it was announced, necessitated the retirement of a portion of the Allied troops from the line of the Sambre to their original defensive position on the Franco-Belgian frontier. The British position was not fully revealed until Sir John French’s despatch was published on 10 September.

On 22 August, French stated, he had moved the troops into position in pursuance of General Joffre’s plans, apparently to cover the French left on the Sambre. They occupied a line of about twenty-five miles from Condé westwards through Mons to Binche: the Second Corps from Condé to Mons, the First from Mons to Binche, the 5th Cavalry Brigade on the extreme right at Binche. After cavalry reconnaissances on the 22nd and 23rd, the actual engagement began at 3 p.m. on the 23rd. Believing himself faced by only one or at most two German Army Corps, French learned at 6 p.m. from Joffre that there were at least three — a reserve corps and the 4th and 9th Corps — while a fourth was engaged in a turning movement on his left flank, and the French on his right were retiring before the Germans, who on the 22nd had secured the passages of the Sambre between Charleroi and Namur.

He therefore began to retire at daybreak to the line Jeumont–Maubeuge, some ten miles farther back, a position previously surveyed but difficult to hold, and reached it before nightfall. During the retirement the Second Cavalry Brigade under General De Lisle attempted a flank attack on the enemy’s infantry, was stopped by a wire entanglement, and the 9th Lancers and 18th Hussars suffered severely. Supported by the 19th Infantry Brigade, the Second Corps under General Smith-Dorrien effected a retreat, but with two German corps on its front and one threatening the flank it suffered great loss. From the new position a further retreat was forced by the enemy’s efforts to outflank the British and drive them on Maubeuge; on the 25th a further retirement was effected to a line some sixteen miles to the south-south-west, running from Cambrai by Le Cateau to Landrecies.

On 28 August the British retirement was followed closely by two German cavalry corps moving south-east from St. Quentin; the Third and Fifth Cavalry Brigades, under General Gough and General Chetwode, respectively repelled the Uhlans of the Guard south of the Somme and routed the eastern German column near Cerizy. Next day the Sixth French Army got into position on the British left; but the German numbers were overwhelming. After a visit from General Joffre, Sir John French agreed to retire towards the line Compiègne–Soissons; and, as his communications with Havre were threatened, the British base was changed to St. Nazaire, on the Atlantic near Nantes, with an advanced base at Le Mans. Joffre ordered a general retirement to the line of the Marne until he could reach a position enabling him to assume the offensive. Rearguard actions were frequent. On 1 September the First Cavalry Brigade, south of Compiègne, were overtaken by German cavalry; they momentarily lost a Horse Artillery battery, but with the help of detachments from the Third Corps they recovered it and captured twelve German guns. The First Corps were also engaged at Villers-Cotterets, the Fourth Guards Brigade suffering considerably.

Panic in London and the Turn of the Tide

On Sunday, 30 August, London was horrified by accounts of the retreat published in the Daily Mail and The Times — the latter speaking of “a retreating and a broken army,” the former of a “pitiful story” and an incessant German advance. The gaps left by the Censor’s editing suggested that the whole truth might be worse; the Daily Mail telegram closed with an appeal for reinforcements at once. For a few hours the news produced something like a panic. Its diffusion was restricted because the day was Sunday; in the afternoon the War Secretary issued a report of the four days’ battle showing that since the 26th, apart from cavalry fighting, the British Army had rested, that reinforcements covering double the loss suffered had already joined, and that the French armies had that day stopped the German advance. A decisive British victory in France, the statement added, would probably be fatal to the enemy; the continuance of Anglo-French resistance on a scale that kept the enemy’s best troops in the closest grip could, if prolonged, lead only to one conclusion.

Next day in Parliament the alarmist accounts were severely condemned by the Lord Chancellor and the Prime Minister, Mr. Asquith, the latter describing them as a regrettable exception to the patriotic reticence of the Press. It appeared, however, that the Press Bureau had actually requested their publication, and that the closing paragraph urging reinforcements was due to the head of the Bureau, Mr. F. E. Smith, himself.

Behind the public alarm the strategic crisis was resolving. Lord Kitchener journeyed to Paris. Correspondence passed between him and Sir John French. Of the retirement to the Marne it was later stated that losses were 15,000, not a third of those inflicted on the enemy; but the spirit of the force was not affected; drafts amounting to 19,000 men had arrived or were approaching, and the interval of quiet since 1 September had been used to fill up the gaps and refit and consolidate the units. The British Army was south of the Marne, in a line with the French forces on its right and left; the enemy was neglecting Paris and marching south-eastwards, having apparently abandoned its flanking movement on the Allies’ left — a change in German plans made about 3 September. The British troops, French reported, had definitely established their superiority to the Germans alike in rifle fire and in cavalry and artillery work; despite the heat, men and horses were in excellent condition; but “we must have more men.”

The Marne and the Aisne

On 3 September, when the British forces were in position south of the Marne between Lagny and Signy-Signets, and Sir John French had taken steps to defend the passage of the river, General Joffre invited him to retire twelve miles farther, to a position behind the Seine. The enemy crossed, and there were several outpost actions. On 5 September Joffre announced his intention of taking the offensive, and, at his request, the British Army changed front to its right, its left coming to rest on the Marne and its right on the Fifth Army. On 6 September the new battle began on a front running from Ermenonville through Lizy, Mauperthuis, Cortecon, Esternay, Charleville, to a point north of Verdun. About noon a German retreat began, and a series of battles followed till, on the evening of 10 September, the enemy, driven back to a line running from Compiègne to Soissons, were preparing to dispute the passage of the Aisne.

French specially mentioned the forcing of the passage of the Petit Morin River (8 September) by the First British Corps, and of the Marne by the First and Second Corps, and of the battle on 10 September, when these Corps, assisted by the Cavalry division on the right and the Third and Fifth Cavalry Brigades on the left, drove the enemy northwards, capturing thirteen guns, seven machine guns, some 2,000 prisoners, and much transport. Some further encouragement was given by the announcement (10 September) that the Fleet had swept the North Sea up to and including the German coasts in certain waters.

A further despatch, dated 8 October but published with the narrative of 19 October, practically completed the history of the fighting on the Aisne. On 12 September the enemy made a stand and prepared to dispute the passage of the river somewhat to the east of Soissons. The river valley ran east and west; the bottom was flat, the hills bordering it were about 400 feet high, with numerous spurs and re-entrants; they were backed on the north by a high plateau with patches of wood, admirably adapted for concealing troops. The enemy held a very strong position on the north of the Aisne — a winding stream, 170 feet wide and unfordable — commanding all the bridges, and with great facilities for concealment.

On 13 September the British forces were ordered to advance and make good the Aisne. Portions of them that day crossed at various points a distance of about twelve miles from Bourg to Venizel (the latter some three miles east of Soissons), with comparative ease on their right, with great difficulty on their left — the Fifth Infantry Brigade, in particular, crossing in single file under heavy fire on the broken and only remaining girder of a bridge. The crossing was not completed till the evening of the 13th, when the enemy (though still holding some points on the Aisne) effected a general retirement and entrenched on the high ground two miles north of the river, leaving detachments of infantry supported by powerful artillery in commanding points on the slopes of the spurs. The river was further bridged, under heavy artillery fire, by the Royal Engineers, and on the 15th there was a general advance, to ascertain whether the enemy intended to hold his position. The bombardment of Reims Cathedral and the announcement that, on the eleventh day of the Battle of the Aisne, the Allies were gaining ground, supplied the public with such encouragement as the secrecy of the time permitted.

Antwerp, Flanders, and First Ypres

The fate of Antwerp next absorbed attention. Sir John French stated that General Paris had handled the British force sent to the city with great skill and boldness; its action had considerably delayed the enemy and enabled the Belgian Army to be withdrawn and regain its value as a fighting force, and had also facilitated the destruction of war material which would have been of great value to the enemy; moreover, the moral effect of this “necessarily desperate attempt” to succour the Belgian Army had greatly conduced to their efficiency as a fighting force. The despatch was virtually a reply to Press strictures on a step regarded as essentially the enterprise of the First Lord of the Admiralty, Mr. Winston Churchill, who had, in fact, visited the city during the British occupation. The Morning Post of 13 October described it as “a costly blunder, for which Mr. Winston Churchill was primarily responsible”; the relief had come too late, and kept the Belgian Army there too long. In other quarters it was pointed out that the despatch of the force must have been the act of the whole Cabinet, and that it had a moral value as a demonstration of sympathy with Belgium.

Meanwhile the Germans occupied Bruges and Thielt, bombarded Arras, and were evidently making a desperate effort to reach the coast at Dunkirk and Calais, hoping to interfere at least with British shipping in the Channel. The capture of Antwerp seemed to increase their means of interference. The defenders had destroyed the stores of petrol, and sunk or disabled the steamers in the port; and to have used it as a naval base even for submarines would have involved the violation of the neutrality of Holland. But it increased the danger of a Zeppelin raid on London; the Mayor of Gravesend issued a warning against hostile aircraft; and it opened the way for a German advance to the more suitable bases, at Zeebrugge and Ostend, for a naval or aerial attack.

The fall was accompanied by a fresh influx into England of Belgian refugees: in four days (7–10 October) some 10,000 landed in Folkestone from Ostend; on Sunday, 11 October, 4,250 landed there from Ostend and 900 from Flushing; by 17 October the total number in England was 100,000. Crowds reached other ports, notably Lowestoft, in sailing craft, amid great suffering. Relief was promptly given by committees at London and Folkestone, and shelter and hospitality were offered throughout Great Britain; numbers of Belgian wounded were provided for in improvised hospitals, private houses being frequently lent and fitted up by their owners. Many German spies were said to be among the refugees, and for that reason they were withdrawn from Dover and Grimsby. The fate of Belgium was a powerful factor in the issue of a Labour manifesto (15 October), signed by twenty-five Labour members of Parliament and thirty-five leading trade union officials, declaring that in view of Germany’s conduct there must be no peace until the enemy had been defeated.

After the Aisne the struggle became a race for the northern flank and the Channel ports. A daring attempt, not fully revealed until six weeks later, was made by Sir John French to outflank the German forces in northern France. The general idea, set out in his lengthy despatch published on 30 November, was to effect a turning movement north of Lille and then, with the French, to press the German right. The British force was transferred from the Aisne between 3 and 19 October. Battle was joined in the Ypres–Armentières region from about 11 October; the first great stage of the struggle around Ypres ran on to about 31 October. The fighting round Gheluvelt, some six miles from Ypres, on 29–31 October became the emblem of the British stand. The professional army that had marched from Mons, diminished and reinforced, held the line against repeated German attempts to break through to the Channel. By the end of November the open warfare of August and September had closed into the continuous trench system that would dominate the Western Front for the next three years — the long road that would later lead through the Somme, Passchendaele, and the crises of 1918.

Home Waters: Command of the Sea and the Yorkshire Raid

While the Expeditionary Force fought its way from Mons to Ypres, the naval war in home waters entered a new phase. With the destruction of Admiral von Spee’s squadron at the Falklands the enemy had no organised squadrons outside his home waters, and command-of-the-sea, judged by traditional standards, was practically done. Yet the Dresden’s escape and the unsolved mystery of the Karlsruhe still occupied cruisers; and in Home waters mining and submarines of increasing range made free movement precarious. Over 150 trawlers and drifters formed the nucleus of the Auxiliary Patrol; the Grand Fleet itself now depended on fishing craft for free movement near its bases. Still the key fact remained that the Grand Fleet held its controlling position. The High Seas Fleet, under the Emperor’s absolute control, was confined to submarines and destroyers for offensive action, with only occasional sorties of heavy cruisers; outside the Baltic Britain enjoyed a command as complete as in the old blockades of Brest. Until Fisher’s new programme of monitors and fast heavy ships for inshore work was forward, the enemy might use the long winter nights for some sudden blow to diminish the blockade or deter further troop movements to France.

By 10 December news of the Falklands had revealed the temporary weakening of the Grand Fleet. Now if ever was the enemy’s moment to strike, and by the 14th reports of an impending attack were so far confirmed that the fleet was put in motion. It appeared to be a cruiser raid, with or without transports; without undue prejudice to concentration it was possible to ensure that the enemy should have no time to land any serious force, or if a mere naval raid, that subject to the chances of the sea he should never get back. The 5th Battle Squadron stood at shortest notice; Tyrwhitt with his Harwich flotillas was directed to get touch and shadow; Beatty was to take the four remaining battle cruisers from Cromarty, joined by Goodenough’s light cruisers from Scapa, with one battle squadron in support.

Ingenohl, fretting at the inaction imposed since Heligoland, took the Kaiser’s limited permission for big-cruiser sorties as a hint and devised a coast raid — stretching his instructions by supporting Hipper with three battle squadrons. Early on 15 December Hipper left the Jade with Seydlitz, Moltke, Von der Tann, the newly completed Derfflinger, and Blücher; the battle squadrons left Cuxhaven in the afternoon and after dark took supporting station while Hipper proceeded on his errand toward the Yorkshire coast. The raid of 16 December was a purely naval, not a combined, operation; yet intelligence made it impossible to relax measures against an attempt to land troops.

Redistribution and the Cuxhaven Raid

The southern areas had been weakened by the loss of Bulwark. On 20 December Beatty was ordered from Cromarty to join the 3rd Battle Squadron and 3rd Cruiser Squadron at Rosyth; Princess Royal, delayed hunting the ghost of the Karlsruhe, was already homeward bound. The new 10th Cruiser Squadron under de Chair in Alsatian completely relieved the Grand Fleet of the north-about trade-route watch. Christmas Day tested the new stations with an air raid on the Cuxhaven Zeppelin sheds — nine seaplanes from Engadine, Riviera, and Empress, supported by Harwich forces, the whole Grand Fleet concentrated in the middle of the North Sea in hope of provoking a fleet action. Dense frost fog frustrated location, but in Schillig roads seven battleships and three battle cruisers weighed in such hurry that Von der Tann fouled another cruiser and both were severely damaged. Of the returning airmen three were rescued by Nasmith in E 11 off Norderney under the approach of a Zeppelin — founding the reputation he completed in the Sea of Marmara.

Those winter dispositions — Beatty at Rosyth, the Grand Fleet still controlling the North Sea, the Auxiliary Patrol watching the approaches — set the scene for the next meeting of battle cruisers in January 1915 at the Dogger Bank, and for the wider strategic debates that would soon turn part of British effort toward the Dardanelles and gallipoli. On land, the professional army of 1914 had held the line from Mons to Ypres at a cost that already pointed to the need for the mass armies Kitchener was raising at home. The Western Front of 1914 closed not in victory but in a continuous trench system; the year had denied Germany its short war and committed the United Kingdom to a continental struggle of a scale no Cabinet of July 1914 had fully imagined.
