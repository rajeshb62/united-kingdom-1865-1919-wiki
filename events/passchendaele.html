Messines and the Third Battle of Ypres (Passchendaele) (1917)

The British Flanders campaign of 1917 opened with a carefully prepared success on the Messines ridge in June and then, at the end of July, passed into the prolonged offensive known as the Third Battle of Ypres — a struggle that culminated, on 6 November, in the storming of the village of Passchendaele by Canadian troops. It was the principal British land effort of the year on the Western Front after the inconclusive fighting of the Somme the year before, and it was fought under a War Cabinet formed when the first Coalition of 1915–16 had already given way to Lloyd George’s smaller directing body. The Annual Register of 1917 records the operations in the language of the contemporary official narrative: limited advances under extreme bombardment, heavy fighting east of Ypres through the autumn, and no large geographical change. Hankey’s diary and the War Cabinet minutes of June 1917 place those operations against a darker political background — the first clear warning that French army discipline was breaking, Lloyd George’s desire to abandon the Flanders offensive and seek a separate peace with Austria, and the claim that the Messines mine plan itself had originated with Hankey and been carried out by Norton-Griffiths.

After Arras: The Battle of Messines

After the Battle of Arras and the unsuccessful French offensive in April there was a lull in the fighting. British captures of prisoners during May were small, and only one gun was taken. At the beginning of June the British opened a new offensive, carried out with even greater vigour than had been displayed at Arras. The new operations received the name of the Battle of Messines.

To the west of the small town of Messines the ground rises to a ridge, a long low hill which the Germans then held, and from which their guns commanded much of the British Front south of Ypres. It was this ridge which was the immediate objective of the assault. North-west of Messines the German line, as it existed at the beginning of June, formed a marked salient and ran westward of Wytschaete. After a violent artillery preparation lasting for several days, the infantry of the British Second Army moved to the assault at 3 a.m. on 7 June. The attack extended over a front of nine miles from Klein Zillebeke, just north of the Ypres–Comines Canal, to a point about a mile south of Messines village. The Second Army was under the command of General Plumer.

The attack upon the ridge was preceded by the explosion of twenty enormous mines (Hankey later wrote of nineteen), upon the preparation of which months had been spent. The sound of this explosion was heard in the outskirts of London. The German defence was literally blown to pieces. The whole of the contested ridge was captured in the first rush of the infantry. The villages of Wytschaete, Messines, Oosttaverne, Hollebeke, and Klein Zillebeke were also captured. Five thousand prisoners were taken on this one day, but the number of guns which fell to the British was not large. The advance reached a maximum depth of two miles. In the course of the next week the Crown Prince of Bavaria, who commanded the troops on the British Front, launched a series of violent counter-attacks, but all his attempts to retake the ridge were successfully frustrated. During the remainder of June the British made further slight advances, and by the end of the month the toll of captures increased to sixty-seven guns and nearly 9,000 prisoners — figures, the Annual Register noted, not so large as those relating to the Battle of Arras.

Hankey recorded that the plan of concentrating mines and preparing for an offensive months ahead had been suggested by him and pressed on the soldiers both personally (to General Fowke, the C.R.E. in France) and through Swinton by letter in the first six months of 1915. “I believe the first idea was mine but Norton-Griffiths carried it out.” The purpose of the Messines stroke, as Hankey’s biographer summarises it, was to straighten out the notorious Ypres salient. The capture of the dominating Messines ridge was a heartening, though limited, success. As soon as the Germans had recovered from the initial shock the battle resumed a static form — though with Plumer’s Second Army better placed through possession of the geographically insignificant but tactically vital high ground, and the German “bulge” south of Ypres eliminated. Nothing further was attempted in this sector until the start of the Third Battle of Ypres at the end of July.

The French Crisis and the Question of Flanders

While Messines was in progress, General Henry Wilson brought to the Cabinet the warning that the French would not stick it much longer, and that Pétain had failed in his promise to carry out an offensive on the 10th in support of Haig’s attack on the Messines ridge. The report, confirmed more or less by Lord Esher, made the War Cabinet very anxious. It was the Cabinet’s first clear intimation that something was seriously wrong with the discipline of the French Army. Hankey’s chapter headings for the spring and early summer of 1917 place the same crisis in a wider strategic frame: the failure of the Nivelle offensive and the French mutinies; the Russian Revolution and its effect on Allied strategy; the entry of the United States into the war; and the problems of co-ordination with the Allies. In a Specially Secret Cabinet Minute covering seven foolscap pages in Hankey’s own hand, he recorded that the War Cabinet felt the very grave information communicated by Wilson strongly reinforced Lord Milner’s arguments in favour of a fresh stocktaking of the whole war situation.

Lloyd George took a more sanguine view than Wilson. After harsh remarks about the large number of instances in which the advice of the experts had proved wrong, he emphasised his belief that Britain could achieve both a military and a diplomatic success. Once again he had his eyes on the Middle East, where he hoped that if Syria could be got into British hands both Turkey and Bulgaria might sue for peace. The final decisions of 8 June included the setting up of a Committee on War Policy (Milner’s proposal, with Smuts brought in as a fresh mind) and a resolve that Austria was at least entitled to a reply on peace feelers — despite Italian objections. Hankey recorded the gist of the discussion: Lloyd George now proposed to abandon the British offensive in Flanders and try to isolate Germany by making a separate peace with Austria. Haig and Robertson’s reaction to those proposals would follow in due course. The Flanders offensive, nevertheless, went forward.

At home the same months were marked by the determination, recorded in the Annual Register, that the war must be continued “at whatever sacrifices” until a victorious conclusion had been reached. Man-power and the disposition of the forces continued to occupy the House when the war was under debate. Critics of the Salonika expedition and advocates of fuller use of the Military Service Acts argued over where the next drafts should come from; Mr. Churchill, in a general strategic survey early in the year, doubted whether decisive results could be expected by mere attrition and hoped the Government would not commit its armies to attrition on a gigantic scale unless they were absolutely certain they could pursue it to a decisive point. The Flanders offensive was precisely the kind of commitment that debate had in view.

The Third Battle of Ypres

The next British offensive was carried out with some assistance from the French and was known as the Third Battle of Ypres. During the last ten days of July the bombardment of the German line in Flanders was increased to a pitch of extreme violence, and according to the German account the artillery duel on this occasion exceeded in intensity anything previously witnessed. After the bombardment the infantry went to the attack on 31 July, a small contingent of French troops making a joint attack and operating on the British left wing, close to the sea.

The new offensive suffered the same fate as the other great attacks in the Western Theatre. Advances were made in the initial stages only: the opposing lines were driven back a short distance, but were not broken. The attack extended over a front of fifteen miles, from Steenstraate on the river Yser to La Basse Ville on the river Lys. The French contingent captured Steenstraate and Bixschoote and drove back the German lines to a depth of about two miles. This advance was achieved in the first rush, on the morning of 31 July, and the British advances further south were likewise made on the first day. The British captured the villages of Verlorenhoek, Frezenberg, St. Julien, and Pilkem in the centre, and the villages of Hooge, Westhoek, La Basse Ville, and Hollebeke on the right wing. The British took over 5,000 prisoners on 31 July, and the French captured about 1,000.

The German counter-attacks were prompt and determined. Throughout the night of 31 July–1 August they endeavoured to recapture the ground they had lost, hoping to seize the disputed terrain before the British had had time to consolidate. In this the enemy were frustrated; on 1 August the Germans did succeed in recapturing St. Julien, but they obtained no appreciable advantage at any other spot, and the British once more entered St. Julien two days later.

During the next fortnight the fighting on the British Front was less severe, but on 15 August Sir Douglas Haig again took the offensive. On that day Canadian troops delivered a successful assault upon a dominating height known as Hill 70, near Lens, and on the following day the village of Langemarck, north-east of Ypres, was taken. During these two days about 2,500 prisoners were captured, and a few guns also fell into British hands. The total number of prisoners taken by the British in these offensives from 31 July to 31 August inclusive was 10,697, and about forty guns were captured.

Hankey’s narrative of July–September 1917 centres the same months on the third battle of Ypres itself, his visits to the front, and his impressions of Haig and the commanders, together with the growth of the Cabinet Secretariat and the co-ordination of Allied strategy. The offensive remained Haig’s great Flanders effort of the year; the political doubts of June had not cancelled it.

Autumn Fighting and the Storm of Passchendaele

Although no large geographical changes were brought about, extremely heavy fighting took place in the Western Theatre during the last four months of the year. The Allies had the initiative throughout, and it was invariably they who took the offensive, although the German defence remained powerful and vigorous. By means of very heavy bombardments and desperately hard fighting, the British pressed back the Germans for short distances along the front east of Ypres. On 20 September assaults were delivered along the Ypres–Menin road, and in the successful though limited advance over 2,000 prisoners were captured. The village of Veldhoek was rushed, and on 21 September 1,000 more prisoners were taken. During the next few days the Germans made several powerful counter-attacks, but the British held their new positions. In the month of September the British captured 5,296 German prisoners and eleven guns, and in October they similarly captured 9,125 prisoners and fifteen guns.

On 6 November another assault was delivered east of Ypres, this time a short distance north of the Menin road, and the village of Passchendaele was stormed by Canadian troops. Passchendaele stands on relatively high ground. The name of the village would become, in later British memory, a byword for the cost of the Flanders offensive; the contemporary Annual Register records the storm as one more limited success in a year of extremely heavy fighting that left the Western Front still locked in deadlock. Hankey’s circle would look back on the campaign with the resolve, later attributed in his biographer’s narrative, that there should be “no more Passchendaeles.”

Caporetto, Versailles, and the Close of the Year

Hankey’s October–December chapter places the conclusion of the Passchendaele offensive beside the Italian disaster at Caporetto, the dispatch of British and French divisions to Italy, and the creation of the Supreme War Council at Versailles — inter-Allied machinery in which Hankey himself took a central part. War-weariness and a growing demand for a negotiated peace form the political atmosphere of that autumn in his account, even as the Annual Register recorded at home unbroken public determination to prosecute the war to victory, food restriction and national service experiments, daylight air-raids on London, and the great Representation of the People measure still the central domestic business of Parliament.

The Flanders ridge had been bought in short advances under extreme bombardment; the Ypres salient had been improved by Messines and by the autumn gains east of the town; the German line had not been broken. When the year closed, Britain remained, in Hankey’s phrase for the midsummer months, the cornerstone of the Allied effort — but the strategic situation of early 1918, as his next chapters open it, was already the preparation for the German spring offensives that would test the Western Front more severely than the limited Flanders advances of 1917 had done.
