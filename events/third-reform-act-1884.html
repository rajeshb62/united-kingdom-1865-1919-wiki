The Third Reform Act (1884–1885)

The Third Reform Act — consisting of the Franchise Act of 1884 and the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885 — was the crowning legislative achievement of Gladstone’s second ministry, extending the household suffrage that had been granted to boroughs in 1867 to the county constituencies, and fundamentally redrawing the electoral map of the United Kingdom. The Acts added some two million new voters to the electoral register, enfranchised the agricultural labourer for the first time, and established the single-member constituency as the dominant form of parliamentary representation.

Background: The Anomalies of Representation

By the early 1880s, the defects of the existing electoral system had become glaring. As Justin McCarthy records, “the most flagrant anomalies and injustice were still existing” in the distribution of seats. “In some cases pitiful little boroughs with only a few hundred inhabitants returned as many representatives as some great and important counties. Then there were purely imaginary divisions of constituencies which threw the balance of representation quite out of gear.”

The county franchise had remained far more restrictive than the borough franchise established by the Reform Act of 1867. While the artisans of the towns had obtained the household vote, the agricultural labourer of the countryside — who formed a vast and growing portion of the population — remained disenfranchised. Sir George Trevelyan had brought forward year after year his motion for “the equalising of the county franchise with that which prevailed in cities and boroughs,” but without any actual success. Only a strong Liberal Government, McCarthy observes, could possibly deal with the evils from which the country suffered.

The external circumstances of 1884 were inauspicious for reform. The Egyptian question darkening the air, and the Irish troubles exasperating men’s minds in all quarters of the House of Commons, many sincere Liberals felt that the Government was only courting defeat by venturing on the contentious question of a reduced franchise. Mr. Gladstone, however, was not deterred.

The Franchise Bill of 1884

Gladstone divided his great reform scheme into two separate bills. The first — the Franchise Bill — he introduced into the House of Commons on February 29, 1884. In a speech of remarkable breadth, he declared that the bill was introduced “in fulfilment of a pledge, in obedience to a widely expressed demand, and for the purpose of adding strength to the State.” He declined to argue anew the case of the classes to be enfranchised, as that case had been admitted in regard to borough populations fifteen years before, and had been “completely approved by the experience of those fifteen years.”

“I am not prepared,” Gladstone told the House, “to discuss admission to the franchise now as it was discussed fifty years ago, when Lord John Russell had to state, with almost bated breath, that he expected to add in the three kingdoms half a million to the constituencies. It is not now a question of nicely calculated less or more. I take my stand upon the broad principle that the enfranchisement of capable citizens, be they few or be they many — and if they be many, so much the better — is an addition to the strength of the State.”

The Government proposed to put the county population on the same level as the populations of cities and towns. In boroughs, the ancient rights franchises were left untouched; the household franchise of 1867 remained; the ten pounds clear yearly value franchise was extended to land held without houses or buildings; and a new service franchise was created for persons who were inhabitants of a house but were neither occupiers nor tenants — including the officers of great public institutions and the humbler men who occupied houses as caretakers. In the counties, the franchise was reduced to ten pounds yearly value, and the household, lodger, and service franchises of the boroughs were established in the county constituencies. No one already qualified was to be disfranchised. Everyone who was the head of a household would be enabled to become a voter.

The bill extended to Ireland and Scotland with whatever slight differences of detail might be necessary under the existing conditions of those countries. Gladstone insisted that “England, Ireland, Scotland and Wales were to be treated in this measure on a principle of absolute equality.” Nothing, he declared, would induce the Government to depart from that determination. “I could bear no part in the responsibility of passing, perhaps, a Reform Bill for England and for Scotland, and then leaving a Reform Bill for Ireland to take its chance.” As McCarthy observes, these words “were addressed to the House of Commons, but they were levelled at the House of Lords.”

The first reading was carried without serious opposition. The measure passed through its third reading on June 26 and was at once sent up to the House of Lords.

The Lords’ Challenge and the Autumn Crisis

In the House of Lords, Lord Cairns immediately gave notice of a resolution declaring that, while the peers would be prepared to concur in a well-considered and complete scheme for the extension of the franchise, they did not think it right to assent to the second reading of a bill “having for its object a fundamental change in the electoral body, which is not accompanied by provisions ensuring the full and free representation of the people, or by any adequate security that the bill shall not come into operation except as an entire scheme.” The amendment was carried by 205 votes to 146, and the further progress of the measure was practically stopped for that session.

Unlike the crisis of 1831, when reform had been opposed on the ground that it would invite revolution, the Conservative peers now disclaimed any desire to prevent the passing of a bill for the further extension of the suffrage. “What they contended was, that it was the interest of the people to have a symmetrical scheme before them, in order that they might know whether full justice was to be done to the popular claim.” Some spoke of themselves as “much better friends of the people than Mr. Gladstone and the Liberal Government.”

Negotiations were instantly opened between the Government and the leaders of the Opposition. Meetings were held all over the country, and the negotiations went on day after day, giving rise to “all manner of misunderstandings,” public explanations repudiated, and frequent complaints about unauthorised publication of private interchanges. Still, two great facts were manifest to the public: first, that the House of Lords disclaimed any intention of setting itself up against the popular will; and second, that the Liberal Government was prepared, while surrendering no principle, to invite the Opposition’s assistance in moulding a satisfactory measure. Parliament was prorogued on this understanding.

During the recess, Gladstone declared, “I do not believe that the House of Lords has as yet placed itself in a position of irretrievable error. I believe it to be possible that it may go back, and may go back with dignity and with honour.”

There was some disturbance in Birmingham in October 1884, when a Conservative meeting was held in Aston Park and the Liberals organised a counter-demonstration. The park was broken into, rival crowds came into collision, and there was wreckage and destruction of property. Lord Randolph Churchill endeavoured to fix Mr. Chamberlain with responsibility, and used his memorable expression about “drawing the badger” — compelling Chamberlain to defend himself.

The Winter Session and the Compromise

The winter session of Parliament was opened on October 23, 1884, when the Queen’s Speech announced that the two Houses had been called together “to give further consideration to the great subject of the representation of the people in Parliament.” The Franchise Bill, in substance unchanged, was brought in and read a first time without division or even discussion. On November 6, Gladstone moved the second reading. An amendment insisting on a redistribution bill was formally moved, but as it was now known that negotiations were proceeding favourably, the amendment was not pressed. The second reading was carried by 372 votes against 232 on November 7. On November 11, the bill was read a third time without a division.

The negotiations were conducted on the Government side by Mr. Gladstone, Lord Hartington, and Sir Charles Dilke, and on the Opposition side by Lord Salisbury and Sir Stafford Northcote. The result was that the House of Lords became willing to accept the Franchise Bill, and Gladstone announced that he would soon introduce his Redistribution Bill.

The Redistribution of Seats Act 1885

On December 1, 1884, Mr. Gladstone brought in the redistribution measure. The bill proposed to increase the number of members of the House by 12, raising the total from 658 to 670. The 12 additional members were given to Scotland, which had been left with an admittedly inadequate representation; Scotland was now to have 72 members. Six disfranchised seats were restored to representation and given to England. Ireland and Wales retained their representation as before — Ireland kept 103 members, a number defended on the ground that the Act of Union provided that Ireland should have not less than 100 members.

The most significant innovation was the adoption of single-member constituencies as the dominant principle. As Clayton records, “the principle of single-member equal electoral districts — on a basis of 54,000 inhabitants — was adopted. Only twenty-three boroughs, the City of London and the Universities of Oxford, Cambridge, and Dublin, retained double-member representation.” This represented a fundamental reorganisation of the electoral map.

There was considerable discussion about the proposal to divide nearly all the great cities into different wards or districts, each returning one member. Mr. Courtney, then Financial Secretary for the Treasury, was so strongly opposed to this principle that he resigned his office. Sir John Lubbock moved an instruction to sanction the transfer of votes to provide for the representation of minorities, but the instruction was rejected by a large majority.

The Franchise Bill passed through all its readings in the House of Lords and became law just before the adjournment for the Christmas recess. The Redistribution Bill came on again early in March and was finally carried through both Houses of Parliament without much further trouble.

McCarthy records the outcome with satisfaction: “Nothing seems to us to have been more satisfactory than the principle of practical arrangement which was adopted in this instance by the leaders of the Government and the leaders of Opposition.” While extreme Radicals insisted the Government could have got better terms, and some Tories declared their leaders had sacrificed too much, the great majority on both sides were satisfied. “No serious fault has since been found in the working of this scheme of reform, which was agreed to by the common consent of the leading minds representing Liberalism and Conservatism in the country.”

Significance and Impact

The Third Reform Act completed the work begun by the First Reform Act of 1832 and the Second Reform Act of 1867. As the Annual Register for 1918 would later record in summarising the successive reforms, the Act of 1832 enfranchised 500,000 new voters; that of 1867, 1,000,000 new voters; and that of 1884, some 2,000,000 new voters.

Joseph Clayton, writing in his contemporary survey The Rise of the Democracy, summarised the transformation: “The third Reform Act, giving household suffrage to the country districts, was passed by Gladstone in 1884, and it was followed by a Redistribution of Seats Act in 1885. By these two Acts the agricultural labourer was enfranchised, a service franchise was created for those who were qualified neither as householders nor lodgers, and the principle of single-member equal electoral districts — on a basis of 54,000 inhabitants — was adopted.”

The effect on the balance between the Liberal and Conservative parties was profound, though not immediate or straightforward. The enfranchisement of the agricultural labourer was generally expected to benefit the Liberals, who had championed the reform and who now commanded the allegiance of a vastly expanded rural electorate. In the event, the general election of 1885 — the first held under the new franchise and the new boundaries — produced a hung parliament in which Parnell’s Irish Nationalists held the balance of power. The newly enfranchised county voters proved more independent than either party had anticipated, and the question of Home Rule for Ireland, which Gladstone embraced in the aftermath of the election, came to dominate the next decade of British politics.

The Act also had a lasting constitutional significance. As Clayton noted, when the Labour Party began to send working-class representatives to Parliament in growing numbers after 1900, it was the extension of the franchise in 1867 and 1884 that had “made the entry of the workman inevitable.” The Third Reform Act thus laid the electoral foundation upon which the modern democratic political order in Britain was built.

Yet McCarthy, writing while the memory of the debates was still fresh, cautioned that no reasonable man could suppose it to be a final settlement. “The principle of ‘One Man, One Vote’ was enunciated more than once during the debates, and the time must come when that principle will have to be established in our legislation, and a man will no longer be allowed to have one vote for his house in the West-end, a second for his office in the City, a third for his country house in one of the shires, a fourth for his shooting-place in Scotland, and so on.”
