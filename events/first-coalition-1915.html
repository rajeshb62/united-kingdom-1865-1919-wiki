The First Coalition Government (1915)

In the late spring of 1915 the pure Liberal ministry that had carried the United Kingdom into the Great War came to an end. Nine months of fighting on the Western Front, the opening of the Dardanelles enterprise, a public agitation over shells and drink, and a rupture at the Admiralty between Lord Fisher and Mr. Churchill made the continuation of single-party government impossible. Mr. Asquith remained Prime Minister, but he reconstructed his administration as a Coalition in which Unionists and Labour sat on the Treasury Bench with Liberals. The change did not settle the war; it marked the moment when party government, as peacetime Britain had known it, yielded to a wartime national ministry whose first great domestic instrument was the Ministry of Munitions under Mr. Lloyd George.

Drink, Shells, and the Spring Crisis

By the spring of 1915 the country was already deep in the industrial and social strain of a long war. The Annual Register records that the daily cost of the war had risen above three and a half millions sterling, and that recruiting for 1916, blockade policy, and the partial application of the Declaration of London were under constant public scrutiny. Into this atmosphere the Government thrust a second controversy — drink.

On 29 April Lloyd George brought before the House proposals framed as a clearing of the road to munitions output. Production of shells and war material, he insisted, was “life for the Empire.” The drink trade was presented as an obstacle to that production; restriction of the liquor traffic was therefore urged not as a temperance crusade but as a munitions measure. The same weeks saw a fierce Press campaign, led by The Times and the Daily Mail, on the shortage of high-explosive shell at the front. The Northcliffe papers hammered the War Office and the Government; the cry for a Ministry of Munitions became a political force that no Cabinet could ignore.

Lord Kitchener remained Secretary of State for War, the great figure of voluntary recruiting and of the New Armies. The War Office, however, was increasingly identified in the public mind with the shell shortage. The munitions agitation and the drink proposals together destroyed the atmosphere in which a purely Liberal Cabinet could claim unchallenged authority over the conduct of the war.

Fisher, Churchill, and the Breaking of the Liberal Ministry

At the Admiralty the strain took a different form. The Dardanelles enterprise, pressed through the early months of 1915 as a naval and then a combined operation, had divided counsel. Corbett records that the resignations of Lord Fisher and Mr. Churchill, coinciding with the formation of a Coalition Government, closed the first phase of the naval war of 1915. Fisher’s departure as First Sea Lord made the position of the existing Liberal ministry, already under fire over munitions, untenable. The Annual Register treats the reconstruction of the Government as the political consequence of that double crisis — shells and Admiralty — rather than as a sudden conversion to the principle of coalition for its own sake.

Asquith did not resign the Premiership. He reconstructed. The Unionist leaders, who had given patriotic support since the outbreak of war, were brought into office; Labour accepted a place on the Treasury Bench. The new Unionist members of the Cabinet required, under the existing law, re-election on taking office, and an emergency Bill was prepared to deal with that inconvenience. The King, George V, remained the constitutional centre of the reconstruction; the party truce of August 1914 was converted into a formal sharing of power.

The Coalition Meets: 3 June 1915

Both Houses reassembled on 3 June for the first sitting of the Coalition. The Commons presented a novel appearance. Unionists and Labour sat on the Treasury Bench with Liberals; the old party geography of the chamber was visibly altered. The Annual Register marks the occasion as the opening of a new political chapter: not the end of party, but its suspension for the duration of the war emergency.

The reconstructed ministry retained Asquith at its head and Kitchener at the War Office. Lloyd George left the Exchequer to take the newly constituted Ministry of Munitions — the institutional answer to the shell crisis and to the Press campaign of the spring. The Admiralty passed into new hands after Fisher’s and Churchill’s resignations. The Coalition was thus, from the first day of its parliamentary life, a munitions ministry as much as a war ministry: its domestic priority was the organisation of national output for the armies in France and for the expedition at gallipoli.

Summer and the Unsettled Year

Through the summer of 1915 the Coalition governed under the continuous pressure of war news — Zeppelin raids, the air war, the second anniversary of the war marked by patriotic meetings on 4 August, and the successful campaign to place cotton on the absolute contraband list. The Ministry of Munitions began its work of concentrating and expanding shell and war-material production. The Northcliffe Press continued to criticise Ministers on munitions, compulsion, and the general conduct of the war; collision between Press and Cabinet became a standing feature of the Coalition’s first half-year.

At the close of 1915 the Annual Register records that National Service had “not escaped” public discussion though it remained unsettled. Recruiting for 1916 was already a source of anxiety. The blockade and the partial Declaration of London were under scrutiny. Compulsory military service was left as the chief internal question for the new year — a question the Coalition would meet in 1916 with the Military Service Acts, after the Derby Scheme had shown the limits of voluntary attestation. The first Coalition had been formed to win the war and to produce the munitions the war required; it had not yet decided how far the State would go in compelling men as well as organising shells.

Aftermath

The Coalition of May–June 1915 ended the last purely Liberal government of the long reform era that had run from the People’s Budget through the Parliament Act and the Irish Home Rule crisis. It did not end Asquith’s Premiership; that would wait until the further reconstruction of December 1916. It did, however, fix the pattern of wartime British politics: multi-party government, a Ministry of Munitions under Lloyd George, Kitchener still at the War Office, and a public argument over compulsion that would dominate the following year. The Easter Rising of 1916 and the military-service legislation would test the Coalition on Irish and recruiting ground; the gallipoli evacuation would close the Eastern enterprise that had helped to break the old Admiralty. For the moment, in the summer of 1915, the achievement was simpler and more constitutional: the war would be fought by a national ministry, not by a single party.
