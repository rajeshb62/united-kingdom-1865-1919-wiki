The Allied Counter-Offensive (1918)

From mid-July 1918 the Associated Powers reversed the strategic position created by the German Spring Offensive. Marshal Foch, already Generalissimo of the Allied armies in France, opened a continuous counter-stroke on 18 July against the Marne salient; on 8 August the British Fourth Army under General Sir Henry Rawlinson, with the French First Army, broke the German front east of Amiens; successive British, French, American, and Belgian assaults then drove the enemy from the Hindenburg system and out of Flanders, Picardy, and Champagne until the Armistice of 11 November. The Annual Register of 1918 treats the sequence as “The Victory of the Associated Powers” — naval and economic pressure first, then the land offensives that closed the war. Newbolt’s naval history insists that the final collapse was mainly the fruit of command of the sea and the economic strangulation of Central Europe, of which the military triumphs were the last expression. For Britain the campaign was the work of Haig’s armies under Foch’s supreme direction, and of a Coalition still headed by Lloyd George.

From Marne Counter-Stroke to Continuous Advance

The last German offensive of the early summer — the attempt to cross the Marne — was, in the Register’s judgment, the weakest of the series. It was promptly stopped by the French. On 18 July Marshal Foch began the great counter-offensive he had been quietly preparing for many weeks. Early that morning French and American troops struck a tremendous blow against the right flank of the German army whose advanced divisions had crossed the Marne. The offensive ran on a front of nearly thirty miles from Fontenoy (on the Aisne, west of Soissons) to Belleau, a few miles west of Château-Thierry. The initial assault was delivered without artillery preparation; the infantry were preceded by a large fleet of tanks. On the first day the French and Americans advanced at least three miles on the whole front, and at certain points as much as eight.

Within a few days the essential difference between this Allied offensive and the previous German ones became apparent. The advance of the Allies was a continuous movement, whereas the German advances, formidable though they had been, had been series of brief isolated rushes, each brought to a complete halt within a few days. The Allies never executed quite such swift rushes as the Germans in their most effective offensives, but from 18 July onwards the Allied armies were never really stopped. Their advance was resistless.

Progress continued on 19 July, especially between the Aisne and the Ourcq under General Mangin, and south of the Ourcq under General Dégoutte. In the first two days 360 guns and 17,000 prisoners were taken. On 22 July the French recaptured Château-Thierry; the threat to the new German salient south of the Marne became so serious that the enemy beat a speedy retreat to the right bank. The Allies won crossings near Dormans; French troops fought northwards, retook Fère on 28 July, forced the Ourcq, and by that date the Germans were everywhere behind the lines from which they had started on 15 July. Seventy-one German divisions were reported engaged in the Reims–Soissons region between 15 July and the end of the month. At the end of July British contingents reinforced the French on the Ourcq. On 2 August the Allies re-entered Soissons; two days later the Germans retreated behind the Aisne and the Vesle. German Headquarters Staff seem then to have realised that they would henceforth be compelled to wage a purely defensive struggle: they ordered spontaneous retirements to defensive positions near Montdidier and also on the Ancre in front of the British.

8 August: Rawlinson and the Battle of Amiens

On 8 August an Allied offensive was launched on a new sector — the blow which Ludendorff later called the black day of the German army, and which Hankey’s biographer places as the opening of the “hundred days.” The assault was delivered on the line from Morlancourt (north of the Somme) to Braches on the Avre, by the British Fourth Army under General Sir Henry Rawlinson and the French First Army. The immediate object was to drive the enemy back from his positions near Amiens, where his presence had constantly hampered communication between the French and British armies ever since the March offensive.

The British broke through at the first assault; cavalry and light tanks pursued the fleeing Germans. At some points an advance of seven miles was made on the first day; in five days the Fourth Army captured 400 guns and nearly 22,000 prisoners. On 10 August the French, who had extended their offensive southwards, recaptured Montdidier; on the 15th they carried the Lassigny massif, which dominated Roye and Noyon.

Byng, Bapaume, and the Advance to Arras–Soissons

On 21 August a new offensive was set in motion by Sir Douglas Haig. The attack took place on a front of ten miles north of the Ancre; the troops were those of the Third Army under General Sir Julian Byng. The initial attack was aided by fog and there was no artillery preparation; tanks played a great part. On the next day the offensive was extended southwards and Albert was taken. Meanwhile the French co-operated on the right; Chaulnes, Nesle, and Roye were retaken. The British Fourth Army also came into action between Byng and the French, so that by the end of August the entire Allied line from Arras to Soissons was steadily advancing. On the 29th New Zealand troops took Bapaume and the French captured Noyon. During August the British captured 57,318 prisoners and 657 guns.

Drocourt–Quéant and the Hindenburg Line

It was at this stage, after the Allies had been attacking and advancing successfully for six weeks, that the most decisive blow of the entire summer campaign was struck. In their retirement east of Arras the Germans had reached the elaborate defensive line constructed on chosen ground when they executed their voluntary retirement in the early spring of 1917 — the “Hindenburg Line,” of which the northern portion now reached was called the Drocourt–Quéant Switch Line. The Hindenburg Line was the strongest part of the entire German defensive system in France; the Drocourt–Quéant section was considered the key of the famous line and was deemed invulnerable.

At the end of August the First Army under General Sir Henry Horne came into action north of Byng. The right wing of the First Army was composed of the Canadian Corps commanded by Lieut.-General Sir A. W. Currie. After advancing for several days the Canadian Corps and the extreme left of the Third Army were brought up against the Drocourt–Quéant Line. There was no halt. At 5 a.m. on 2 September the 1st and 4th Canadian divisions, together with the 4th English division (forming part of the Canadian Corps), went to the attack; on their right the 52nd, 57th, and 63rd divisions — the left wing of the Third Army — co-operated. The attack was assisted by forty tanks of the 3rd Tank Brigade, motor machine-gun units, and Canadian cavalry. The offensive was entirely successful: advancing rapidly south of the Trinquis Brook, the Canadians and their comrades captured the key positions of the famous Drocourt–Quéant Line.

The Germans were so weakened by the successive blows showered upon them that they were unable to make any really formidable attempts to reconquer the Switch Line; from this point onwards, through the remaining two months of the campaign, their retreat became continually more rapid until finally it was little better than a rout. After taking Dury, Villers, and Cognicourt on 2 September, the Canadians pressed on and were soon threatening Cambrai from the north-west; German divisions operating south of those defeated at Quéant beat a rapid retreat to the main portion of the Hindenburg Line. Péronne fell on 1 September.

Americans, Argonne, Flanders, Ostend

On an entirely different part of the line, the German front south of Verdun had formed since 1914 a pronounced salient with its apex at St. Mihiel. The American Army was stationed in this region; on 12 September General Pershing sent his troops to destroy the salient. In two days the entire salient was flattened out — an advance of nearly twenty miles in the centre — and the Americans captured 200 guns and 15,000 prisoners. The German General Staff alleged a voluntary retreat long contemplated; the number of guns captured went far to disprove that the retirement was a matter of choice.

On 26 September the Americans and the French Army under General Gouraud carried out a great combined offensive in the Argonne, on a front of forty miles west of the Meuse. The Americans advanced to a depth of seven miles, freeing many villages; further advances followed in the next days. On 28 September a new offensive opened in the extreme north by a combined British and Belgian army under the supreme command of King Albert; the British contingent was the Second Army. The offensive ran on a front of twenty-three miles from Dixmude to Ploegsteert. On the first day Dixmude, the Forest of Houthulst, Stadenberg, Westroosebeke, Passchendaele, Moorslede, Gheluvelt, and Messines were captured. After this there was never again any deadlock in north-western Belgium.

During October the retreat became a rout all along the line. In three days at the end of September the British captured 300 guns on the Cambrai front alone. On 29 September the French got across the Bazancourt–Challerange railway, long an objective of French offensives in Champagne. The British captured over 700 guns and about 65,000 prisoners in September; in the two months from 8 August the Canadian Army alone captured 500 guns and 28,000 prisoners; during September the French, Americans, and Belgians together captured about 900 guns.

St. Quentin fell to the French on 1 October; on the 9th and 10th the British entered Cambrai and Le Cateau. French forces moved up north took Roulers on 14 October. During the next few days the advance in Belgium was very rapid. King Albert’s army met with but feeble resistance and pursued the enemy across the flats. The Germans evacuated Ostend on 17 October; that afternoon Sir Roger Keyes, commanding the Dover Patrol Force, landed and took possession of the town, Belgian soldiers also entering from the south. Lille was entered by the British Fifth Army (now under General Birdwood) on 17 October; on the same day the British took Douai. In the next three days the Germans abandoned the entire Belgian coast and withdrew their right wing along the Dutch frontier; they evacuated Bruges on 20 October.

The further east the British, French, American, and Belgian armies progressed, the weaker became the opposition. On 2 November British troops entered Valenciennes. Further south a Franco-American army began an offensive between the Aisne and the Meuse the same day and completely cleared the Argonne; in two days the Americans advanced thirteen miles, and on 6 November they entered Sedan. Negotiations for an armistice with Germany were opened, but this did not cause a halt in the British or Franco-American advance. Early on 11 November Canadian troops entered Mons — so that, from the British point of view, the war ended at the spot where it began.

Italian and Macedonian Side-Theatres

In the last phase the Italian armies were not idle. At the end of October a general offensive against the decomposing Austro-Hungarian army was undertaken. Special interest attached to the Italian Tenth Army, composed of Italian and British troops under Lord Cavan. On 26 October this army forced a crossing of the Piave at the island of Grave di Papadopoli, about ten miles below the Montello. Further north the 8th and 12th Armies forced passages at many points. The defence was weak except where German-Austrian or Magyar contingents still retained their morale; Lord Cavan’s troops advanced nine miles north-east of the Piave in four days. His advance obliged the Austrians further south to fall back to avoid being outflanked, enabling the Italian Third Army to cross the lower Piave at San Donà di Piave. On 30 October the Eighth Army captured Conegliano; up to that date the combined forces had taken over 33,000 prisoners. On 1 November advanced guards of cavalry crossed the Livenza. Two days later the Austro-Hungarian Government capitulated; an armistice was signed on 3 November and took effect on 4 November. Between 24 October and 4 November the Allied armies on the Italian front captured 300,000 prisoners and 5,000 guns. On 3 November Italian military and naval forces landed at Trieste. The Italian war ended with a victory much more complete than most observers had thought probable since the defeats of the previous autumn.

The final developments of the war also afforded dramatic justification of those strategists who had always contended that it was advisable to attack the Central Powers in the Near East as well as in Western Europe. The first of the mighty series of blows which brought down Central Europe in ruins in the autumn of 1918 was delivered by the Salonika Army — whose very existence as such had given rise to much criticism, and which had almost exclusively marked time since 1915 — now commanded by General Franchet d’Esperey in succession to General Sarrail. On 15 September the Allied forces — French, Serbian, British, Greek, and some escaped Jugo-Slav volunteers — delivered an offensive on the largest scale. The chief assault was made by the French and Serbians; the French pierced the Bulgarian front between Dobropolje and Vetrenik. In the first two days 4,000 prisoners and 30 guns were captured. On the 18th the French reached the Tcherna, ten miles behind the Bulgarian lines; the same day British and Greek contingents attacked near Lake Doiran to prevent transfer of reinforcements. Within a few days panic ensued in the Bulgarian ranks; French horsemen reached Prilep on the 23rd and Ishtip two days later; British cavalry drove the Bulgars towards the Strumnitza. On 29 September the French entered Uskub. The Bulgarian army completely collapsed; M. Malinoff applied to General d’Esperey for an armistice, granted on severe terms including immediate evacuation of Greek and Serbian territory, demobilisation, and surrender of arms. Bulgaria’s fall was the first rupture of the Central Alliance. After the capitulation of Bulgaria, Turkey followed: an armistice was signed at Mudros on 30 October and came into operation the next day, opening the Dardanelles and providing for occupation of the Bosporus forts.

Character of the Victory

The Annual Register opens its victory chapter by insisting that the Associated Powers brought crushing pressure from all directions — sea, air, economic sphere, and finally land. The strictly military pressure did not become menacing, or even very formidable (apart from the original Russian offensive), until July 1918; the naval and economic forces had begun to cause deadly distress and strangulation in Central Europe at a much earlier date. Even at the end it was mainly the naval power of the Entente which brought about the final collapse, for the technical and mechanical superiority which the Allied armies eventually enjoyed was itself the result, in the main, of their freedom to communicate with the resources of the whole world. Newbolt’s Naval Operations develops the same theme: the victory was not a single land battle but the cumulative effect of blockade, shipping control, and the failure of the U-boat campaign, of which the continuous advance from 18 July and the Amiens breakthrough of 8 August were the military expression. The entire line from the North Sea to Nancy was driven back; great assaults were delivered in rapid succession in all parts of the front; and the German retreat became a rout. The campaign closed with the Armistice of 11 November — complete military surrender under terms that left Germany no power to renew the war — while at home the Coalition under Lloyd George turned from war to the franchise already widened by the Representation of the People Act and to the “coupon” election that followed.
