The Military Service Acts and Conscription (1916)

The Military Service Acts of 1916 ended the British tradition of a purely voluntary army in the greatest of modern wars. After a year and a half of recruiting by appeal, attestation, and the last great voluntary canvass known as the Derby Scheme, the Coalition under asquith-herbert-henry redeemed its pledge and brought unmarried men of military age under compulsion. A second Act, debated through the spring almost to the end of May, placed recruiting upon a still broader compulsory basis. The passage of the two measures ranked, with the Irish Rebellion and the reconstruction of the Government, among the outstanding domestic events of the year.

The Strain on Voluntary Recruiting

From the first months after the outbreak-of-the-great-war, the War Office had lived upon the extraordinary flood of volunteers. By early 1915 that flood was no longer enough. When the House of Lords re-assembled on 6 January 1915 to hear kitchener-horatio, the Secretary of State for War could still report that recruiting at home had proceeded on “normal lines,” and that over 218,000 names of persons willing to serve had been registered by the Parliamentary Recruiting Committee; the shortage of officers had been made good by more than 29,000 appointments. The language was reassuring, and deliberately unspecific.

By mid-September the tone had changed. Parliament re-assembled on 14 September with National Service uppermost in members’ minds. asquith-herbert-henry refused Captain Guest a day for discussion and would say only that compulsory service had “not escaped the attention of the Government.” John Dillon declared strong opposition unless an overwhelming case were made out, and criticised officers who had published in The Times a manifesto for compulsion. The Prime Minister begged officer-members to remember that they did not sit as representatives of the Army.

The next day, 15 September, Kitchener told the Lords that provision for the forces in the field during 1916 had caused him anxious thought, owing to the recent falling off in the numbers coming forward to enlist. A large addition to the recruit supply would be needed, and he hoped for an early solution of the problem. On the same day in the Commons the Prime Minister asked for a further Vote of Credit of £250,000,000 — the seventh since the outbreak of war, bringing the seven together to £1,262,000,000 — and noted that the average daily net expenditure was running rather over £3,500,000. The war was consuming men and money at a rate that made voluntary recruiting alone look increasingly precarious.

The autumn also saw open collision between Ministers and the Northcliffe Press, whose papers had combined criticism of the Government with advocacy of compulsory service and had led the agitation that helped produce the Ministry of Munitions. Sir John Simon, then Home Secretary, denounced in the Commons a Daily Mail map of 11 October, “The Road to India,” as a dangerous instrument in enemy hands — a railway sketched from Berlin through Vienna to Constantinople and Asia, finger-posted “to Suez and India,” already reproduced in a German newspaper for propaganda. The year closed with National Service still unsettled though “not escaped,” with recruiting for 1916 already a source of anxious thought to the Secretary of State for War, with the daily cost of the war over three and a half millions sterling, and with the solution of compulsory military service left as by far the most important subject of internal settlement for 1916.

The Derby Scheme and the Unattested Residue

The last great voluntary effort was Lord Derby’s canvass. The Scheme ran from 23 October to 15 December 1915. The understanding was explicit: if any considerable proportion of unmarried men remained unattested when it closed, Compulsory Service would follow. The report of the result, issued with the New Year, showed that the unattested residue could by no means be called a “negligible quantity.”

The grand total of men of military age in the country was estimated at 5,011,441. Of these, attested, enlisted, and rejected men amounted to 2,829,263, leaving an unaccounted residue of 2,182,178, of whom 1,029,231 were set down as unmarried. Deducting 378,071 as an estimated figure for badged, reserve, and indispensable men, the number of single men left over amounted to 651,160. On all hands it was agreed that the first business of Parliament must be to redeem the pledge given by Asquith and introduce Compulsory Military Service.

Sir John Simon’s Resignation and the First Act

The end of 1915 left the country in considerable ferment over compulsion. Prophecies of a revolutionary movement among the working classes, and of an internal condition more unsettled than for a very long time past, had been freely made. Those anticipations were altogether dissipated by the reception accorded to the measure on its introduction at the beginning of the year.

The only dissentient member of the Cabinet was Sir John Simon. His resignation was announced on 1 January 1916. When the House of Commons met on 4 January, Asquith officially confirmed that Simon had resigned the office of Home Secretary. The greater part of that day, however, was occupied by lloyd-george-david, then at the Ministry of Munitions, defending his department from criticism, including the suppression of the newspaper Forward.

The first Military Service Act thus came in as the redemption of a public pledge, not as a sudden conversion. It dealt first with the unmarried men whose failure to attest had broken the Derby Scheme’s bargain. The country that had dreaded industrial revolt found, in the event, that the measure was accepted as a necessity of war. Compulsion arrived not as a party triumph of the Northcliffe campaigners of 1915, but as the Coalition’s reluctant fulfilment of a condition that Asquith himself had attached to the last voluntary canvass.

Tribunals, Hardship, and the Working of the Act

Acceptance in principle did not mean smooth administration. On the last day of February 1916, on the third reading of the Consolidated Fund Bill, the House returned to the difficulties and misunderstandings that had already gathered round the Act. Simon opened the debate by complaining that the Local Tribunals had failed to give effect to the Government promise that young men who were the sole support of their mothers should not be sent into the Army. He declared that, notwithstanding War Office undertakings, excepted men were still being bluffed into attesting. The muddle, he argued, had arisen out of Mr. Tennant’s statement that single young men rejected under the Derby Scheme must be re-examined. He quoted cases in which rejection certificates had been torn up by Recruiting Officers, and others in which the certificates had never been delivered at all. Mr. Pringle adduced further instances of the same character.

Tennant admitted that his earlier answer had been unfortunate. The Recruiting Offices had not kept lists of the rejected since 14 August, and for that reason the “Yellow Form” had been sent out indiscriminately to all men of the prescribed qualifications. Every endeavour was being made, he said, to transfer married men liable to service into reserved occupations so that single men might be set free for military duties. Mr. Long assured the House that he would do everything in his power to prevent hardship to widowed mothers and other dependants of single young men.

The Tribunals were attacked from the opposite direction as well. On 2 March Lord Derby told the House of Lords that the charge against them was that they had given altogether an unnecessary number of exemptions to attested single men. Between the complaint of over-harshness and the complaint of over-leniency, the new machinery of compulsion settled uneasily into the life of the home front.

Wimbledon, Secret Sessions, and the Second Act

Political pressure for a still broader measure continued into the spring. The last event recorded before Easter was the Wimbledon bye-election. The Coalition candidate, Sir Stuart Coats, supported by both great party organisations, was opposed by Mr. Kennedy Jones, standing as an Independent in general opposition to the Government with the cry “Do it now” against “Wait and see.” The seat, long held by Mr. Chaplin with immense majorities, returned Coats on 20 April by a majority of only 1,811. The result was taken to imply a weakening of the Government’s position in the country; there could be little doubt that hesitation on compulsory military service had cost support even in this traditionally Conservative constituency. The Government soon found itself unable any longer to resist the obvious feeling of the country. The most important business of Parliament after Easter was that of placing the whole subject of recruiting upon a new basis.

The Easter holidays were no sooner over than matters of the utmost importance came before the public. On 25 April the first announcement was made of the outbreak in Dublin — the easter-rising-1916 — and on the same day Secret Sessions were held in both Houses of Parliament to inform members of the facts and figures underlying the problem of recruiting. When the Commons met that day, Asquith called the Speaker’s attention to the presence of strangers, who were ordered to withdraw. In the Lords a Secret Session had already been provided for by an order of the Lord Great Chamberlain excluding all strangers. No report of the proceedings could of course be given; but an official account was afterwards communicated to the Press, in which the proposals of the Government were embodied under three headings.

Before turning fully to the Irish Rebellion, the contemporary record concluded the account of the final adoption of conscription, the discussion of which was carried on in Parliament almost to the end of the month of May. The second Military Service Act completed what the first had begun, extending compulsion beyond the unmarried residue that the Derby Scheme had failed to bring in. Together the two Acts transformed the British Army’s relation to the civil population and made man-power a permanent subject of parliamentary and administrative struggle for the rest of the war.

Sacrifice, Resolution, and Later Man-Power

Looking back from the end of 1916, the outstanding historical events of the domestic year were judged to be the passage of the two Military Service Acts, the Irish Rebellion, and the Reconstruction of the Government. Most classes of the community had been called upon to bear sacrifices little anticipated at the commencement of the war. That those sacrifices would be increased in the new year seemed beyond question: reference was made especially to the scheme for introducing universal national service, and to the plans for reducing the consumption of food. The prospect of these further sacrifices appeared quite powerless to modify the national resolution to prosecute the war to a successful conclusion.

The pressure of prices had already shown how closely man-power and the civilian economy were bound together. The increase in the price of food had given rise to discontent among railway workers, who demanded a 10s. advance in wages; South Wales railwaymen threatened to stop work unless the demand were conceded within a week; and only Board of Trade intervention produced a settlement on 20 September 1916 that raised the previous war bonus of 5s. to 10s. Compulsion of men and regulation of the home front advanced together.

The logic of military compulsion did not end in 1916. In 1917 critics still argued that full advantage was not being attained from the Military Service Acts because they were applied only to a section of the population. The proposed scheme for universal national service, already prominent at the end of 1916 and intended to secure a better utilisation of the man-power of the nation for both military and industrial purposes, remained in the experimental stage through the following year. In the spring of 1918, under the shadow of the german-spring-offensive-1918, the need for fighting men became again acute. When the House of Commons reassembled on 9 April 1918, a feeling of deep anxiety brooded over members. The German onslaught on the Western Front had had serious results for the Allies; the news from Neuve Chapelle spread through the Lobbies some time before the rising of the House; and it was generally agreed in the country that further drafts on man-power would be unavoidable. So urgent was the need considered that lloyd-george-david, now Prime Minister, moved on the opening day the first reading of a new Man-Power Bill — “A Bill to make further provision with respect to military service during the present war” — drafted by a committee of Ministers with Sir George Cave as Chairman. The House waived the customary day’s notice; all other Government business was set aside until the Bill had passed through all its stages. The enemy, the Prime Minister told the House, was committed to an attempt to secure a military decision that year by breaking the Armies of the Allies; seven or possibly eight months of campaigning weather remained; strategic unity under General Foch was a fundamental condition of victory, but success would be impossible without a further supply of men. Several hundred thousand men were rushed across the Channel at the end of March and during April from the great army hitherto kept at Home; British troops were also brought up from Italy, and American regiments were temporarily brigaded with French or British battalions to meet the extreme need of the next few weeks.

Thus the Acts of 1916 were not a single legislative episode but the beginning of a man-power regime that lasted until victory. What had begun as the redemption of Asquith’s Derby pledge became, under the successive pressures of the Irish rising, the reconstruction of the Government, and the German spring offensive, the organising principle of the British home front in total war.

Chronology

  • 1915, 6 January: Kitchener addresses the Lords; recruiting still described as proceeding on normal lines.
  • 1915, 14–15 September: National Service uppermost in the Commons; Kitchener warns of the falling off in recruits for 1916.
  • 1915, 23 October–15 December: Derby Scheme in operation.
  • 1916, 1 January: Sir John Simon resigns; Derby Scheme results published; 651,160 single men left over after deductions.
  • 1916, 4 January: Asquith announces Simon’s resignation as Home Secretary; first Military Service measure the first business of Parliament.
  • 1916, end of February: Commons debate on tribunal grievances and the working of the Act.
  • 1916, 2 March: Lord Derby on exemptions granted by Local Tribunals.
  • 1916, 20 April: Wimbledon bye-election; Coalition majority reduced to 1,811.
  • 1916, 25 April: Announcement of the Dublin outbreak; Secret Sessions on recruiting in both Houses.
  • 1916, to end of May: Final adoption of conscription; second Military Service Act.
  • 1918, 9 April: Lloyd George introduces further Man-Power Bill under the spring crisis in France.
