The Irish Home Rule Crisis (1886–1914)

The Irish Home Rule Crisis was the central political conflict of the United Kingdom from the mid-1880s to the outbreak of the First World War. It revolved around the demand of the Irish Parliamentary Party and the wider Irish nationalist movement for a devolved parliament in Dublin — Home Rule — and the determined resistance of Ulster Unionists, who feared domination by a Catholic-majority Dublin assembly. The crisis split the Liberal Party, brought down its dominant leader, drove the Conservative Party into an alliance with armed resistance, brought the British Army to the brink of mutiny, and was ultimately suspended by the outbreak of European war, only to erupt in armed rebellion two years later.

Gladstone’s Conversion & First Bill (1886)

The demand for an Irish parliament was not new. The Home Government Association had been founded in 1870 by Isaac Butt, and by the mid-1870s a disciplined Irish Parliamentary Party of some sixty members pressed for constitutional devolution. But the issue became a crisis when William Ewart Gladstone — who had already disestablished the Irish Church (1869) and passed an Irish Land Act (1870, extended 1881) — underwent a dramatic conversion to the cause.

By late 1885, Gladstone had become convinced that the only way to reconcile Ireland to the Union was to grant a measure of legislative independence. His son Herbert publicly revealed the “Hawarden Kite” in December 1885, signalling that Gladstone intended to introduce a Home Rule Bill. The General Election of 1885 returned a hung parliament: the Liberals and Conservatives were nearly level, with Parnell’s 86 Irish Nationalists holding the balance.

When Gladstone formed his third ministry in February 1886, he moved swiftly. On 8 April 1886, he introduced the Government of Ireland Bill — the First Home Rule Bill. It proposed a Dublin parliament with two chambers to legislate on Irish domestic affairs, while reserving imperial matters (foreign policy, defence, customs and excise, and coinage) to Westminster. Irish MPs would no longer sit at Westminster.

The Bill split the Liberal Party down the middle. A faction of Liberal Unionists — led by Joseph Chamberlain and Lord Hartington — broke away to oppose the measure. Chamberlain, who had previously advocated a less ambitious “Central Board” scheme for Ireland, resigned from the Cabinet and led the revolt. On the night of 7–8 June 1886, the Bill was defeated in the House of Commons by 343 votes to 313 — 93 Liberals voting against their own government. Gladstone dissolved Parliament and appealed to the country, but the ensuing general election returned the Conservatives and Liberal Unionists with a commanding majority of 118. Lord Salisbury formed a Unionist administration that would hold power — with one brief interlude — for the next nineteen years.

The Liberal split was enduring. The Liberal Unionists, led by Chamberlain and Hartington, gradually merged with the Conservatives, and the Liberal Party would not see power with an independent majority again until 1906.

Parnell’s Fall & Second Bill (1890–1893)

The First Home Rule Bill’s defeat did not extinguish the cause. Charles Stewart Parnell, the “Uncrowned King of Ireland”, remained the dominant figure in Irish politics. But his position was shattered in 1889–1890 by a scandal that convulsed British and Irish politics alike.

In 1887, The Times published a series of articles, “Parnellism and Crime”, alleging that Parnell and his movement were linked to the agrarian outrages of the Irish National Land League. Central to the accusation was a letter purportedly signed by Parnell that condoned the Phoenix Park Murders of 1882. Parnell denounced the letters as forgeries. A Special Commission (the Parnell Commission), sitting from 1888 to 1889, investigated the charges. In February 1889, the forger Richard Pigott confessed under cross-examination by Sir Charles Russell that the letter was a forgery and subsequently fled and committed suicide. Parnell was utterly vindicated, and his popularity surged.

But catastrophe followed swiftly. In December 1889, Captain William O’Shea filed for divorce from his wife Katharine, naming Parnell as co-respondent. The divorce case was heard in November 1890; Parnell did not contest it, and the decree was granted. The news caused a firestorm. Gladstone, fearing that Nonconformist Liberal voters would abandon Home Rule if Parnell remained leader, wrote a letter (published 25 November 1890) making clear that Parnell’s continued leadership would cost the Liberals the next election and therefore the Home Rule cause.

When the Irish Parliamentary Party met in Committee Room 15 at the House of Commons (1–6 December 1890), the party split bitterly. After days of rancorous debate, a majority of 44 MPs withdrew from Parnell’s leadership, forming the anti-Parnellite majority under Justin McCarthy. Parnell retained only 26 loyalists. He fought three by-elections in Ireland in a desperate attempt to regain his position, campaigning with ferocious energy, but his health collapsed. Parnell died at Brighton on 6 October 1891, at the age of 45.

After Parnell’s death Gladstone formed his fourth and final ministry in 1892, reliant on the votes of the now-divided Irish Party. On 13 February 1893, he introduced the Second Home Rule Bill. It resembled the first but retained 80 Irish MPs at Westminster (while limiting their voting rights). The Bill passed the House of Commons on 1 September 1893 by 301 to 267 — on its third reading, the first Home Rule Bill ever to pass the Commons. But the Conservative-dominated House of Lords rejected it overwhelmingly, by 419 votes to 41. With no prospect of overriding the Lords’ veto (the Parliament Act was still eighteen years away), the Bill was dead. Gladstone resigned as Prime Minister in March 1894, and the Home Rule cause entered a decade of political eclipse.

Third Home Rule Bill & Ulster Resistance (1912–1914)

Home Rule returned to the centre of politics after the Liberal landslide of 1906 and, critically, after the Parliament Act 1911 removed the Lords’ absolute veto, replacing it with a suspensory veto of only two years. The Liberals, dependent on the Irish Party’s 84 votes for their majority, were now bound to deliver Home Rule.

The Third Home Rule Bill was introduced by Prime Minister H. H. Asquith on 11 April 1912. It proposed a bicameral Irish Parliament (a Senate and an elected House of Commons) with authority over Irish domestic affairs, while reserving the Crown, imperial defence, foreign policy, and certain other powers to Westminster. Ireland would continue to send 42 MPs to Westminster.

The Bill passed the Commons in each of the sessions 1912, 1913, and 1914 under the provisions of the Parliament Act, but the real drama unfolded outside Parliament. Ulster Unionists, led by the Dublin-born barrister and former Solicitor-General Sir Edward Carson (who had nonetheless become the champion of Ulster’s cause) and the Conservative leader Andrew Bonar Law, mounted a resistance of unprecedented scale.

Ulster Covenant (1912)

On 28 September 1912, Ulster Day, nearly 500,000 men and women signed the Ulster Covenant. The men’s covenant, modelled on the 17th-century Solemn League and Covenant, bound signatories to use “all means which may be found necessary to defeat the present conspiracy to set up a Home Rule Parliament in Ireland” — and, if Home Rule were imposed, to refuse to recognise its authority. The women’s declaration was signed separately. Carson was the first to sign at Belfast City Hall, followed by Bonar Law and other Unionist leaders. The signing was a mass civic spectacle, conducted across Ulster in churches, halls, and market squares.

Ulster Volunteer Force

In January 1913, the Ulster Unionist Council authorised the formation of the Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) , a paramilitary militia raised to resist Home Rule by force if necessary. By the end of 1913, the UVF claimed 90,000 volunteers, drilled openly and backed by a fund of £1 million guaranteed by Ulster’s business and industrial community. The UVF was commanded by Lieutenant-General Sir George Richardson, a retired Indian Army officer, and organised on conventional military lines. Meanwhile, in the South, the Irish Volunteers were formed in November 1913 by nationalists in response to the UVF, initially under the control of moderate constitutional nationalists associated with John Redmond.

The Curragh Incident

By March 1914, the Asquith government faced an impossible situation. The Home Rule Bill was about to receive Royal Assent under the Parliament Act, but Ulster was armed and mobilised. The government decided to reinforce the military garrisons in Ulster to protect arms depots and, if necessary, to deal with any UVF attempt to seize control of the province.

On 14 March 1914, the Cabinet ordered precautionary moves. General Sir Arthur Paget, Commander-in-Chief in Ireland, was instructed to send cavalry and infantry to guard key military stores in Ulster. Paget summoned his senior officers to Dublin on 20 March 1914 and — in what remains a matter of controversy — appears to have told them that “active operations” against Ulster were imminent and that officers who lived in Ulster would be permitted to “disappear” for the duration, while all others who refused to serve would be dismissed rather than simply transferred.

The commander of the 3rd Cavalry Brigade at the Curragh, Brigadier-General Hubert Gough, and 57 of his 70 officers (including all the officers of the 16th Lancers and nearly all of the 4th Hussars and 5th Lancers) stated their preference for dismissal rather than initiate “active military operations against Ulster.” News of the so-called “Curragh Mutiny” exploded in the press.

The government initially capitulated. Colonel John Seely, the Secretary of State for War, and the Chief of the Imperial General Staff, Sir John French, drafted a written assurance to Gough (later amended by the Prime Minister) that the Army would not be used “to enforce the Home Rule Bill on Ulster.” When the Cabinet realised the political implications — that it had effectively conceded that the law could not be enforced — Seely and French were forced to resign. General Gough was nevertheless reinstated.

The Curragh Incident had two profound consequences. It convinced Ulster Unionists that the government would not, in the end, coerce them — and it convinced Irish nationalists that the British state could not be relied upon to uphold the rule of law against Ulster’s defiance. The annual register noted Hankey’s private alarm: “The present situation has already led to the resignation of officers; if prolonged, it will lead to the resignation of more; the supply of officers, already a serious problem, is likely to be even further diminished.”

Larne Gun-Running

Emboldened by the government’s humiliation at the Curragh, the UVF moved to arm itself decisively. On the night of 24–25 April 1914, the UVF landed some 25,000 rifles and 3 million rounds of ammunition at the ports of Larne, Bangor, and Donaghadee. The operation was a model of paramilitary logistics: the arms were shipped from Germany aboard the collier Clydevalley, transferred to smaller boats, and distributed across Ulster within hours by an organised network of motor cars. The authorities were entirely outmanoeuvred, and by the time Dublin Castle learned of the landing, the arms were beyond recovery. The government’s response was limited to the posting of warships on the Ulster coast and a formal condemnation.

The nationalists responded in kind. On 26 July 1914, the Irish Volunteers landed 1,500 rifles and 45,000 rounds of ammunition — smuggled from Germany — at Howth, near Dublin. British troops and police attempting to seize the arms were met by a hostile crowd on Bachelors Walk in Dublin; the troops opened fire, killing three civilians and wounding 38. The incident inflamed nationalist opinion and widened the gulf between constitutional nationalists and the growing undercurrent of revolutionary separatism.

Suspension & Easter Rising (1914–1916)

As Ireland edged toward civil war, the British political establishment made a final attempt at a negotiated settlement.

Buckingham Palace Conference

On 21–24 July 1914, King George V summoned the leaders of all parties to a conference at Buckingham Palace, an extraordinary intervention by the monarch. The conference was chaired by the Speaker of the House of Commons, James Lowther, and was attended by Asquith and Lloyd George for the government, Bonar Law and Lord Lansdowne for the Unionists, and Redmond and Dillon for the nationalists, and Carson and James Craig for the Ulster Unionists. The issue was narrowed to the question of which Ulster counties would be temporarily excluded from Home Rule and for how long. After four days of intense discussion, the conference reached deadlock on the precise boundaries of the excluded area — specifically, whether the counties of Tyrone and Fermanagh (both with narrow nationalist majorities) would be included in or excluded from Ulster’s opt-out.

Two days after the conference collapsed, the nationalist gun-running at Howth and the Bachelors Walk shootings inflamed the situation further. But the crisis was overtaken by events on the Continent.

Outbreak of War & Suspension

On 28 July 1914, Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia; by 4 August, Britain was at war with Germany. The Home Rule Bill received Royal Assent as the Government of Ireland Act 1914 on 18 September 1914, but was simultaneously suspended for the duration of the war by the Suspensory Act. An Amending Bill providing for the temporary exclusion of Ulster — which had been promised by the government in March 1914 — was abandoned.

John Redmond, leader of the Irish Parliamentary Party, pledged the support of the Irish Volunteers for the British war effort, urging them to fight “in defence of the rights of small nations” — a phrase that would be turned back against the British government by Irish republicans. The majority of the Irish Volunteers followed Redmond (becoming the National Volunteers, some 140,000 strong), but a rump of perhaps 10,000–11,000 held aloof, keeping the original title “Irish Volunteers” and preparing for an insurrection.

The Easter Rising (1916)

The suspension of Home Rule and the war in Europe created the opportunity for a republican insurrection. The Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB), working within the rump Irish Volunteers, planned an uprising timed for Easter 1916. On Easter Monday, 24 April 1916, approximately 1,200 Volunteers and members of the Irish Citizen Army seized key buildings in Dublin. Patrick Pearse read the Proclamation of the Irish Republic from the steps of the General Post Office, declaring Ireland an independent republic.

The British response was overwhelming. Martial law was declared; troops and artillery were rushed to Dublin. By 29 April, after a week of heavy fighting that devastated the city centre and left over 450 dead and 2,500 wounded, Pearse surrendered unconditionally. Fifteen of the rebel leaders — including Pearse, James Connolly, and the executed signatories of the Proclamation — were shot by firing squad in Kilmainham Gaol between 3 and 12 May.

The executions proved a catastrophic political miscalculation. Initially, Dublin public opinion had been largely hostile to the Rising; the destruction and loss of life had alienated many. But the summary executions, the internment of thousands, and the extension of martial law transformed the political landscape. The executed leaders were transformed into martyrs, and public sympathy swung decisively toward the republican cause.

Aftermath

The Irish Home Rule Crisis — spanning three decades, four Prime Ministers, and three Home Rule Bills — shattered the Liberal Party, radicalised Irish politics, and destroyed the moderate constitutional nationalism of the Irish Parliamentary Party.

The Government of Ireland Act 1914 remained on the statute book, suspended and never implemented. In 1918, Asquith’s successor David Lloyd George attempted to introduce Home Rule with partition, but the proposal was overtaken by the rise of Sinn Féin — the political party of the Easter rebels — which won 73 of Ireland’s 105 seats in the December 1918 general election. Sinn Féin’s MPs refused to take their seats at Westminster, instead establishing the First Dáil in Dublin in January 1919, which declared Irish independence. The Irish War of Independence (1919–1921) followed.

The Government of Ireland Act 1920, enacted during the war, partitioned Ireland into Northern Ireland (six counties) and Southern Ireland, each with its own devolved parliament. The southern parliament was never effectively implemented; instead, the Anglo-Irish Treaty of December 1921 established the Irish Free State as a self-governing Dominion within the British Empire, with Northern Ireland electing to opt out. The crisis that began with Gladstone’s conversion in 1885 thus ended with the partition of Ireland and the creation of two states on the island — outcomes that neither the Home Rulers nor the Unionists had originally intended.
