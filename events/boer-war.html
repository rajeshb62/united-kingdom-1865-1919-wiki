The Boer War (1899–1902)

The South African War, commonly called the Boer War, was the struggle between the British Empire and the two Dutch republics — the South African Republic (Transvaal) and the Orange Free State — arising from the collision of imperial expansion with Boer resistance. The war passed through three distinct phases: a conventional campaign in which the Boers besieged British garrisons and inflicted a series of shocking defeats; a British counter-offensive that captured the republican capitals and led to the formal annexation of both territories; and a protracted guerrilla struggle that dragged on for nearly two years more before the Treaty of Vereeniging brought peace in May 1902.

Origins and Prelude

The roots of the conflict lay in the long encounter between the British Empire and the Boers — descendants of Dutch Protestants and French Huguenot exiles who had settled in South Africa generations before. The Boers had repeatedly moved their settlements inland to escape British authority, establishing themselves in the Transvaal and the Orange Free State. As McCarthy observes, the Boer “lives apart and unto himself… His one great terror was of English domination. Wherever he set up for himself, it seemed to him that England was determined to follow him, to surround him, and to prevent him from carrying on the sort of life that suited him best.”

The discovery of gold in the Transvaal transformed this encounter into a crisis. Adventurers and settlers from all over the world — the Uitlanders, or Outlanders — flooded into the region, rapidly outnumbering the Boer population even within the Transvaal itself. The Outlanders demanded citizenship rights and a voice in the government of the Republic, but President Paul Kruger, a Boer of the Boers — “undoubtedly a man of great ability, of absolute integrity, firm as a rock” — regarded them with deep suspicion, identifying their demands with English designs on the country.

The antagonism was personified in two great figures. Paul Kruger stood for the old Boer order — religious, stubborn, self-sufficient. On the other side stood Cecil Rhodes — “above all things an adventurer by temperament and by career” — who had risen to become Prime Minister of Cape Colony, founder of the Chartered Company, and the driving force behind British expansion in southern Africa. “Sooner or later,” writes McCarthy, “the influence of President Kruger and the influence of Mr. Cecil Rhodes must come into collision.”

The Jameson Raid (1895)

On 29 December 1895, Dr. Leander Starr Jameson, the administrator of the Chartered Company in South Africa, led a raid from Bechuanaland across the Transvaal frontier with the avowed purpose of compelling President Kruger to do justice to the Outlanders’ claims. A small force of mounted men, including some officers in regular British service, crossed into the Transvaal. The British Government, through the High Commissioner Sir Hercules Robinson, telegraphed instructions to stop the invasion; Jameson refused and pressed on. He was swiftly defeated by Boer forces, and he and his officers were taken prisoner.

President Kruger acted with “wise clemency” — the lives of all the invaders were spared, and the leaders were sent to England for trial. Jameson became for a time a popular hero among certain classes in England, though a Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry later found that Cecil Rhodes, in his capacity as controller of the British South Africa Company, De Beers Consolidated Mines, and the Gold Fields of South Africa, “directed and controlled the combination which rendered such a proceeding as the Jameson Raid possible.”

The Raid had far-reaching consequences. The German Emperor Wilhelm II sent a telegram of congratulation to President Kruger on the defeat of the raiders — a message widely regarded in England as “a compliment to President Kruger and a snub to the English Government.” This Kruger Telegram inflamed Anglo-German relations and became a lasting symbol of German hostility.

The Road to War (1896–1899)

After the Raid, relations between Britain and the Transvaal worsened steadily. In April 1897 the two governments were on the verge of war. Sir Alfred Milner was sent out as High Commissioner; his estimate of the situation led him to recommend a strong policy. Milner and Kruger met at Bloemfontein at the close of May 1899 to discuss the Outlander franchise. Kruger offered some concessions but would not accept the terms Milner demanded. The Bloemfontein Conference broke up without agreement.

Negotiations continued through the summer of 1899. Joseph Chamberlain, the Colonial Secretary, pressed for a five-year franchise for the Outlanders. Kruger ultimately withdrew his qualified offer and reverted to his original seven-year residence requirement. Simultaneously, Boer troops began massing along the frontier; Outlanders started leaving the Transvaal; and the Boer government stopped trains and confiscated gold belonging to Outlander capitalists.

On 9 October 1899, the Transvaal issued an ultimatum demanding that British troops be withdrawn from the borders. The British government rejected the terms, and on 12 October 1899 war was declared.

Conventional Phase (1899–1900)

The war opened with a general advance of the Boer forces. They invaded Natal and laid siege to the British garrisons at Ladysmith, Mafeking, and Kimberley. The British public, McCarthy records, expected a short, sharp campaign: “The general impression amongst these was that the war would be very short, not very sharp, and promptly decisive.” This confidence was founded on a profound misunderstanding of the enemy. The Dutch Boers were “descended from the race who held out so long against Spain” and had shown at Majuba Hill (1881) that “in fighting capacity they were not unworthy of their heroic Dutch ancestors.”

General Sir Redvers Buller arrived in Natal on 25 November 1899. In December, Lord Roberts was appointed Commander-in-Chief in South Africa, with Lord Kitchener as his Chief of Staff; Buller remained in command of the Natal Army, with Sir Charles Warren leading the Fifth Division under him.

Black Week (December 1899)

The week of 10–17 December 1899 became known as “Black Week” for the British. At Stormberg, at Magersfontein, and at Colenso, the British suffered three consecutive defeats in the space of a few days. At Colenso on 15 December, Buller’s attempt to cross the Tugela River failed disastrously; Lord Roberts’s son was mortally wounded while trying to save the guns. The Boers, fighting on ground they knew, from behind rocks with rifles they had used since boyhood, proved far more formidable than the British command had anticipated. As McCarthy acknowledges, “it very soon became apparent that the Boers, however limited their numbers, were well prepared for the war, and that they were animated by a spirit of desperate resistance.”

The British Counter-Offensive

The British responded by mobilising on a massive scale. The obstacles — “rivers and mountain ranges, and the apparently limitless stretches of country” — were such as could be overcome only by commanders of the highest gifts. Lord Roberts and Lord Kitchener reorganised the British forces and launched a counter-offensive. Cronje’s army was surrounded and forced to surrender at Paardeberg on 27 February 1900. On 13 April Roberts entered Bloemfontein, the capital of the Orange Free State.

Relief of the Besieged Towns

The sieges of Ladysmith, Kimberley, and Mafeking became the focus of intense public emotion in Britain. When the relief of Ladysmith and, above all, the relief of Mafeking were “successfully and brilliantly accomplished by the British forces, there was an outburst of national enthusiasm which could not have been exceeded if the success on the British side had been that of another Waterloo.” The word “Mafeking” itself entered the English language as a term for exuberant patriotic celebration.

Annexation of the Transvaal and Orange Free State

On 28 May 1900, the Orange Free State was formally annexed and renamed the Orange River Colony. At the end of the same month, President Kruger fled from Pretoria. Lord Roberts entered Pretoria and hoisted the British flag there on 5 June 1900. The Transvaal was also annexed to the British Crown.

A very general impression prevailed in England that the war was substantially at an end. “Yet the war was by no means at an end,” McCarthy notes. “It was for the most part a guerilla warfare on the side of the Dutch and of the Orange Free State, and such a war might be carried on for an almost indefinite time even after all the strongholds of the invaded States had been occupied by the enemy.”

Guerrilla Phase (1900–1902)

After the capture of Pretoria, the war entered a new and more difficult phase. The Boer commandants, led by able and determined commanders — Louis Botha, Christiaan De Wet, and Koos de la Rey — dispersed into the vast countryside and carried on a mobile guerrilla war. “De Wet,” writes McCarthy, “had a positive genius for sudden surprises, unexpected attacks, and instantaneous disappearances, and had given immeasurable trouble to the British Commanders by the mere fact that they never knew where to have him.”

The Boers were ideally suited to this kind of warfare. “The Boers were men trained from their early boyhood to just that kind of work… The Boers had to make their living in great measure by the pursuit of game, and every one was trained to the use of the rifle.” The British forces, by contrast, included many new levies who had to learn a completely unfamiliar style of campaigning.

The guerrilla phase stretched across an area “nearly equal to the area of Continental Europe.” The British commanders adopted a strategy of relentless pursuit, building a network of blockhouses to control the countryside and restrict Boer movement. By 1902, Mr. Brodrick could report in Parliament that “the blockhouse system had had excellent results” — the safety of the railways was now practically secured, and large portions of the country had been freed from the ravages of war. The remaining Boer forces in the field — perhaps 2,000 men each under De Wet, Botha, and Delarey — were steadily pressured by Kitchener’s columns.

Concentration Camps

As the guerrilla war continued, Lord Kitchener adopted a policy of clearing the countryside. Farms were destroyed to deny supplies to the Boer commandos, and the women and children of the Boer population — as well as black Africans — were gathered into camps established along the railway lines. These were originally described as “places of refuge for those willing to submit,” to which “burghers may take themselves, their families, and their stock for protection.”

This was, as the Annual Register records, “the beginning of the concentration camps, of the conditions of life and the mortality in which much was to be heard later in the year.” Terrible conditions in the camps led to a humanitarian outcry. In the House of Commons debate of June 1901, the camps were denounced as “an outrage against humanity.” Emily Hobhouse’s narrative of conditions in the camps moved public opinion profoundly. Campbell-Bannerman, the Liberal leader, denounced the “barbarous” system of the concentration camps, and the issue became one of the great political controversies of the war. The camps remained a deeply controversial aspect of Kitchener’s command, with high mortality rates from disease, particularly among children.

Peace and Aftermath

The Treaty of Vereeniging

By the spring of 1902, the Boer leadership recognised that further resistance was hopeless. The Boer “Governments” — the acting government of the Transvaal under Schalk Burger and the Orange Free State government under President Steyn — entered into negotiations with Lord Kitchener and Lord Milner. A peace conference was held on 15–16 May 1902 at Vereeniging, where sixty delegates from the two republics gathered under safe-conduct from the British military authorities.

The Boer delegates initially sought to preserve some form of independence — proposing a British protectorate with the surrender of foreign relations — but Kitchener and Milner refused to negotiate on any basis that admitted the question of independence. The British terms, as revised by Mr. Chamberlain, were presented for a “Yes” or “No” vote.

The terms of surrender were signed at Pretoria on 31 May 1902 by Lord Kitchener and Lord Milner on behalf of the British Government, and by the representatives of the two Boer Governments — including Generals Botha, Delarey, and De Wet. The terms included: the absorption of the Transvaal and the Orange Free State into the British Empire; no death penalty for Boers who had fought for their country; the use of the Dutch language in law courts where necessary; and a liberal grant of £3,000,000 and loans to enable Boer farmers to re-establish themselves.

The End of the War

When the terms were signed, Lord Kitchener congratulated the Boers on “the stout and gallant struggle they had made” and welcomed them in their position as citizens of the British Empire. General De Wet, in his later book Three Years’ War, dedicated the volume “To my fellow-subjects of the British Empire.” The thanks of the King and Parliament were voted to the army, navy, Indian and Colonial forces, and to Lord Kitchener, who “had dealt with ninety mobile Boer columns over an area nearly as large as the European Continent.” Kitchener sailed for England on 23 June 1902, and the three years’ war was at an end.

Hobson: measure and type

J. A. Hobson’s Imperialism: A Study, dated August 1902, does not narrate the campaigns recorded above by McCarthy, the Annual Register, and Langer. It uses South Africa as a measure of the new Imperialism and as a type of costly annexation. “Quibbles about the modern meaning of the term Imperialism are best resolved by reference to concrete facts in the history of the last thirty years.” His compiled table for 1884–1900 includes Rhodesia under the British South Africa Charter (1889: 750,000 square miles) and the Transvaal and Orange River Colony (1900: 119,139 and 48,826 square miles). For so small a nation to add in one generation some 4,754,000 square miles is “a historical fact of great significance”; one-third of the Empire by area has been acquired within that generation. The new lands are tropical or sub-tropical, thinly settled by whites, held by protectorate, charter, or paramountcy rather than genuine colonisation. “No substantial settlement of Britons is taking place upon any of the areas of the Empire acquired since 1870, excepting the Transvaal and the Orange River Colony.”

If the Imperialism of the last three decades is “clearly condemned as a business policy” — “enormous expense” for “a small, bad, unsafe increase of markets” — it remains “rational enough from the standpoint of certain classes.” Financiers who gained from the Jameson Raid recouped themselves, in his account, by war contracts and by “freezing out” smaller Transvaal interests; they “are essentially parasites upon patriotism.” Hicks-Beach estimated the wars in South Africa and China in the three years to 31 March 1902 at £165,034,000, of which some £119,614,000 was added to the Debt. “The enormous expenditure upon the South African war” would be followed by a demand for a large permanent increase of army and navy outlay. “The South African war, openly fomented by gold speculators for their private purposes, will rank in history as a leading case of this usurpation of nationalism.” The “abnormally rapid swelling of financial needs due to the disastrous policy in South Africa” would, he thought, precipitate an Imperial Zollverein dressed as federation, exploiting colonial loyalty shown in the war. This diagnosis sits beside, and does not replace, Langer’s diplomatic origins and McCarthy’s military narrative.

Consequences

The war had profound consequences for the British Empire. The Canadian and Australian colonies had rendered “splendid service” throughout the conflict, and the war brought about “closer and more cordial relations between the Colonies and the mother country” — a significant step on the road toward Imperial federation and Dominion status.

But the war also left deep scars. Its policy “and many of its conditions” had provoked “serious doubts and misgivings” among many Englishmen. The concentration camps controversy, the immense cost in blood and treasure — at its peak the war cost £5,500,000 a month — and the revelation of British military unpreparedness all made the Boer War a turning point in British politics and military reform. The ex-President Kruger and President Steyn went into exile; the Boer leaders Botha and De Wet later visited England, where they were received by King Edward VII and presented by Lord Kitchener to Mr. Chamberlain at Southampton in August 1902, though they subsequently endeavoured in correspondence to reopen various points of the Vereeniging Agreement, particularly the amnesty for Cape rebels.
