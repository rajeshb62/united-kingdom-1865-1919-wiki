The German Spring Offensive (1918)

In the spring of 1918 the Central Powers struck their last great blow on the Western Front. After the treaty of Brest-Litovsk the Germans transferred large masses of their Eastern Armies westward; once they had finally imposed their will upon Russia, some 70 per cent. of the Eastern Army seems to have been moved. The new offensive stood under the supreme direction of von Hindenburg and von Ludendorff; the tactical details, the Annual Register records, appear to have been devised by General von Hutier, who had distinguished himself at Riga the previous year. Von Hutier brought German tacticians back to assault by enormous masses of men combined with artillery barrage, rifle-fire, flame-throwers, tanks, and other devices more perfectly than anything yet seen. It was against the British Army that the full force of the first blow fell — a crisis that followed the long attrition of Passchendaele, tested the Coalition under Lloyd George, and, by forcing unity of command under Foch, prepared the ground for the Allied counter-offensive that closed with the Armistice.

Extension of the British Line

The British front had been lengthened. The British had taken over a further section from the French, and the front now extended south of St. Quentin as far as Barisis — the first extension since December 1916, when the French section of the old Somme battlefield had been taken over. The new sector was 28 miles long; the total British front was 125 miles. In view of subsequent events the announcement that the British had taken over the St. Quentin line was of peculiar interest.

Hankey’s biographer records the political and military history of that extension. At the Boulogne Conference in September 1917 the principle that the British line should be extended was accepted, though distance and timing were left to the two commanders-in-chief. Haig and Pétain met at Amiens in mid-October; Haig, though reluctant to divert strength from Flanders, accepted extension as far as Barisis (between St. Quentin and Soissons, just south of the Oise). The German counter-attack at Cambrai at the end of November and the need to reinforce the Italians after Caporetto caused Haig to postpone execution. Clemenceau and Pétain pressed for a still longer extension to Berry-au-Bac on the Aisne; the British refused that larger demand but completed the take-over to Barisis by the end of January 1918. Hankey, after visiting the front with Smuts in January, opposed further extension south and judged the Army “in splendid fettle” and able to “hold against any attack,” though he held it would not be right to expose it to another prolonged attack of the Somme–Flanders type — confidence later judged over-optimistic on the defensive side.

At home the winter was cast as a civilian campaign. On 1 January the Prime Minister appealed for War Bonds, economy, and work as the home firing-line; submarine sinkings in 1917 had cost over 6,000,000 tons of merchant shipping (British net tonnage down some 3,500,000). When Parliament opened on 12 February the King’s Speech insisted that until honourable peace principles were recognised the war must be prosecuted with all vigour. In the Debate on the Address, Asquith pressed for information about the Supreme War Council at Versailles and whether it had assumed executive powers; Lloyd George refused detail that would inform the enemy, but assured the House that the extension of the Council’s powers had been adopted unanimously by military as well as civil Allied representatives — Russia’s elimination having completely changed the Western balance.

21 March: The Blow

It was known many weeks before that the German General Staff were preparing an attack by far the most terrible yet seen in the war. At dawn on Thursday, 21 March, an intense bombardment opened along over 50 miles of the British front, from the Scarpe to the neighbourhood of the Oise. Haig’s despatch, among the Public Documents of the Annual Register, records that shortly before 5 a.m. a bombardment of great intensity, with gas and high explosive from all natures of artillery and trench mortars, opened against practically the whole fronts of the Fifth and Third Armies from the Oise to the Scarpe, while road centres and railways as far back as St. Pol were engaged by high-velocity guns. By about 9.45 a.m. a general attack had been launched on a 54-mile front between the Oise and the Sensée. Favoured by a thick white fog which hid S.O.S. signals from artillery and machine-gunners, and in numbers which made loss of direction impossible, the German infantry forced their way into the foremost defensive zone. Until 1 p.m. the fog made it impossible to see more than 50 yards in any direction.

The Germans obtained a considerable numerical superiority over the Fifth Army, west of St. Quentin, and, partly for that reason and partly because the method of attack was both new and ingenious, broke clean through the British lines at the first attempt. The preliminary bombardment, though short, was so intense that the defensive works in the advanced lines were completely destroyed, and the Fifth Army under General Sir H. Gough was compelled from the first day to execute a rapid retreat. The first German report claimed 200 guns and 16,000 prisoners on 21 March; the Register finds nothing improbable in those claims. The British Third Army under General Sir J. Byng, north of the Fifth, was obliged to fall back. During the next few days the German advance was extremely rapid.

Haig’s despatch sets the British order of battle on the morning of 21 March: twenty-nine infantry divisions and three cavalry on the original battle front, nineteen infantry in line (Third Army eight in line and seven in reserve; Fifth Army fourteen divisions and three cavalry, three infantry and three cavalry in reserve). Launched on about 54 miles, the offensive spread northwards on 28 March until from La Fère to beyond Gavrelle some 63 miles were involved. On that front seventy-three German divisions engaged the Third and Fifth Armies and the right of the First, opposed at first by twenty-two British infantry divisions in line with twelve infantry and three cavalry in close reserve. Before the end of March eight further British divisions came south; prior to 9 April four more were engaged, making forty-six British infantry and three cavalry divisions on the Somme battle front.

Amiens, Arras, and the Check on the Somme

The strategic object of the first blow was to break the British–French junction and open the road toward Amiens and the Channel. In the last days of the advance upon Amiens Allied losses remained serious; on 29 March the Germans reported 70,000 prisoners and 1,100 guns, of which no fewer than 40,000 prisoners and 600 guns had been taken by von Hutier’s army, which had first broken Gough west of St. Quentin. North of Montdidier the Germans were not more than twelve miles from Amiens.

On 28 March the enemy attacked north of the Scarpe intending to capture Arras, but completely failed — partly because his new method had now been realised by the British, and partly because he was not, as at St. Quentin, favoured by thick fog. In the first days of April the rush was stemmed at least temporarily; advances became local, and French and British reinforcements arrived. The British Government largely depleted the great army kept at Home; several hundred thousand men were rushed across the Channel at the end of March and during April, and troops came up from Italy. The United States decided that numerous partially trained American regiments in France, not yet ready as separate Divisions, should be temporarily brigaded with French or British battalions to meet the extreme need for man-power.

On 4 April the Germans attacked again east of Amiens with new reserve divisions, but gains were of the slightest local significance; all main roads to Amiens were held, and between Hamel and the Roye road even advanced Allied positions held firm, only villages such as Morisel and Mailly-Raineval being surrendered. Public confidence in France and England in the security of Amiens and of Anglo-French communications somewhat revived. The German rush had depended mainly on speed; once the pace slowed at the end of the first week, the Allies ceased mere resistance to a continuous flood. Haig records that by early April the hope of opening the Amiens road ended in an exceedingly costly repulse; with the failure of 4 and 5 April the Somme battle-front offensive ceased for the time being. The price was a considerable sacrifice of ground and a great expenditure of reserves — material to the fighting farther north.

Haig attributed the Fifth Army’s retirement first to inadequate forces for so strong an attack: the front’s extent made adequate reserves at all threatened points impossible; vital sectors had to be secured and risks accepted elsewhere. In the northern and central portions no ground could be given up; in the southern sector alone, over the area devastated in the enemy’s retreat of spring 1917, ground could be yielded under extreme pressure without the same consequences, and French reinforcement was easier there than elsewhere on the British line. He therefore held it unsound to keep considerable reserves south of the Somme while the enemy’s commitment of reserves was still unknown. The front south of the River Omignon, moreover, had been taken over only some seven weeks before the attack — too short a period for an efficient scheme of defence.

Man-Power and Foch

When the House of Commons reassembled on 9 April a feeling of deep anxiety brooded over the members. The German onslaught had had serious results; news from Neuve Chapelle spread through the Lobbies; further drafts on man-power were judged unavoidable. So urgent was the need that Lloyd George on the opening day moved the first reading of the new Man-Power Bill (drafted by a committee of Ministers under Sir George Cave), the House waiving a day’s notice; all other Government business was set aside until the Bill had passed every stage. Submitting “A Bill to make further provision with respect to military service during the present war,” he set forth the critical situation: the enemy was clearly committed to a military decision this year by breaking the Armies of the Allies — assault piled upon assault, with seven or possibly eight months of campaigning weather. He gave the first authoritative review of the recent fighting and of the events which led to Foch’s appointment to co-ordinate Allied strategy. Strategic unity was fundamental; success would still be impossible without more men.

Up to this date British and French forces in France had been entirely separate armies, though for many months there had been agitation for a single Generalissimo — a reform which had met strong opposition notwithstanding that it was known to be favoured by the British Prime Minister. Under the intense stress Lloyd George obtained the Cabinet’s general consent to placing both armies under a French general; on 16 April it was announced that General Foch had been appointed “Commander-in-Chief of the Allied Armies in France.” A Register footnote records that Foch had been in supreme command, for all practical purposes, since 26 March. Hankey’s chapters place the unity-of-command debate and Foch’s elevation as Generalissimo in the calm before the storm and the March–April crisis, with Lloyd George’s conflict with the military leadership and Hankey’s mediating role running through April and May.

The Lys and Armentières

Before the Somme battle had fully settled, a fresh German offensive opened farther north. Haig’s despatch describes the Lys fighting: progress about Armentières; strong attacks toward Nieppe and Neuve Eglise; recovery and loss of Messines; withdrawal from Nieppe to Pont d’Achelles and abandonment of Hill 63 and remaining Messines positions; critical days about Merville, Pacaut, and the La Bassée Canal; later, loss of Kemmel village and Mount Kemmel and readjustment of the Ypres salient on the night of 26–27 April to the line Pilckem–Wieltje–west end of Zillebeke Lake–Voormezeele. Bailleul fell on 15 April; on the 16th the enemy gained Messines Ridge; on 26 April Kemmel village and Mount Kemmel were lost. On 29 April, after a bombardment of exceptional intensity, strong attacks from west of Dranoutre to Voormezeele were completely repulsed with the heaviest losses. In the first days of May violent attempts to extend the Armentières successes were driven back with terrible losses. Thus the battle ended, as Amiens had done, in what it is legitimate to describe as a German defeat: preliminary successes had not won the great strategic object, and territorial gains had been bought at a cost the enemy could ill afford.

Soissons and the Exhaustion of the Offensive

This was not yet the end of Ludendorff’s attempt. After the failure on the Channel ports there was a pause of several weeks; at the end of May fighting broke out afresh north of the Aisne, west of Reims. The attack of 27 May, supported by numerous tanks, extended about 30 miles from Vauxaillon to the vicinity of Reims. Defending troops were mainly French, with several British divisions on the Allied right; the British held, but most of the French line was overwhelmed in the first rush. The enemy gained five miles on the first day, crossed the Aisne and the Vesle, captured Soissons with Brandenburg troops, reached the outskirts of Reims without taking the city, and by 31 May reached the Marne. Allied reserves arrived; the advance slowed; further extensions followed, but by early summer the German effort was spent. German Headquarters claimed, between 21 March and the end of June, 2,476 guns, 15,024 machine-guns, and 191,454 unwounded prisoners, of whom 94,939 were British. The Register closes the phase as “End of the German Offensive” and opens the next as “The Victory of the Associated Powers.”

Through July and into early August Marshal Foch prepared the counter-stroke which brought victory. At Westminster the five weeks from 1 July to 8 August were a period of intense feeling under a war still undecided. The military turning of the tide, Hankey’s biographer places under the second battle of the Marne and planning for the final campaign, ran into the Battle of Amiens of 8 August and the “hundred days” that followed. For Britain the spring crisis had meant extension of the line, the near-rupture of the Fifth Army, the Lys losses, Foch’s elevation, and the Man-Power drive that drained the Home army and brigaded unready American regiments into the line — the last German bid for a decision before the Allied counter-offensive and the Armistice, under a Coalition lineage running back to the first Coalition of 1915 and a franchise already widened by the Representation of the People Act.
