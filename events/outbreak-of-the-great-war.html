The Outbreak of the Great War (July–August 1914)

The Great War came upon Great Britain with a suddenness that belied years of naval preparation, yet it closed a political year already darkened by the irish-home-rule-crisis, bye-election losses, Labour and Nationalist pressure, and the Ulster and Army crises of the spring. Within twelve days the Cabinet that on Friday, 24 July 1914, was still debating Fermanagh and Tyrone found itself at war with the German Empire; the Fleet concentrated for a test mobilisation stood at war stations in the North Sea; and Parliament, cheered by a Vote of Credit of one hundred million pounds, placed the resources of the Empire at the disposal of a struggle which Mr. Asquith described as one of the greatest in history. The unfinished Irish and Welsh measures were disposed of only by wartime suspensory arrangement; Sir Edward Carson transferred Ulster energies, under protest, to the national cause; and Earl Kitchener of Khartoum took the War Office as the recruiting and military organisation of a long war began.

The Austrian Note and the Crisis in Cabinet

Churchill’s later narrative of the Admiralty places the Cabinet of Friday, 24 July, still immersed in the Irish boundary question when Sir Edward Grey brought in the Austrian note to Serbia. The note was clearly an ultimatum of a severity seldom seen in modern diplomacy; its arrival cut across the domestic quarrel at the very moment when the Curragh incident, the Larne gun-running, and the Buckingham Palace Conference had shown how thin was the line between constitutional agitation and force in Ireland. The Irish crisis had divided Cabinet and Army while Europe moved toward war, and had delayed decisions on any continental commitment.

Within hours the European capitals were in motion. Serbia’s acceptance of nearly all the Austrian demands seemed for a moment to open a path of escape. By Sunday, 26 July, Austria was not satisfied; the First Sea Lord, Prince Louis of Battenberg, had already, on Churchill’s instruction, told the Fleet not to disperse after its practice mobilisation; and Grey viewed the situation very gravely. At Grey’s and Sir William Tyrrell’s insistence the Admiralty published a communiqué announcing that the First Fleet at Portland would not disperse for manœuvre leave and that the vessels of the Second Fleet would remain at their home ports. The notice appeared in the Monday papers under the heading “British Naval Measures” — the first public signal that Britain was holding her sea power in readiness under the doctrine of command-of-the-sea.

The Cabinets which now met daily or twice a day found an overwhelmingly pacific majority. Churchill estimated that at least three-quarters of the members were determined not to be drawn into a European quarrel unless Great Britain herself were attacked. Against this current Grey held to two fixed points: the obligation involved in Belgian neutrality, and the danger that would arise if France were left to face Germany alone. Churchill pressed the naval precautions which the Fleet’s concentration made possible. On 30 July he obtained authority for the Fleet to pass the Straits of Dover by night and take up its war stations, and the great ships moved north in secrecy and darkness.

Finance Collapses as Diplomacy Ends

The Chronicle of the last days of July and the first of August fixed the sequence of financial and diplomatic collapse that closed the old order. On 31 July the London Stock Exchange was closed by order of the Committee, and the Bank rate raised to 8 per cent. On 1 August the Bank rate was advanced to 10 per cent.; the National Penny Bank suspended payment; Germany declared war on Russia and France. On 2 August German invasion of Luxemburg and France began; prayers were offered for the nation throughout the United Kingdom; the Admiralty called out the Naval Reserves; there was a patriotic demonstration before Buckingham Palace; and a partial Moratorium was decreed by Royal Proclamation.

On the verge of war the Cabinet met twice; divisions of opinion previously existing among Ministers disappeared in the course of the day almost entirely. The King held a Council at 4.30 p.m.; the Government took control of all wireless telegraphy; a moratorium of a month was established for certain bills of exchange; and the Admiralty called out the Naval Reserves, including naval and marine pensioners under fifty-five, and the Royal Volunteer Reserve. On the announcement of that last step a crowd of some 6,000 marched up the Mall to Buckingham Palace and sang the national anthems of Great Britain and France.

Next morning London awoke to a new kind of Bank Holiday. The morning papers announced the Germans’ violation of Luxemburg, their invasion of France, and their ultimatum to Belgium; crowds gathered outside Buckingham Palace and Whitehall, impartially cheering Ministers and Unionist leaders. A large section of Liberal opinion was still uncertain. Many condemned all wars as criminal; many more held that the Foreign Office was prejudiced against Germany, and abhorred fighting to preserve the balance of power or to strengthen Russia. Protests against departure from neutrality were specially noticeable in the Manchester Guardian and the Daily News; manifestoes came from the Bishops of Lincoln and Hereford, the British Neutrality Committee, and a Cambridge group who called war against Germany in the interest of Russia and Servia “a sin against civilisation.” Those divisions, the Annual Register records, were rapidly closing as knowledge of the real position grew.

Grey, Belgium, Ultimatum, and Declaration of War

On 3 August came the abandonment of Cowes Regatta by special desire of the King; a German ultimatum to Belgium; Royal Proclamations authorising the Admiralty to requisition British ships and forbidding the export of warlike stores; and Sir Edward Grey’s statement in the House of Commons.

The decisive turn had come with the German violation of Luxemburg and the German ultimatum to Belgium. The appeal of the King of the Belgians that the guaranteeing Powers should uphold the sanctity of the Treaty regarding Belgian neutrality settled the issue for the majority of Asquith’s colleagues. By Monday, 3 August, most of them regarded war as inevitable, though resignations were still expected. Formal sanction was given that morning to the already completed mobilisation of the Fleet and to the immediate mobilisation of the Army. No Cabinet decision was taken, Churchill insists, to send an ultimatum to Germany, to declare war, or to despatch an army to France; those supreme acts “were compelled by the force of events, and rest on the authority of the Prime Minister.”

That afternoon Grey informed the House that the Germans were willing to comply with the British demand that no German warships should be sent into the English Channel; then, with the sombre march of his argument, he carried the assembly with him. Outside the Chamber Churchill asked him what would happen next. “Now,” Grey said, “we shall send them an ultimatum to stop the invasion of Belgium within 24 hours.”

On 4 August an Order in Council placed the railways of the United Kingdom under Government control; the British ultimatum to Germany was delivered; British Army mobilisation was ordered by Proclamation; British Ministers resigned; and the German invasion of Belgium began. Throughout that day the Admiralty lived under the double strain of the expiring ultimatum and the chase of the German battle-cruiser Goeben and light cruiser Breslau in the Mediterranean. Churchill sought authority to engage if the Goeben attacked French transports; the Cabinet adhered formally to the view that no act of war should be committed before the ultimatum expired. “The moral integrity of the British Empire,” Churchill records, “must not be compromised at this solemn moment for the sake of sinking a single ship.” The order was cancelled. The Goeben escaped into Turkish waters.

At 5.50 p.m. the Admiralty warned all ships that the war telegram would be issued at midnight. Mobilisation was complete; every ship was in its station. At eleven o’clock at night — midnight by German time — the ultimatum expired. Along the Mall an immense concourse was singing “God save the King.” The chimes of Big Ben broke over the sound, and as the first stroke of the hour boomed out the war telegram — “Commence hostilities against Germany” — was flashed to the ships and establishments under the White Ensign all over the world. Churchill walked across the Horse Guards Parade and reported to the Prime Minister that the deed was done. On 5 August the German mine-layer Königin Luise was sunk by H.M.S. Amphion and the Third Torpedo Flotilla. The political year of 1914 had ended; the war year had begun.

Great Britain at War: Empire, Vote of Credit, and Kitchener

The war found a Government prepared to withstand the enemy and a Parliament and people whose divisions — on which the Germans had staked their hopes — were rapidly closing. Promises of help poured in from all parts of the Empire. Austrian and German reservists were detained; enemy ships in British ports and within reach of Allied cruisers were seized — twenty on the first day alone — and German oceanic trade was stopped at once. The Fleets that had been brought together at Spithead took up their stations in the North Sea; cruisers went out to protect the trade routes. Horses and motor-lorries were requisitioned for the despatch of the British Expeditionary Force — the opening of what would become the long narrative of the Western Front in 1914 — and a system of secrecy as to movements, loyally observed by the Press and rigorously enforced by authority, settled over the country.

The first day of war in the Commons brought the announcement of ministerial resignations, of the violation of Belgian neutrality, and of the impending Vote of Credit of £100,000,000, which was loudly cheered. Two war measures passed almost without debate: one amending Prize Court procedure, the other empowering the Crown to impose restrictions on aliens with a view to the removal or detention of spies. Late that evening a White Paper (Cd. 1467) was published containing nearly 160 documents of correspondence respecting the European Crisis. It eventually enabled the British people to form their own opinion as to the responsibility for the war.

On 5 August Asquith moved the Vote of Credit. After tribute to Grey’s unremitting efforts for peace, he quoted the German Chancellor’s appeal for British neutrality — free licence, as regards France, to annex French possessions overseas if successful; as regards Belgium, bartering away Britain’s obligation to keep her plighted word. He characterised the proposal as infamous. “The war has been forced upon us.” The Government had thought it both the duty and the interest of Great Britain to go to war — first, to fulfil a solemn international obligation; secondly, to vindicate the principle that small nationalities were not to be crushed by the arbitrary will of a strong and overmastering Power. He asked for £100,000,000 not only for naval and military operations but to assist food supplies, promote trade and industry, relieve distress, and meet all expenses arising out of the state of war. He had taken the War Secretaryship temporarily; Lord Kitchener, with great public spirit and patriotism, had now undertaken it. Kitchener was not a politician; his acceptance did not identify him with any set of political opinions.

The War Council of 5 August, as Churchill records, debated whether four divisions or six should go to France; and Kitchener’s vast recruiting task began at once. In an early military statement he reported rather more than six divisions of British troops and two cavalry divisions in the field, further Regular divisions being organised from units withdrawn from oversea garrisons and replaced where necessary by Territorials who had volunteered for service abroad. Troops were coming from India and the Dominions, and the response at home to the call for recruits had afforded a remarkable demonstration of the energy and patriotism of the young men.

Irish Home Rule Suspended

When the European crisis absorbed the nation, the unfinished Irish and Welsh measures still had to be disposed of under conditions which the Opposition denounced as an abuse of the war emergency. In the House of Lords the second reading of the Home Rule Bill was moved, but ultimately adjourned by 93 votes to 29. Crewe said that any expectation on the Continent of civil war in Ireland had been encouraged quite as much by the threats from Ulster as by any action of the Government; what was important was that the Home Rule Bill and the Amending Bill should come into operation at the same time. Lansdowne complained that the Government were enabling the Nationalists to obtain without a struggle what would otherwise have cost them a very serious struggle. A similar motion adjourning the Welsh Church Bill was carried by 89 to 27. Lord Lansdowne’s Legislation (Suspension during War) Bill was then passed through all its stages.

Sir Edward Carson issued an indignant manifesto to the Ulster loyalists, attacking the Government for taking advantage of the war to pass the Home Rule Bill, but reminding them that their motto, now as always, was “Our Country First.” But they would never have Home Rule — never! Next day the Commons disagreed with the Lords’ amendments to the Suspensory Bill; the Lords gave way. The political struggle of the session thus closed, not by a settlement on the merits, but by a wartime arrangement which left both statutes on the book subject to suspensory provisions, while Carson transferred Ulster energies, under protest, to the national cause — the suspended irish-home-rule settlement that would later be transformed by the Easter Rising and by the debates of the First Coalition.

The Money Market and Emergency Finance

On the adjournment of the first war sitting, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Mr. Lloyd George, made his statement on the financial position. The emergency, he said, was due to temporary causes, largely to the stoppage of remittances from abroad; there was no failure of credit. After conferences at the Treasury it had been decided to suspend the Bank Charter Act in order to economise gold. Condemning the hoarding of gold as helpful to the enemy, he stated that on 7 August Government notes for 1l. and 10s. would be issued, convertible into gold at the Bank of England; postal orders would also be legal tender; the Bank rate would be reduced to 6 per cent.; and the moratorium extended for a month. Mr. A. Chamberlain fully endorsed the statement.

Not a market in the City was unmoved: the machinery of the money-market and the banks, the Stock Exchange, Lloyd’s, the Baltic, the shipping lines, the Corn Exchange — all felt at once the shock of a war for which no peacetime organisation had been prepared. Later work turned on the discrimination of bills, the restoration of the foreign exchanges, and the protection of the Stock Exchange from a forced realisation of securities. Some 70–80 million pounds of securities were hypothecated in respect of pre-war debts; the Government agreed to advance 60 per cent. of their value on 29 July on condition that the banks undertook not to put them on the market till six months after the war. Gold at the Bank rose during the war from 26 to 85.5 million pounds; the loan of 350 million pounds was over-subscribed; in all some 440 million pounds was raised with the Stock Exchange closed. A financial catastrophe, probably without parallel, had been avoided under a system of Treasury control and State guarantee which preserved British credit for the war.

Food prices and the spy danger at once claimed public attention. Sugar jumped from 15s. to 38s. per cwt.; of flour and imported meat there was no shortage. Territorial troops were posted to guard railways, bridges, and waterworks. The Aliens Restriction Act enabled the Government to require all enemy alien residents to register and to restrict their freedom of movement and residence.

Within hours of the war telegram the machinery of a nation at war was thus in motion: Prize Courts, alien restrictions, financial emergency measures, the White Paper, the Vote of Credit, Kitchener at the War Office, the silent preparations for the passage of the Army to France, and the wartime suspension of the Irish and Welsh statutes. The long Victorian and Edwardian peace was over.
