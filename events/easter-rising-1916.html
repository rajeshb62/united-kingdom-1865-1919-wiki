The Easter Rising (1916)

In the week after Easter 1916 a rising broke out in Dublin that the Annual Register ranked among the outstanding events of the year, with the two Military Service Acts and the later reconstruction of the Government. Armed men identified with the Sinn Fein movement seized the General Post Office and other public buildings, proclaimed an Irish Republic, and held the centre of the city against British troops until the last days of April. The rebellion was suppressed under martial law; its leaders were tried by court-martial and shot; Sir Roger Casement, landed from a German submarine on the eve of the outbreak, was later convicted of high treason and sentenced to death. The rising did not overturn British rule in Ireland, but it transformed the politics of irish-home-rule that had been suspended for the duration of the war after the Home Rule crisis of 1912–14, and it forced the Coalition under Mr. Asquith into a new and bitter Irish settlement debate.

Background: Home Rule Suspended and Sinn Fein

Before the outbreak of the Great War Ireland had stood on the edge of civil conflict over the third Home Rule Bill. The irish-home-rule-crisis had armed Ulster and the Nationalist volunteers alike; the Curragh incident and the gun-runnings had shown how thin was the line between constitutional agitation and force. When war came, the Home Rule Act was placed on the statute book but its operation was suspended for the duration. John Redmond pledged Nationalist support to the Empire; recruiting in Ireland became a test of that pledge.

By early 1916 another current was running. As early as 4 March The Times published an article drawing attention to the growth of the Sinn Fein movement and commenting on the neglect of the Government to take strong measures against the disaffection brewing in the country. Military authorities had already, by the middle of 1915, represented to the Government that their recruiting efforts were being frustrated by the hostile activities of Sinn Fein supporters. In February 1916 they pointed out on their own initiative the general danger of the situation; the warning, the Royal Commission later found, fell on unheeding ears. The Chief Secretary, Mr. Birrell, and the system of Irish executive government — a political Chief Secretary in the Cabinet, a largely ceremonial Lord-Lieutenant — would be held primarily responsible for the lawlessness that had been allowed to grow up unchecked.

Casement and the German Arms Ship

On the Tuesday after Easter the Secretary of the Admiralty announced that during the night of 20 April an attempt had been made to land arms and ammunition in Ireland by a vessel disguised as a neutral merchant ship but in reality a German auxiliary, acting in conjunction with a German submarine. The auxiliary was sunk and a number of prisoners were made, among them Sir Roger Casement, who had already acquired an unenviable notoriety since the outbreak of the war by endeavouring to seduce Irish prisoners in Germany from their loyalty to the British Crown. The attempt to land arms was evidently a preconcerted signal for a general rising.

Dublin, 24 April: Outbreak

On 24 April serious disturbances broke out in Dublin. A large body of men identified with the Sinn Feiners, mostly armed, occupied St. Stephen’s Green and took forcible possession of the Post Office, where they cut the telegraphic and telephonic wires. Houses were also occupied in Stephen’s Green, Sackville Street, Abbey Street, and along the quays. In the course of the day soldiers arrived from the Curragh, and fighting immediately began. The rebels made a half-hearted attack on Dublin Castle which was not pressed through. They held up troops on their way from barracks and fired on them from the windows of houses; by the following day they had been driven out of Stephen’s Green with a number of casualties, though they remained in occupation of various important buildings, including the Post Office, the City Hall, and the Law Courts.

A proclamation issued in the name of a “Provisional Government” announced that the new Irish Republic was a sovereign independent State. The signatories pledged their lives and the lives of their comrades in arms to the cause of its army, its welfare, and its exaltation among the nations. The Republic claimed the allegiance of every Irishman and Irishwoman, guaranteed religious and civil liberty, equal rights and equal opportunities to all citizens, and declared the intention of establishing a permanent national government representative of the whole people of Ireland and elected by the suffrages of all her men and women. Until success of arms made such a government possible, civil and military affairs were to be administered by the Provisional Government on whose behalf the proclamation was issued, signed by seven of its members.

Suppression and Martial Law

Reinforcements speedily arrived from Belfast and from England and established a cordon round the centre of the town on the north bank of the river, entirely enclosing the area of the rebellion. By 26 April Liberty Hall, the headquarters of the citizen army and formerly of Mr. Larkin, had been wholly or partially destroyed and occupied by the military. Field guns from the south bank of the Liffey and a gun from the patrol ship Helga attacked Liberty Hall; fires broke out in the Sackville Street quarter which the fire-brigade could not reach because it was fired on by the rebels. By that time the troops had suffered casualties to the extent of about nineteen killed and twenty-seven wounded. Risings of a far less serious character took place in other parts of Ireland — notably at Ardee in County Louth, and at Swords and Lusk near Dublin. On 25 April German warships again crossed the North Sea and bombarded Lowestoft and Yarmouth for about half an hour, doing very little damage; the purpose of the raid was no doubt to cause the British Government to hesitate before sending large numbers of troops over to Ireland.

On 27 April the situation seemed sufficiently serious to justify the Irish Executive in proclaiming martial law over the whole of Ireland. Sir John Maxwell left London that day with plenary powers over the whole country; the Irish Executive placed themselves at his disposal to carry out his instructions. In the Commons Mr. Butcher expressed the view that the Government were stamping out the rebellion with all possible vigour and promptitude. Sir Edward Carson affirmed his satisfaction and said he would gladly join with Mr. Redmond in everything that could be done to put down the rebels. Redmond himself asked leave to express, on behalf of the Nationalist party and, he believed, of the overwhelming majority of the people of Ireland, the feeling of detestation and horror with which they regarded the recent proceedings; he joined with Carson in the hope that no attempt would be made to use the rising as a political weapon against any party.

At midnight on 28–29 April an official communiqué described the progress of suppression. The organised forces of the rebels in Dublin were confined to a few localities, the principal one being the Sackville Street district where they had established their headquarters in the General Post Office. The cordon of troops was gradually drawn closer, though outside it there was not infrequent sniping from houses in various parts of the city.

Surrender

Owing to considerable opposition at barricades, especially in North King Street, it was not until 9 a.m. on 29 April that the Four Courts area was completely surrounded. Throughout that morning the squeezing out of the surrounded areas was vigorously proceeded with. Infantry were greatly assisted by a battery of field artillery under Major Hill, who used his guns against the buildings held by the rebels with such effect that a Red Cross nurse brought in a message from the rebel leader P. H. Pearse asking for terms. The reply was that only unconditional surrender would be accepted. At 2 p.m. Pearse surrendered himself unconditionally and wrote and signed notices ordering the various “Commandos” to surrender. During the evening the greater part of the rebels in the Sackville Street and Four Courts area did so.

Early on 30 April two Franciscan monks informed the military that the rebel leader MacDonagh, declining to accept Pearse’s orders, wished to negotiate. He too was told that only unconditional surrender would be accepted; at 3 p.m., when all preparation for an attack on Jacob’s Biscuit Factory (which he held) had been made, MacDonagh and his band surrendered. In the St. Stephen’s Green area Countess Markievicz and her band surrendered and were taken to the Castle. These surrenders practically ended the rebellion in the city of Dublin. Through the night of 30 April–1 May isolated rebels continued to snipe the troops; during 1 May they were gradually cleared out, and a systematic house-to-house search for rebels and arms proceeded in conjunction with the police. Disquieting reports had come from many counties — Dublin, Meath, Louth, Galway, Wexford, Clare, Kerry — and small detachments had been sent to reinforce exposed points.

Executions, Birrell, and Asquith

By 3 May three of the seven signatories of the Republican proclamation — P. H. Pearse, T. J. Clarke, and Thomas MacDonagh — had already been sentenced to death by court-martial and shot, and from day to day for some time afterwards further executions were announced. When the House of Commons was at last able to discuss the question at length, on 3 May, the sitting was chiefly devoted to Mr. Birrell’s apology for his share of responsibility. He confessed frankly that he had made an untrue estimate of the Sinn Fein movement — not of its character, numbers, localities, or frequent disloyalties, but of the possibility of disturbances of the kind that had broken out, of the mode of fighting pursued, and of the desperate folly displayed by the leaders and their dupes. As soon as the insurrection was over (on 1 May) he had placed his resignation in the hands of the Prime Minister, who had accepted it, “no other course being open to me or to him.” He had subordinated everything in order to maintain unbroken the front of Ireland towards the enemies of the Empire, and in that work he had been gallantly assisted by Mr. Redmond. The insurrection, he said, had been no Irish rebellion, and he hoped it would not be associated with past rebellions in the minds of the Irish people. The House listened with emotion.

Asquith, defending General Sir John Maxwell and the troops, reminded the House that only thirteen rebels had been executed. Apart from one execution for murder, it was only the leaders of the rising who had been subjected to the extreme penalty; two more such leaders were awaiting death, and that would complete the tale of the condemned insurrectionists. In any other case of murder there would be a public trial. He suggested that the rank and file would be “given an opportunity of redeeming what was in their case a venial and more or less pardonable error.” He announced his intention of going to Ireland that same evening to consult the civil and military authorities with a view to some arrangement for the future government of the country.

Casement’s Trial

Sir Roger Casement, whom the Annual Register calls the main instigator, was placed upon his trial in the civil courts. The preliminary hearing of the charge of high treason opened at Bow Street on 15 May; beside him in the dock was a soldier of the Royal Irish Rifles, Daniel Julian Bailey, alleged to have joined the “Irish Brigade” raised in Germany and to have accompanied Casement on the expedition to the coast of Kerry. The two were committed for trial on 17 May; a Grand Jury in the High Court found a true bill on the indictments of high treason. Casement’s trial began on 26 June. The indictment charged him with “adhering to the King’s enemies elsewhere than in the King’s Realm, to wit, in the Empire of Germany.” After a trial of four days he was convicted of high treason and sentenced to death. From the beginning there never could be any doubt as to his guilt, though he was defended with conspicuous ability by Serjeant A. M. Sullivan, K.C., of the Irish Bar. It was not denied that he had gone to Germany and that, with the connivance and assistance of the German authorities, he had endeavoured to seduce from their allegiance the King’s Irish soldiers who were prisoners of war.

Royal Commission and Political Aftermath

A Royal Commission inquired into the causes of the outbreak and into the conduct and degree of responsibility of the civil and military executive in Ireland. It found that the main cause of the rebellion appeared to be that lawlessness had been allowed to grow up unchecked, and that Ireland for several years past had been administered on the principle that it was safer and more expedient to leave law in abeyance if collision with any faction of the Irish people could thereby be avoided — a policy which was “the negation of that cardinal rule of Government which demands that the enforcement of the law and the preservation of order should always be independent of political expediency.” The Commissioners acquitted the Lord-Lieutenant (Lord Wimborne) of responsibility and held the Chief Secretary (Mr. Birrell) primarily responsible for the situation that was allowed to arise and for the outbreak. They remarked, however, that the policy of the Chief Secretary was the policy of the British Government as a whole. Sir Matthew Nathan, the Under-Secretary, was credited with complete loyalty to that policy but was thought not to have sufficiently impressed on Birrell the need of more active measures against sedition. If the Government were convicted of weakness before the rebellion, no such charge could be brought against the action they took after the outbreak.

The political sequel was an attempt, under the Coalition, to find a wartime Irish settlement. Lloyd George was charged with negotiating; proposals involving the exclusion of Ulster and the retention of Irish members at Westminster became the subject of bitter dispute. By late July the negotiations had hopelessly broken down. Redmond denounced any Bill framed at variance with the Lloyd George agreement; Asquith stated that the Government could not agree to the retention of the Irish members in undiminished numbers after the next election, and would not introduce any Bill unless the parties were substantially agreed. Martial law, Asquith later said, proclaimed as a precautionary measure, had not been enforced in practice, and he hoped it might be possible before long to discard even the form of it. Mr. Duke, K.C., Unionist Member for Exeter, was appointed Chief Secretary; the office of Lord-Lieutenant was for the time being not filled up — a restoration of “Dublin Castle with a Unionist Executive” that Redmond said would outrage the feelings of Ireland. He appealed to Englishmen not to let their natural indignation at the wicked outbreak of some two thousand men in Dublin lead them to forget the hundred and fifty thousand Irishmen fighting on the battlefields of France.

The Rising thus left Ireland under a military imprint and a failed settlement, with Home Rule still on the statute book and still in suspense, and with Sinn Fein transformed from a neglected faction into the central fact of Irish politics. The Annual Register’s year-list placed the Irish Rebellion beside the Military Service Acts and the Reconstruction of the Government as the defining domestic events of 1916.
