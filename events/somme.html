The Battle of the Somme (1916)

The great Franco-British offensive of the summer and autumn of 1916 on the Somme was the principal British land effort of the year on the Western Front. It opened after a prolonged preliminary bombardment, became very largely a British responsibility because of the strain Verdun had thrown on the French army, and continued for four months of extremely heavy fighting in which territorial gains remained small while casualties mounted to levels that shocked London and the Empire. The Annual Register of 1916 and the diary of Maurice Hankey together record the strategic design, the first days of the assault, the later use of tanks, the limited advances of September and November, and the contemporary dispute over the scale of British losses. In the calendar of the Coalition year it stood beside the Military Service Acts, the Irish Rebellion, and the reconstruction of the Government as one of the defining facts of 1916.

Strategic Setting

It was common knowledge among the Allies that some strategists held a great offensive on the Western Front to be a mistake, and that a decision should be sought in another direction. The chances of success, these authorities thought, were too small to warrant so hazardous an undertaking; the loss of life would be terrible in failure and even in success. That was not the view of the responsible military chiefs in France and Great Britain. They argued that if the difficulties were greater on the Western Front, that front was also the area where, for geographical reasons, both France and Britain could exert their greatest force — the same logic that had fixed British strategy since the outbreak of the Great War.

The offensive when at last it came was mainly a British movement. The French co-operated but were allotted a minor part — a reversal of the previous September, when the more important operations had been given to the French. The Germans, the Annual Register observed, feared the British more and had concentrated a much more formidable army opposite the British line; the first French assault therefore gained ground somewhat by surprise. The section chosen for the new offensive began several miles south of Arras, north of the river Ancre, and extended to several miles south of the Somme. Between Arras and the Somme the line was held by British troops; south of the Somme the operations were carried out by the French under General Foch.

The German line ran westwards of the small town of Combles and at that point penetrated deeper than almost anywhere else into French territory, the front lines standing over forty miles in advance of the Belgian frontier. The object of the offensive in its first stage was to smash those front lines, drive the enemy back into the Ardennes about Maubeuge, reconquer the greater part of the industrial district lost since 1914, and threaten the German position near Lille and in the north-west. The line of assault fell naturally into three sections: north of the Ancre (British), between the Ancre and the Somme (almost wholly British, and the scene of the most severe fighting), and south of the Somme (French).

Bombardment and 1 July

On 24 June the great preliminary bombardments started on the Somme front. Hankey, full of anxiety over what would happen when the infantry attacks followed, listened apprehensively to the thunder of the guns, which could be clearly heard at Highstead. That same day he lunched with the Asquiths and compared notes on Jellicoe’s Jutland despatch; the Prime Minister called it “the worst despatch I have ever read,” and Hankey was sent to the Admiralty to have it amended. The guns on the Somme continued. Towards the end of June the Cabinet devoted almost its whole attention to Asquith’s endeavours to resolve the Irish question left by the Easter Rising — so much so that on 30 June the War Committee did not get through its agenda.

On 1 July, at 7.30 a.m., the assault was launched along the whole of the selected part of the line. Successive waves of British infantry went “over the top,” to be met by withering crossfire from the machine guns which had survived the prolonged bombardments. Because of the strain which the German offensive at Verdun had thrown on the French army, the Somme battle finally became very largely a British responsibility. It very soon proved, in a phrase Hankey’s biographer records, “both the glory and the grave of Kitchener’s Army.”

On the left wing, between Gommecourt and the Ancre, the British advanced with the rest and after heavy losses entered the German advanced trenches in many places; but in that section the German guns had never been properly mastered, and before evening the enemy succeeded in killing or driving back the British and recapturing their trenches. Although the British losses were heavy north of the Ancre, it was in the centre that the most fearful fighting occurred. The length of the line between the Ancre and the Somme was about twelve miles. Over the whole of this section the British advanced on the first day, the progress most marked on the right, where it was helped by French successes, and least important on the left, where the troops were liable to be enfiladed owing to the failure north of the Ancre. On either side of the main road from Albert to Cambrai the British pressed forward. The villages of La Boisselle, Mametz, Montauban, and Fricourt were seized in the first rush. For seven miles northwards of the Somme the British advanced to a depth of over a thousand yards; northwards again as far as the left bank of the Ancre smaller advances were made. In the first two days the British captured 3,500 prisoners and the French about 5,000, but few guns were taken.

At first the largest advances were made by the French, whose extreme left wing extended across to the right bank of the Somme and whose immediate objective was Péronne. South of the Somme they were fortunate in finding the Germans relatively unprepared — the enemy having deemed the French army incapable of any really serious offensive after its losses before Verdun. On either side of the main road from Bray to Péronne they pushed forward, and in three days advanced to a depth of three miles on a front of about six miles, capturing Frise, Feuillères, Herbecourt, Flaucourt (three miles from Péronne), Assevillers, and Fay. On the third day the French took over 2,000 prisoners. On 4 and 5 July Foch’s troops gained more ground and on the north bank of the Somme captured Hem — the most rapid part of the offensive, during which the French once more used their cavalry, a striking incident, for no cavalry had dared to show itself for many months. By the 9th, in spite of counter-attacks, the French had advanced as far as Biaches, only one mile from Péronne. By about the 10th of the month it became apparent that neither the French nor the British were advancing at the speed necessary for any dramatic success. Speed, the Annual Register insisted, was essential once a fortified line was to be burst.

War Committee Reaction

On 3 July Hankey recorded a War Committee meeting confined to Cabinet members on a finance question, with very strained relations between Lloyd George and McKenna. After the meeting he talked with Balfour and Lloyd George about the offensive, which, as he had fully anticipated, appeared to have almost completely failed, with very heavy losses. “We had 23 Divisions and the Germans only 6 or 7½; we had about 750 big guns and an equal number of field guns. It always seemed to me to be utter folly. I cannot see a trace of any strategical conception in it. To deliver a frontal attack on an entrenched force provided with every possible defensive organisation violates every military principle.” Lloyd George reminded him of his memorandum of December 1914, in which he had argued that no increase in men would enable the Allies to break the front in the west and that victory was only to be obtained in the eastern theatre.

Three weeks later hard evidence enabled Hankey to assess the results more accurately. Esher had written that he thought the battle “the beginning of the end” — which Hankey considered highly optimistic. In truth, he confided to his diary, the 120,000 casualties already suffered in three weeks exceeded the losses in the whole Dardanelles campaign of eight months. The offensive seemed to him “to involve the maximum of effort and the minimum of result,” though he admitted that more had been accomplished than he had expected, and that if the “Caterpillars” were a success the yield might be even greater. A few days later Churchill circulated a strongly worded memorandum about the futility of the huge sacrifice of life on the Somme: the Allies, he argued, could have held the Germans on the front just as well by threatening an offensive as by making one; once the kick was out of an army, the enemy recovered his freedom of movement. The comments on Asquith’s copy of the paper showed that it was very ill received in government circles.

July to November: Pozières, Tanks, Combles, Beaumont Hamel

At the end of July the British made an advance along the main road to Bapaume and took the village of Pozières, lying over two miles from the spot at which the original British line crossed the road. Longueval, Bazentin-le-Grand, and Bazentin-le-Petit were won in the middle of the month; on one occasion the British used cavalry for the first time since 1914 — detachments of the Dragoon Guards and Deccan Horse. The maximum depth of the British advance in July was slightly over three miles. In the same period the British captured altogether fifty-four guns, sixty-six machine guns, and nearly 11,000 prisoners; the French took eighty-five guns, eighty-nine machine guns, and 12,000 prisoners — figures which, in comparison with the numbers engaged, the Annual Register judged not large. Elsewhere on the Western Front the only severe fighting was at Verdun: the Crown Prince of Prussia continued his offensive through the spring, capturing Fort Vaux on 6 June and Fleury and Thiaumont on 23 June; but the British offensive on the Somme paralysed the German effort on the Meuse and in all probability was the chief means of saving Verdun from capture.

Extremely heavy fighting continued throughout August, September, October, and November. The number of casualties was very high, especially among the French and British, who were almost invariably the attacking parties, but the territorial changes were negligibly small. In early September Hankey visited the front and set down his impressions: the Germans were still very strong, dug better than the British, and the prisoners he saw were fine, well set-up, intelligent-looking men; German artillery fire was very heavy though probably not as heavy as the British, and British losses were considerable. He believed Haig’s tactics quite wrong — “It is no good hammering away at the Somme, where the enemy has accumulated vast numbers of men and guns to resist us. No war was ever won by such methods.” Haig ought to open up a new front as a surprise; the “Caterpillars” would provide the means. Hankey did not think the British would see a decisive success in the west that autumn: the Caterpillars were the only chance, and they were being mishandled.

In the September offensive the British employed a new and formidable type of armoured motor-car, nicknamed “tanks.” These tanks were so heavily built that they were invulnerable to anything except a direct hit from a big gun. The first use took place on 15 September — exactly a week after Hankey’s visit. Only sixty were available, and more than half never reached the starting point; their crews had not been fully trained. In the shell-mangled chaos of the Somme they were thrown away, in the judgment of Hankey and Swinton, for the price of a minor tactical success — the capture of the village of Flers. Small wonder that those who had worked for nearly two years on the new development were bitter about the folly and conservatism which had in a few hours undone that inventiveness. At a War Committee meeting Grey said that Hankey’s name ought to be mentioned when the first employment of the tanks was announced. That same day, 15 September, Asquith’s eldest son Raymond was killed in action on the Somme — a blow from which those closest to the Prime Minister, including Hankey, felt he never recovered.

On the Somme the most notable later advance was the capture of Combles on 26 September. The Germans maintained their hold on Péronne. As summer drew into autumn the offensive continued to produce heavy losses for small gains until, in mid-November, the onset of the winter rains brought it to an end. By that time the men were utterly exhausted, and their transport was bogged down in the all-pervading mud of the devastated battlefield. North of the Ancre, where the failure had been complete in July, the villages of Beaumont Hamel and Beaucourt-sur-Ancre were taken on 14 November, over 5,000 prisoners being captured. On the road to Bapaume some progress was made, and at the end of the year the British line ran eastward of the village of Le Sars. On 16 October the dreadful losses were brought home personally to Hankey by the news that his brother Donald, the “Student in Arms,” had been killed while leading his platoon of the 1st Royal Warwickshire Regiment in an attack.

Casualties and Contemporary Judgment

In the western theatre the casualties of the opposing sides were probably approximately equal — which, the Annual Register reminded its readers, meant that they were very unequal in proportion to the total numbers of the respective armies. The German casualties in the unsuccessful assault upon Verdun were very heavy, but these were counterbalanced by the very serious losses incurred by the Franco-British forces in the precisely comparable offensive on the Somme. The absence of official British statistics, though no doubt necessary, led to exaggerated statements; and it was sometimes forgotten that although the battle of the Somme continued for four months, the rate of losses incurred in the first month, July, was not subsequently maintained. “It was not the case that the British had half a million casualties on the Somme.” The aggregate wastage of the white manhood of the British Empire up to the end of 1916 was certainly not much more than one-third that of the manhood of Germany — the latter country having a population only slightly greater.

The only compensation, and it was certainly not a negligible one, was that the heavy British sacrifices contributed to saving Verdun, where German pressure declined in July. In the last two months of the year the French, by a welcome change of tactics, regained most of the lost ground at surprisingly small cost — Douaumont on 24 October, Fort Vaux at the beginning of November, and further gains beyond Douaumont in mid-December with nearly 12,000 more prisoners and over 100 guns. Hankey had never believed in the Somme offensive, though he held some hope that if the tanks were used skilfully they might bring a substantial success. His old friend Colonel Seely, formerly Secretary of State for War, returned on leave from France and described the offensive as “a ghastly and tragic blunder” in which the British had “lost men out of all proportion to the results,” calling British tactics “antiquated” and the general staff “wooden and unreceptive to modern ideas.” “There is much truth in this,” Hankey commented, but the Red-Hatted Staff were in complete control, and until they could be convinced there was nothing to be done. After the summer of 1916 he himself became a reluctant “westerner,” on the ground that it was better to have a second-best plan which the army had its heart in than a more perfect plan which those who had to carry it out did not believe in.

The battle closed without the dramatic rupture of the German front that its designers had sought in July. Verdun had been relieved; a strip of ground between the Ancre and the Somme, and later north of the Ancre, had been taken at enormous cost; tanks had appeared as a new instrument of war, mishandled on first employment yet holding, for those who had conceived them, the promise of a different method. The strategic deadlock on the Western Front remained unbroken into 1917, when the same front would again be the scene of a great British offensive at Passchendaele.
