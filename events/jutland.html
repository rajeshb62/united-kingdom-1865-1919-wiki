The Battle of Jutland (1916)

On 31 May and the night following, in the North Sea off the coast of Jutland, the Grand Fleet under Admiral Sir John Jellicoe and the High Seas Fleet under Vice-Admiral Scheer met in the greatest naval battle since Trafalgar. The battle-cruiser forces of Vice-Admiral Sir David Beatty and Vice-Admiral Hipper opened the action; the main fleets closed in the evening mist; Scheer, finding himself face to face with the whole Grand Fleet, extricated himself by practised battle-turns under destroyer smoke; and through the short northern night he forced a passage home across the British wake. No second Trafalgar followed. Yet when Sir Julian Corbett summed the purely strategical result, Scheer had failed in his object of cutting off and overwhelming part of the British advanced forces, and Jellicoe had increased the Grand Fleet’s large margin of superiority as a combative force, a defence against invasion, and an instrument of blockade. British control of the North Sea remained unbroken. Public judgment, fed by a candid first communiqué and by German claims that reached neutrals first, was another matter.

Strategic Setting and the Eve

The Lowestoft–Yarmouth raid of late April came with something of a shock after the enemy’s long inactivity. Faith in the navy was scarcely shaken, but the Admiralty thought a reassuring pronouncement desirable. It took the form of a letter from Mr. Balfour, First Lord, to the Mayors of Lowestoft and Yarmouth: home forces were about to be redistributed so as to make a repetition of such a raid highly dangerous. Earlier it had been necessary to keep the main fleet in northern waters, able to concentrate against invasion but not to intercept raids; now new construction allowed important forces to be brought south without imperilling preponderance elsewhere.

A redistribution of that character had been under consideration since the beginning of the year. On 17 February, at a meeting of the War Committee which Jellicoe came down to attend, the whole strategical aspect of the naval situation was investigated. The Committee explored a naval offensive on the lines of Lord Fisher’s still-born plan — seizing a German coastal island as an aircraft and submarine base, a landing on the Schleswig coast, or in Denmark if the enemy should violate Danish neutrality, with as ultimate objective the coast of Pomerania within a hundred miles of Berlin. Fisher’s special fleet had been eaten away by the Dardanelles; no troops were available; mines and submarines made Baltic supply harder than ever. The conclusion was to preserve the old expectant attitude, while persevering to goad the High Seas Fleet into the open sea.

On the German side Scheer, since his accession to command, had worked to produce a situation of exceptional tactical advantage — using battle cruisers and light forces to entice similar British units within reach of his battle fleet. The stress of the British blockade had come to be severely felt in Germany. Scheer’s object was not a direct general engagement between entire forces: it was to take the British fleet in detail if possible, destroy a portion of it, and retreat to the protection of the German coasts. The Imperial Admiralty entertained no illusion that the chance was anything but very small; the risks were grave, yet the naval and economic situation had become such that those risks had to be taken.

Battle-Cruiser Action, 31 May

On 31 May Beatty, leading the 1st Battle Cruiser Squadron (Lion, Princess Royal, Queen Mary, Tiger), had been zigzagging at nineteen knots towards the Jutland bank. Eight miles ahead light cruisers were spread in pairs; the 2nd Battle Cruiser Squadron (New Zealand and Indefatigable) was three miles on his port bow; Rear-Admiral Evan-Thomas with the “Queen Elizabeths” (Barham, Valiant, Warspite, Malaya) was five miles astern. At 2.0, believing himself only ten miles short of his assigned position (in fact over fifteen), he signalled for the fleet to turn northward at 2.15.

Hipper, with five battle cruisers (Lützow flag, Derfflinger, Seydlitz, Moltke, Von der Tann), five light cruisers and thirty-three destroyers, had left the Jade at 2.0 a.m. and was about fifty miles east of the Lion. Following him, over fifty miles astern, was Scheer with his two Dreadnought squadrons and the pre-Dreadnought “Deutschlands.” As yet Scheer had no suspicion of the Grand Fleet’s presence. It looked as though Jellicoe’s dispositions were exactly what was needed — if nothing incalculable happened Scheer would have little chance of avoiding the battle for which the British had so long been striving. But the incalculable did happen.

Contact opened through the light cruisers. The Galatea’s signals began to reach Jellicoe about 2.20; Beatty, nearer the scene and much faster than the main battle fleet, came up with Hipper long before the battleships. The run to the south followed — Beatty engaging Hipper’s five battle cruisers while Evan-Thomas’s four fast 15-inch battleships came into the fight. In that phase the Queen Mary and the Indefatigable were destroyed. When Scheer’s battle fleet was sighted, Beatty turned north, drawing the High Seas Fleet after him toward Jellicoe — the “run to the north” that converted Scheer’s intended partial stroke into a meeting with the main British force. The four “Queen Elizabeths” fought a rearguard against superior numbers; the Warspite was badly damaged. The Annual Register called Beatty’s earlier stand an action in keeping with the most dashing traditions of the British Navy.

Fleet Contact and the Main Battle

When at 5.30 the firing died away Jellicoe was twenty-three miles to the northward, with his three Dreadnought squadrons in divisions in line ahead disposed abeam. Rear-Admiral Hood with the 3rd Battle Cruiser Squadron (Invincible, Inflexible, Indomitable) was 21 miles ahead. Spread in advance were the 1st Cruiser Squadron (Defence, Warrior, Duke of Edinburgh, Black Prince) under Rear-Admiral Arbuthnot and the 2nd under Rear-Admiral Heath. Without counting Evan-Thomas’s four ships, Jellicoe had twenty-four Dreadnoughts against Scheer’s sixteen and six older pre-Dreadnoughts. The Germans had no gun heavier than 12-inch, while British main armament ranged from 12-inch to 15-inch; German ships were better protected and carried more submerged torpedo tubes. Jellicoe’s best chance was a smashing blow with his main weapon; Scheer would naturally seek to avoid such a blow, or at least to weaken it.

As reports grew more serious Jellicoe ceased zigzagging, increased speed, signalled prepare for action, and altered course toward Horn Reefs. Urgent messages from the Lion reported that Beatty was engaging. As the battle fleets closed, Hood entered the fray not far in advance of the main body, fell into line with Beatty, and within a few minutes the Invincible — veteran of Heligoland Bight and the Falklands — sank with the famous admiral on board. Arbuthnot’s armoured cruisers pressed into the zone of heavy fire; the Defence was destroyed and the Warrior disabled (later abandoned). The action was fought at about 9,000 to 12,000 yards in misty evening light; during the junction the British battleships had to exercise the greatest care not to mistake, at long range, British for German ships.

The explosion in which the Invincible perished heralded a new phase. A period of manœuvring ensued comparable with Howe and Villaret-Joyeuse in 1794 — but now a tactical contest of days was condensed into hours. Scheer had come suddenly upon his enemy in the act of deployment; instead of throwing him into confusion, he found his own van being enveloped by a superior force ready for action. Persuaded by submarine reports that the Grand Fleet had been split up, he did not as yet realise that he was face to face with the whole of it. Out of the mist fire was coming from about eight points of the compass. Nearly all British ships were firing and getting hits, while they themselves suffered not at all. The Lützow was completely disabled; Hipper was about to board a destroyer to shift his flag. The Derfflinger, with masts and rigging cut to shreds and water pouring through a large hole in her bows, was little better off. So boldly had Captain Heaton-Ellis in the Inflexible led on past the wreck that the rest of the German battle cruisers swerved away to the westward about 6.35.

Completely out-manœuvred, Scheer had no choice but to get his neck out of the noose. To retire in succession was not to be thought of — the turning-point would be a deathtrap. In the German tactical manuals the simultaneous withdrawal was the Gefechtskehrtwendung (battle turn-away), practised so that an inferior fleet could disengage at any moment. The only means of preventing escape was a resolute immediate chase; to baffle pursuit the Kehrtwendung was made under cover of a destroyer attack and a smoke screen.

It soon appeared to Jellicoe that the enemy must have turned away. An immediate turn by divisions to follow was out of the question: it would have placed the fleet open to long-range torpedoes from the enemy capital ships — a danger then new and unmeasured — and would have brought German destroyers directly ahead. A turn in succession would have led the fleet into waters the enemy had occupied, and German capital ships were all believed to carry mines. Scheer retreated south-east as night fell and left the British in possession of the scene of action.

Night and Morning, 1 June

At 9.14 — three minutes before Jellicoe gave directions for night cruising order — Scheer signalled the main body to maintain course S.S.E. ¼ E. at 16 knots, the rallying course toward Horn Reefs. Shortly after 9.30 the van began to lead round to port on that course. Foiled once if not twice in his attempt to get by to the eastward, he could no longer delay another determined effort to cross the enemy’s wake.

The courses of the two fleets converged. Scheer’s advanced-guard cruisers struck the destroyer rear guard where the Castor with the 11th Flotilla had taken station, Commodore Hawksley at the head. Scheer altered a point to starboard at 10.6, but that proved insufficient. Commodore Goodenough with the 2nd Light Cruiser Squadron, keeping touch with the Marlborough (torpedoed, limited to 16 knots), met the 4th Scouting Group (Stettin, München, Frauenlob, Stuttgart, Hamburg) on a converging course. Against the faint glow that still tinged the western horizon the silhouettes of five cruisers took shape, very close. Recognition lights and a sudden close action followed. Throughout the night British light cruisers and destroyers harried the retreating Germans; the pre-Dreadnought Pommern was torpedoed and sunk; the light cruisers Elbing and Rostock met their fate; British destroyers Tipperary, Turbulent, Fortune, Sparrowhawk and Ardent were among those lost. Scheer held the course for Horn Reefs.

High up in the North Sea summer nights are short. All eyes strained to know if there was to be another First of June. As Lord Howe made sure he had the enemy fairly to leeward, so Jellicoe had little doubt he was between them and their bases. If there was any misgiving it was mistrust of the weather: the mists began to thicken again, and a wind was getting up that promised anything but a fine summer day. Away to the northward Hipper’s shattered flagship Lützow was being abandoned, as were Elbing and Rostock.

The only flotilla that could still bar the way was the 12th, forced eastward and northward until nearly thirty miles from the battle fleet. Captain Stirling in the Faulknor led twelve powerful 34-knot destroyers. At 1.45, as the first streaks of dawn woke the black horizon, he made out a line of large ships on a south-easterly course; closing, he recognised German battleships, ordered the 1st Division to attack, and signalled the Commander-in-Chief. The enemy turned away together to avoid the attack. The 12th Flotilla’s torpedoes claimed their share, but Scheer’s main body was not brought to a renewed fleet action. When full day came the High Seas Fleet was no longer in a position from which a general action could be forced.

Communiqués and Public Reception

Jellicoe’s Battle Fleet reached Scapa between 10.30 a.m. and noon on 2 June. That evening he reported the Grand Fleet re-fuelled, at four hours’ notice, and ready for sea. Beatty, answering an inquiry on the night of 3 June, reported New Zealand, Indomitable and Inflexible ready and Lion, Tiger and Princess Royal available if necessary. Merchant vessels that had been lying up put to sea on 2 June without hesitation. Control of the North Sea remained in British hands.

It was not from the purely strategical point of view that a general action could at the moment be judged by either nation. Britain had looked for a decisive encounter that would mark a turning-point and give spectacular proof of maritime supremacy; Germany, without experience of naval war, was moved by ambition and apprehension. A second Trafalgar was, among possible results, the only one which could not have surprised either side; what actually happened was unexpected by both. In the German navy the marked feeling among officers was relief at having met the Grand Fleet and escaped without complete destruction.

Guidance of public opinion differed greatly. German censorship and propaganda were practised with premeditation and mechanical efficiency; British practice treated candour as the most effective influence in the long run. Admiral von Holtzendorff’s dictum that “in life it is not things as they are which decide, but the images people make of them” stands against the First Lord’s first communiqué of the evening of 2 June. That announcement, written by Balfour, reported a naval engagement off Jutland: the brunt had fallen on the Battle Cruiser Fleet and supporting forces, with heavy losses; the German battle fleet, aided by low visibility, had avoided prolonged action with the main British forces and returned to port after severe damage. The battle cruisers Queen Mary, Indefatigable and Invincible, and the cruisers Defence and Black Prince, were sunk; the Warrior was disabled and later abandoned; five named destroyers were lost. No British battleships or light cruisers were sunk. Enemy losses were serious: at least one battle cruiser destroyed; one battleship reported sunk by destroyers; two light cruisers disabled and probably sunk.

Looking back, Corbett judged the announcement candid, accurate and restrained. Yet it caused dismay and even indignation, because it disappointed high hopes without offering any estimate of success or of the resulting naval position. Only six hours later the Admiralty claimed three German capital ships sunk instead of two — substantially true: besides the Pommern and the Lützow, the Seydlitz had sunk in shallow water on the way home and was only raised some days later. But the German Official Report, dated 1 June and published on the 2nd, reached neutral countries at least a whole day before English news. It claimed successful heavy engagements between Skagerrak and Horn Reefs, including the destruction of the battleship Warspite (only damaged), and admitted only Wiesbaden and Pommern lost. Corbett notes the overestimates and the deliberate concealments: nothing of the grounding of Seydlitz, nor of the abandonment of Elbing, Rostock and Lützow. British newspaper comment was at first a chorus of disappointment, moderated by responsible voices pointing to the enemy’s retreat. On 5 June a further Admiralty report stated that a very brief period of contact had sufficed to compel the severely punished High Seas Fleet to seek refuge in protected waters.

Aftermath

On returning to London after the action, Maurice Hankey stressed to Balfour a rather pessimistic letter from Jellicoe about German gunnery and night-fighting arrangements. “Rather discouraging,” commented Hankey, “but Jellicoe is always rather a pessimist.” On 24 June, as the Somme bombardment opened, Hankey lunched with the Asquiths and they compared notes on Jellicoe’s Jutland despatch. Asquith called it “the worst despatch I have ever read” and “everything a despatch ought not to be.” Hankey was commissioned to get it amended at the Admiralty. He saw Balfour, who sent for Admirals Jackson and Jellicoe; as soon as his criticisms were accepted he reported to Asquith — “who was much amused.” On his way out he called on Hall, the Director of Naval Intelligence, who agreed in every detail; the Admiralty, Hankey wrote, had never consulted Hall. Thus did Hankey’s waning confidence in the Balfour–Jackson régime suffer a further decline.

When Jellicoe visited the Admiralty late in June to discuss Jutland, the conference agreed that dispositions had stood the test; disasters were attributed to technical faults of material and design — amounting practically to a vote of confidence in the Commander-in-Chief. Three days after the fleet’s return, on 5 June, Lord Kitchener, bound for Russia, lunched with Jellicoe at Scapa and embarked in the cruiser Hampshire. In a north-easterly gale she struck mines laid by U 75 on the night of 28–29 May — one of the submarines watching British bases during the German sortie before Jutland — and sank with almost all hands. The strategic result of the battle and the political shock of Kitchener’s death fell upon the country within the same week.

Scheer had failed and been unexpectedly entrapped into meeting the Grand Fleet; he extricated himself at considerable expense, yet both before and after the main action he had inflicted more serious losses than he could ever have contemplated. Jellicoe had outmanœuvred and surprised the High Seas Fleet; the net result was to increase the Grand Fleet’s large margin of superiority. After Jutland the High Seas Fleet was for some months unequal to covering a serious attack on the Dover Straits; Scheer’s and von Holtzendorff’s opinions brought the official decision restricting fleet action to support of the submarine campaign. When first reports vanished, Corbett observed, the true result was as clear as a well-drawn balance sheet: the High Seas Fleet had inflicted loss on a battle-cruiser squadron and escaped with its life, but control of the sea was irrevocably in British hands. Jutland had ended one stage of the naval war and defined the form of the final struggle — from the outbreak of the Great War through the Dogger Bank action of 1915 into the long attritional struggle for command of the sea, while the armies locked on the Western Front prepared the Somme offensive.
