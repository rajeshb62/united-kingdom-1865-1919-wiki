The General Election of 1906 & Tariff Reform

The general election of January–February 1906 produced one of the most decisive Liberal landslides in British electoral history, returning a government with a majority of 134 over all opposition groups combined. The contest was the climax of a three-year political crisis triggered by Joseph Chamberlain’s campaign for Tariff Reform, which split the Unionist party, drove the reunited Liberals back to power, and cleared the way for a decade of social reform legislation.

The Tariff Reform Split

The rupture within the Unionist party began on May 15, 1903, when Joseph Chamberlain, the Colonial Secretary and the most formidable Radical-turned-Imperialist of his generation, delivered a speech at Birmingham that, as the Annual Register recorded, produced “a condition of astonishment and perplexity bordering on stupefaction” in the public mind. Chamberlain proposed that Britain abandon unilateral free trade and adopt a system of imperial preference — reciprocal tariffs with the self-governing Colonies — to consolidate the Empire commercially. If the Colonies offered to favour British goods, Britain must be free to reciprocate: “We will, nevertheless, recover our freedom, resume that power of negotiation and, if necessary, retaliation whenever our own interests or our relations between our Colonies and ourselves are threatened by other people.”

On the very same day, Prime Minister Arthur Balfour was defending the repeal of the corn duty to a deputation led by Henry Chaplin. Balfour denied that the duty had ever been intended as protective, declared that a preferential scheme was not yet possible, and treated Chamberlain’s proposals as a “remote and very doubtful contingency.” The simultaneous appearance of these two contradictory positions — the Colonial Secretary calling for a revolution in fiscal policy while the Prime Minister dismantled the very tax that might have been its starting point — left the country bewildered.

The Annual Register noted that Chamberlain “was widely held to possess a unique claim on the respectful attention of his countrymen,” but within the Unionist party the divisions were immediate and severe. Among Conservatives there was “a considerable prevalence of theoretical Protectionist opinion,” but vigorous sections — especially among younger Conservatives and Liberal Unionists — discerned “the social and economic danger of perpetual food taxes and the taint of Protection.” The Spectator, the principal weekly organ of thoughtful Unionism, emerged as an energetic opponent. The Liberal party and Liberal press threw themselves “with almost complete unanimity” into opposition.

Chamberlain remained in the Cabinet for several more months, but the fissure proved irreparable. On September 9, 1903, he resigned from the government to campaign publicly for his fiscal programme. The Duke of Devonshire, the Liberal Unionist leader, also left the Cabinet. The Unionist party was now openly divided into three factions: Chamberlainite Tariff Reformers, Balfourite moderates who favoured a flexible but undefined “fiscal reform,” and Unionist Free Traders who adhered to orthodox Cobdenite principles.

Chamberlain’s Campaign (1903–1905)

Chamberlain threw himself into a nationwide speaking tour of extraordinary intensity. His first campaign closed on January 18, 1904, at the Guildhall in London. The Times reported that “he intermits [his campaign] now on the highest level he has yet reached.” Four thousand hearers were admitted by ticket; the enthusiasm of his welcome “was immense, drowning a band which was struggling to perform the National Anthem.”

In his Guildhall speech, Chamberlain argued that free imports — as distinct from genuine free exchange — had not produced the promised benefits. “We had never had free exchange; we had had free imports instead, which was a very different thing.” He pointed to the rising exports of Germany and the United States, the decline of British industrial supremacy, and the imperative need to maintain the Empire as a commercial unit. He drew historical parallels with Holland, Venice, and the Hanseatic cities — commercial powers that had decayed when they lost their industrial base.

The Tariff Reform League, founded in 1903, became a formidable propaganda machine, organising demonstrations, publishing literature, and pressing candidates to commit to the cause. The Unionist Free Traders, led by the Duke of Devonshire and the Spectator, fought back through the Unionist Free Trade Club. The Annual Register of 1904 recorded that the Liberal party, “disunited for twenty years” on the Home Rule question, now found a unifying cause in opposition to Protection. The fiscal question “engrossed the public mind to such an extent as very perceptibly to draw off interest from” other questions.

By-elections told a consistent story. Liberal candidates made steady gains, and even in constituencies where Unionists held on, the reduced majorities reflected the accumulating discontent. The Stalybridge by-election of January 1905 saw the Liberal majority increase from 81 in 1900 to 951 — a result the Annual Register attributed “apart from the discontents accumulating against the Government” to Lancashire’s conviction that “the interests of that great industry would be prejudicially affected by any tampering with” free trade in cotton.

Through 1905, the Unionist government staggered from crisis to crisis. The Chinese labour controversy in the Transvaal — the importation of indentured Chinese coolies to work the Rand gold mines under conditions that Liberals denounced as “Chinese slavery” — became a powerful moral rallying cry. The Education Act of 1902 had alienated Nonconformists. The government’s Irish policy was in disarray after the resignation of George Wyndham as Chief Secretary. By the autumn, as the Annual Register noted, “articles pointing to the Premier’s resignation” appeared in the Times and the Daily Telegraph.

The 1906 Election

On Monday, December 4, 1905, Arthur Balfour resigned the premiership. The King invited Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman to form a government, which he did by December 10. The new Cabinet was remarkable: H. H. Asquith at the Exchequer, Sir Edward Grey at the Foreign Office, Richard Haldane at the War Office, David Lloyd-George at the Board of Trade, John Morley at the India Office, and John Burns — the first working-class man to enter a British Cabinet — at the Local Government Board. The Annual Register observed that the composition of the new ministry “was favourably regarded by public opinion.”

The election campaign began at once. The Annual Register described it as “the most exciting and startling since 1880, if not since 1832.” The reversal of roles was complete: the Opposition (the departing Unionists) were now “arraigned on charges of reckless extravagance, misjudgment of South African affairs, the introduction into the Transvaal of ‘Chinese slavery,’ the endowment of denominational education, and the propounders of crude and self-contradictory ‘fiscal reforms.‘” The Liberals, formerly the party of criticism, now posed as reformers, “claiming to be the real Free Traders and apostles of economic progress.”

The fiscal question was probably the most important single issue, especially in rural constituencies, with “Chinese slavery” next, and then the modification of the Education Act of 1902. The Duke of Devonshire, in a letter to the Unionist Free Trade Club, declared that the Tariff Reform League’s proposals were “indistinguishable from pure Protection.” Chamberlain attacked the new ministry as “essentially a Home Rule and Little Englander Government which must exist, if at all, by Irish votes.” Balfour, in his address to Manchester, denounced the Liberal programme as “surrounded with obscurity” and warned that Home Rule, Disestablishment, and the destruction of voluntary schools “had lost none of their ancient charm for Radical lawmakers.”

Campbell-Bannerman’s address contained no specific programme and no mention of Home Rule. He attacked the Unionist record — wars and adventures abroad, reckless finance, confused Army reorganisation — and declared that “Protection was immoral, oppressive and corrupting.”

The campaign was notable for its intensity. The Churches issued special prayers; the Free Church Council sent ministers travelling through the country by motor car delivering Liberal speeches; full-page newspaper advertisements defended Chinese labour; cartoons, “especially those against Chinese labour, aroused lively indignation.”

The Liberal Landslide

Polling began on January 12, 1906, and the scale of the Unionist defeat was apparent from the first returns. The Annual Register recorded the results as they came in:

  • London: Unionists lost heavily, including five seats on a single day (January 16). Cheltenham, Brighton, two divisions of Liverpool, Wednesbury, and West Bromwich fell to the Liberals.
  • Key defeats: Mr. Lyttelton, the former Colonial Secretary, was defeated at Leamington; Mr. Chaplin, the venerable Protectionist, at Sleaford; Mr. Brodrick at Guildford; Sir W. Hart Dyke, the “Father of the House,” at Dartford — the last to a Labour candidate.
  • Glasgow: Five of seven divisions passed to Liberal or Labour candidates.
  • Tyneside and the West Riding: Heavy Liberal gains in precisely the industrial districts to which Chamberlain had appealed.
  • Birmingham: Alone among industrial centres, Birmingham held solid for the Unionists, generally with increased majorities.

In two seats — Greenwich and King’s Lynn — the Unionist vote was split between a Free Trader and a Tariff Reformer, with the Free Trade Unionist (Lord Hugh Cecil in Greenwich) coming bottom of the poll.

The election ended on February 13, 1906, with the declaration of the result for Orkney and Shetland. By January 30, 666 of the 670 results were already known.

Within the Unionist party, according to a Times analysis, 109 members were Chamberlainite Tariff Reformers, 32 adhered to Balfour’s more moderate programme, and only 11 were returned as Unionist Free Fooders. Wales did not return a single Unionist. Scotland was again “predominantly Liberal.” The House contained 157 Nonconformists — the largest number since Cromwell’s day — and 16 Jewish members.

The prediction that the Liberal government would be dependent on Irish Nationalist votes was decisively falsified: the Liberal and Labour majority alone was overwhelming.

Significance

The 1906 election was one of the most consequential in modern British history. It settled, for a generation, the fiscal question that had dominated politics since Chamberlain’s 1903 speech: Tariff Reform was decisively rejected at the polls, and the free-trade orthodoxy that had prevailed since the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 was reaffirmed. The Unionist party emerged from the contest deeply divided — the leadership question unresolved between Balfour and Chamberlain, the fiscal question still open within the party.

For the Liberals, the landslide represented the reunion of a party that had been fractured by the Boer War and the Home Rule crisis of 1886. Campbell-Bannerman, who had held the party together through its years in the wilderness, was vindicated. The new government commanded an independent majority and could legislate without bargaining for Irish or Labour votes.

The election was also the breakthrough of the Labour Representation Committee, which returned 29 members committed to independent political action. Keir Hardie, the new chairman of the Labour party, seconded the election of the Speaker — a symbolic recognition of Labour’s arrival as a parliamentary force.

Social reform, which the Clayton Rise of Democracy records had received “neither time nor inclination” under the outgoing government, now moved to the centre of the agenda. The King’s Speech of February 1906 promised legislation on Trade Disputes, Workmen’s Compensation, Education, Merchant Shipping, and the reform of Irish government. Old Age Pensions, though not yet funded, were discussed with deputations from the Trades Union Congress. The Annual Register noted that “the social and economic conditions of the rural districts in Great Britain” were to receive “careful consideration,” and the new Parliament included a generation of legislators — Labour members, Nonconformists, Radicals — committed to the active use of state power for social improvement.

The Chinese labour controversy, which had done so much to sway the election, continued to reverberate. The new Colonial Secretary, Lord Elgin, announced that no further Chinese would be imported into the Transvaal pending the establishment of representative government there. The system was gradually brought to an end.

In the longer perspective, the 1906 Liberal government — sustained by the largest majority in a generation — would go on to enact the Trade Disputes Act (1906), the Workmen’s Compensation Act (1906), the Education Act (1906, though blocked by the Lords), old age pensions (1908), labour exchanges (1909), and the Parliament Act (1911) that finally curbed the veto of the House of Lords. The election of 1906 was the political precondition for all of this — a decisive popular verdict that ended the long Conservative ascendancy and opened the Edwardian era of social reform.

Chronology

  • May 15, 1903 — Joseph Chamberlain’s Birmingham speech proposing imperial preference; Balfour simultaneously defends repeal of the corn duty.
  • September 1903 — Chamberlain resigns from the Cabinet to campaign for Tariff Reform. Duke of Devonshire also resigns.
  • 1903–1904 — Chamberlain’s nationwide speaking tour; Tariff Reform League founded; Unionist Free Trade Club formed; Liberal party reunites in opposition to Protection.
  • 1904–1905 — By-elections show steady swing against the government; Chinese labour controversy intensifies; Wyndham resigns over Irish policy.
  • December 4, 1905 — Balfour resigns as Prime Minister.
  • December 5–10, 1905 — Campbell-Bannerman forms the Liberal government.
  • January 12 – February 13, 1906 — General election polling.
  • January 30, 1906 — 666 of 670 results declared.
  • February 13, 1906 — Final result (Orkney and Shetland); Parliament opens.
  • February 19, 1906 — King’s Speech outlines social reform programme.
