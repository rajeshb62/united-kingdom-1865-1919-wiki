The People’s Budget & Parliament Act (1909–1911)

Between the spring of 1909 and the high summer of 1911 the United Kingdom passed through its most serious constitutional conflict since the Reform crisis of 1832. A Finance Bill framed by David Lloyd George as Chancellor of the Exchequer was rejected by the House of Lords; two general elections in 1910 returned a Liberal ministry dependent on Irish and Labour votes; a Constitutional Conference broke down; and the Parliament Act of 1911 stripped the Upper House of its absolute veto. The struggle joined the financing of old-age pensions and the Navy to social-reform, the defence of free trade against Tariff Reform, and the Irish demand that Home Rule should no longer be blocked by an hereditary chamber. It ended only when King George V was prepared, if necessary, to create peers enough to force the Bill through — the same royal instrument that had broken the Lords in 1832.

The Budget of 1909

Parliament opened on 16 February 1909 under King Edward VII with a Speech already signalling the fiscal pressure of the year. An unusually long financial paragraph warned that, owing especially to old-age provision and the increased cost of the Navy, expenditure would rise. In the Commons debate on the Address, Mr. Asquith insisted that finance must be the first business of the session: social reform had to be paid for, and the Old Age Pension Act was only the first instalment. Mr. Balfour charged Ministers with driving capital abroad by rash speech; Labour speakers pressed land-value taxation as a remedy for unemployment.

The Budget was introduced by Lloyd George on 29 April 1909 — the most controversial financial statement of the period. Described by its author as a “War Budget” against poverty, it proposed new taxes on land values: a 20 per cent tax on the unearned increment of land when sold; a duty of a halfpenny in the pound on the capital value of undeveloped land; and a 10 per cent reversion duty on a landlord’s interest at the expiry of a lease — together with increased death duties and a super-tax on incomes above £5,000. The proceeds were to fund old-age pensions and naval construction amid accelerating Anglo-German rivalry. Mr. Balfour declared that the force behind the Budget was “really Socialism.” Great landowners and financiers organised resistance; the Marquess of Lansdowne would later argue that the measure was “not an ordinary Budget” but a bundle of political measures disguised as a finance bill.

The second reading occupied four days in early June. Its rejection was moved on 7 June by Mr. Austen Chamberlain, who indicated that the Opposition alternative was Tariff Reform and insisted that the proposals were collectively confiscatory: more money would be raised than needed; the burden was unfairly distributed, especially on Scotland and Ireland; unearned increment was really deferred interest, taxed thrice; there was no unit of valuation; the undeveloped-land duty would hit agriculture and destroy mortgage margins. Mr. Ure, the Lord Advocate, replied that the Budget taxed superfluities and persons well able to pay; in ten years gross incomes reviewed for income tax had increased 30 per cent. while national expenditure rose only 15 per cent.; land owed its value to the community. The Budget was “a great and democratic Budget.” Mr. Harold Cox condemned reduction of the New Sinking Fund and called the land-value taxes political warfare.

Toward Confrontation with the Peers

By October independent Unionist peers were making the case for rejection. Lord Curzon at Leeds (15 October) attacked Lloyd George as a demagogue, noted some 250 amendments still pending, and insisted that the Lords had only waived, never abandoned, the right to amend a Finance Bill, and had never waived the right of rejection — whether for tacking, as mischievous, or to refer principles to the people. The Budget’s socialistic feature, he said, was that it was intended to “side-track” Tariff Reform. Viscount Milner likewise pressed the constitutional right of rejection. The Edinburgh Review, Old Whig and Free Trade Unionist, strongly deprecated rejection or amendment.

Report stage began on 18 October. An attempt to recommit the land clauses on the ground of 139 new Government amendments was rejected 180–80; Harold Cox’s motion to omit Clause 1 establishing increment duty was rejected 207–89. Revised Budget arithmetic closed near £162,840,000. Reduction of the Fixed Debt Charge from £25,000,000 to £24,500,000 accompanied a further £500,000 draught on the Sinking Fund, making £3,500,000 in all. Lloyd George reported improved death-duty yield, better stamp receipts, and whisky consumption down by as much as 30 per cent. in some places. A contemporary survey observed that the United Kingdom was “saturated with politics.”

The Lords’ Rejection (30 November 1909)

After five months of debate in the Commons, the Finance Bill went up to the Lords in November. On 30 November, by a vote of 350 to 75, the House of Lords rejected the Bill — a breach of the convention, standing since the seventeenth century, that the Lords did not interfere with money bills. The last occasion on which the Lords had rejected a finance measure was in 1860.

The constitutional crisis was immediate. The Earl of Crewe, moving the second reading, faced a resolution from Lansdowne asserting the Lords’ right to reject a Money Bill. Lansdowne argued that “the Commons’ privileges were now interpreted strictly, and ‘tacking’ had increased, culminating in the Finance Bill of 1894,” and declared that the Budget “would merely be reserved for the approval of the people.” The Lord Chancellor (Lord Loreburn) replied that rejection was “legal, but not constitutional. Supply was the exclusive function of the Commons, and rejection was a direct invasion of their privilege and the powers of the Crown.” He warned that the conflict “could only end ultimately in one way.”

The January 1910 Election

The Lords’ rejection forced a dissolution. The general election of January–February 1910 was carried on with unexampled vigour: about 250 meetings were addressed by Peers in the five weeks between the adjournment and the issue of the writs; the Tariff Reform League and Free Trade Union spent lavishly outside the authorised candidate limits. Parliament was dissolved by Royal Proclamation on 10 January; the first pollings followed on 15 January after unopposed returns including Mr. Joseph Chamberlain for West Birmingham.

The election turned on the constitutional struggle opened by the Lords’ rejection of the Finance Bill, on Tariff Reform versus Free Trade, and on Home Rule. Since an obscure sentence in Asquith’s Albert Hall speech of December 1909, no responsible Minister had made any full revelation on Home Rule, and Unionists charged the Ministry with destroying the only machinery which ensured that a Home Rule Bill should be referred to the electors. The Liberal government survived with Irish and Labour support. A later speaker observed that a transfer of 8,000 Liberal votes to the Unionists would wipe out the existing Liberal majority, while a transfer of 8,000 Unionist votes to the Liberals would double it. The Finance Bill was passed promptly by the new Commons and accepted by the Lords in April 1910 — the Peers having, as they claimed, referred it to the people and now bowed to the electoral result on that measure alone.

Veto, Conference, and the Second Election

The constitutional question — the Lords’ veto — now became the central issue. Parliament was opened by Royal Commission on 15 February 1910; Mr. James William Lowther was again chosen Speaker. Opposition speakers declared that there had never been a deadlock between the House of Lords and the country, and that the Veto resolutions would reduce the Lords to legislative impotence. The Attorney-General (Sir W. Robson) replied that if the Lords controlled finance they could control the Administration and force dissolutions; they would have unlimited power without responsibility; Tariff Reform, once imposed, could never be repealed if the Lords could stand in the way.

The death of King Edward VII in May cast a temporary hush over party warfare. On 17 June the Constitutional Conference opened — an attempt by leading men from both sides to compose the differences between the Houses without a further appeal to the constituencies. Settlement of deadlocks by a Joint Committee had been debated; but an influential section of Unionist Peers held that if they were to be deprived of their powers it should be done by the constituencies and not by a conference. Rumours later attributed the final disruption to Lords Lansdowne and Cawdor and to the numerical strength of majorities required on Bills sent back after rejection. The Conference continued through summer and early autumn without public disclosure; its failure was welcomed by advanced Liberals, Labour, and the main body of the Nationalists. Free Trade Unionists urged that food taxes should be postponed; the Morning Post regarded the constitutional issue as of slight importance compared to Tariff Reform. Mr. John Redmond returned from an American and Canadian fund-raising tour on 12 November with some $200,000 for the Home Rule cause; Unionists made much of “the menace of the American dollars.”

On 18 November Asquith, taking all the time of the House for Government business, insisted that Dissolution was best got over at once. It was now admitted that Liberal legislation did not get a fair chance and that the Lords only forced a Dissolution when the Liberals were in office. The Earl of Crewe announced the Dissolution in the Lords; Lansdowne characterised the statement as astounding and doubted whether a Parliamentary manœuvre had ever been carried out with greater cynicism. Lloyd George, addressing 5,000 men at Mile End on 21 November, said the crisis had been precipitated by the rejection of the Budget; the Government needed money for defence and social reforms by taxing great incomes and fortunes, while the Lords demanded that the burden be put on the bread and meat of the people. The Budget, he insisted, was a complete financial success. At Bury St. Edmunds on 12 December Asquith insisted that the sole issue was the supremacy of the people over legislation. Mr. Winston Churchill at Parkstone declared that the first step must be the abolition of the Veto; after that he hoped for national insurance, education, devolution, and an Irish Parliament for purely Irish affairs. “But the Veto must go now.”

The first polls were taken on 3 December. The balance of parties remained substantially as before: the Ministry still depended on Irish and Labour votes. Unionists held that they must continue to fight the Parliament Bill. The Observer urged that the situation should be allowed to end in absurdity by accepting the creation of 500 “puppet peers.” Less extreme Unionists were divided among submission, a Referendum on the Veto Bill, and hopes of compromise. Unionist speakers insisted that the Parliament Bill was a dodge to smuggle through Home Rule; none of the Ministers had touched on it till 500 seats were decided. The Liberal–Irish–Labour coalition now had a renewed mandate to reform the House of Lords.

The Parliament Bill, 1911

Parliament was opened in state for the first time in the new reign by King George V on Monday, 6 February 1911. Before delivering his Speech the King read and signed the Accession Declaration as established by the Act of 1910. Proposals would be submitted without delay “for settling the relations between the two Houses of Parliament, with the object of securing the more effective working of the Constitution.” The only Bills specified were those removing the pauper disqualification for old-age pensions and for insurance against sickness, invalidity, and (in special trades) unemployment — the programme that would become the National Insurance Act.

The Parliament Bill abolished the Lords’ absolute veto: if the Commons passed a bill in three successive sessions over a period of two years, it could become law without the Lords’ consent. For money bills, the Lords’ power was reduced to a one-month delay. The maximum duration of a Parliament was reduced from seven to five years. After two days’ debate, leave was given on 22 February by 351 to 227 to introduce the Bill. A Referendum Bill was also read a first time; opponents argued that it would be used as an engine of obstruction. Balfour ridiculed the idea that the electorate had given a mandate either for the Parliament Bill specifically or for the other measures suggested, or not suggested, by the Government. The debate was protracted and bitter; the guillotine was applied; Committee stage occupied the greater part of the weeks before the Coronation.

The Lords, the Die-Hards, and the Peer-Creation Threat

Committee stage in the Lords began on 28 June. The Earl of Cromer moved to transfer the decision on money bills from the Speaker to a Joint Committee. Viscount Haldane pointed out that the amendment would restore to the Lords powers it had tacitly resigned. Supported by Lansdowne and the Archbishop of Canterbury, it was carried by 183 to 44; the Lord Chancellor said that nothing would induce the Government to accept it.

The subsequent stages divided Unionist Peers between those who counselled surrender and the “die-hards” or “Halsburyites,” led by the Earl of Halsbury and numbering between 100 and 150 peers determined to reject or emasculate the Bill. Lord Ribblesdale counselled surrender: “the game was up,” and “with dear food round their necks and the House of Lords on their backs” the Opposition were not likely to win a general election. Viscount St. Aldwyn pointed out that if resistance were persisted in the Bill would become law after a prorogation, and that the constituencies were absolutely apathetic. He complained that there had been no full disclosure of what had taken place between Ministers and the King, who had been put in a most cruel position; His Majesty had been bound by his hypothetical promise of November 1910. Lord Willoughby de Broke declared that the Government might win a dozen general elections without altering his opposition; the Dukes of Bedford and Marlborough supported resistance. Lord Newton said that, so far from ridicule killing the creation, “they would have the ridicule and the Government the Peers.”

The crisis deepened when Asquith received — and the House was forced to confront — a guarantee from King George V that the King would create sufficient new Liberal peers to force the Bill through if necessary. Lord Midleton claimed the right of the House to know whether the Government had received that authority; the Earl of Rosebery backed the demand for a clear statement as to the “guarantees.” This use of the royal prerogative — the same method used to force through the Reform Act of 1832 — broke the organised resistance of the party leadership. Lord Lansdowne, following Wellington’s example in 1832, advised Conservative peers to abstain rather than compel the King to swamp the House.

Passage and Royal Assent

On 10 August 1911, after a dramatic all-night sitting, the Parliament Act passed the House of Lords by 131 votes to 114. The Halsburyites were defeated; some 300 Unionist peers stayed away rather than vote against their party leadership or join the die-hard resistance. The Act received the Royal Assent on 18 August 1911. By its terms the Lords’ veto was strictly limited: a Bill might be rejected for two sessions but would pass into law on the third; money bills could be delayed only a month; and no Parliament might last more than five years.

The Unionist party emerged disorganised, torn by dissensions, smarting under severe defeat, and suspicious of its own leadership. In the autumn Mr. Bonar Law attacked the Government’s course since the Act: the country was living under a provisional Constitution; the Government had not reformed the Second Chamber, but only readjusted the relations between the Houses so as to prevent the submission of Home Rule to the electorate.

Significance

The Parliament Act permanently altered the British constitution. It removed the Lords’ power to block legislation approved by the Commons, clearing the way for the Home Rule Bill, Welsh Disestablishment, and the social reforms of the Liberal government — including National Insurance. It resolved a conflict building since the Unionist majority in the Lords systematically rejected Liberal legislation from 1906 onwards: Education, Licensing, and now Finance had each demonstrated that a permanent Conservative majority in one House could nullify a Liberal majority in the other.

By making Home Rule possible without the Lords’ consent, the Act helped drive the Ulster Unionist movement toward extra-parliamentary resistance and the formation of the Ulster Volunteer Force in 1912, setting the stage for the Irish Home Rule crisis that would be overtaken by the Great War. The two elections of 1910 had confirmed that the Liberal ministry could govern only with Irish and Labour support; the Parliament Act made that coalition’s legislative programme enforceable. Finance, once the exclusive preserve of the Commons, had been the weapon that broke the hereditary veto — and the instrument by which social reform and free-trade finance were secured against a second chamber that had never accepted either.
