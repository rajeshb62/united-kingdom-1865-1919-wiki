The Egyptian and Sudan Campaigns (1882–1898)

Overview

The British involvement in Egypt and the Sudan between 1882 and 1898 represents one of the most significant episodes in Victorian imperial history. What began as a reluctant military intervention to suppress a nationalist revolt against the Khedive’s regime evolved into a permanent occupation, a disastrous attempt to extricate Egyptian garrisons from the Sudan that ended in the death of General Gordon, and ultimately a reconquest of the Sudan under Kitchener that brought Britain to the brink of war with France at Fashoda. Through it all, the British maintained what Alfred Milner famously described as a “veiled protectorate” over Egypt — ruling indirectly through the Khedive while keeping the country formally part of the Ottoman Empire.

The Occupation of Egypt (1882)

The Egyptian National Movement and Arabi Pasha

By the early 1880s, Egypt was reeling under the weight of enormous foreign debt and the intrusive Dual Control exercised by Britain and France over its finances. European officials filled every government bureau, railway station, and telegraph office. As Justin McCarthy observed: “The inevitable Englishman, with his unwelcome comrade, the equally inevitable Frenchman, could be seen in every bureau and every railway station and every telegraph office.”

A nationalist movement crystallised around the slogan “Egypt for the Egyptians,” finding its leader in Ahmed Arabi (Arabi Bey, later Arabi Pasha), an army colonel who emerged from the “insurrection of the colonels” — a bloodless army revolt. Arabi became Minister for War and de facto master of the Egyptian administration. The winter of 1881–82 saw his popularity at its zenith. Tourists at Shepheard’s Hotel watched him ride past on his white horse, and noted that “the people ran after him as they did not run after the Khedive.” The Khedive Tewfik himself initially coquetted with the movement.

The Alexandria Crisis and Bombardment

Tensions escalated through the spring of 1882. British and French ironclads were ordered to Alexandria. When Arabi and his colleagues resigned office in protest at demands they should leave the country, the Khedive failed to form a new ministry, and the army declared against him.

On June 11, 1882, a riot erupted in Alexandria between natives and Europeans. The English consul was dragged from his carriage and severely wounded; several British and French subjects were killed. Though no deliberate massacre had been planned, the disturbance provided the casus belli.

France, under the ministry of de Freycinet, declined to intervene actively. On the night of July 10, the French fleet steamed away for Port Said, effectively ending the Dual Control. The British admiral, Sir Beauchamp Seymour (later Lord Alcester), was ordered to prevent further fortification of Alexandria’s defences. At 7 a.m. on July 11, 1882, the British fleet — eight ironclads and five gunboats with over 100 guns — opened fire on the Egyptian forts. The bombardment lasted two days. Egyptian gunners fought bravely, with women and children helping to serve the guns. Arabi and his troops withdrew from the fortifications, and Alexandria descended into chaos — “fire, plunder and murder raged in unchallenged possession of the historic and beautiful city” for two days, with an estimated 2,000 Europeans killed.

The bombardment prompted John Bright’s resignation from Gladstone’s Cabinet. Bright declared the action a violation both of international law and of the moral law. He had delayed his resignation out of personal devotion to Gladstone but could no longer remain.

Tel-el-Kebir and the End of the Revolt

With the Canal secured, Sir Garnet Wolseley was sent to command operations with about 11,000 bayonets, 2,000 sabres, and 60 guns. Arabi entrenched his forces at Tel-el-Kebir. Wolseley’s advance was so swift and well-timed that the Egyptians were taken by surprise. “After the first bayonet charge of the English all was practically over.” Arabi was captured, pleaded guilty to rebellion, was sentenced to death, and had his sentence commuted to perpetual banishment in Ceylon — a leniency reflecting the Khedive’s own earlier sympathy with Arabi’s cause.

The Occupation Begins

Though the government had repeatedly declared its objection to a British occupation — Lord Granville had written in January 1882 that “Her Majesty’s Government have a strong objection to the occupation by themselves of Egypt” — the troops did not withdraw. The Dual Control vanished, and England occupied Egypt on her own account.

McCarthy summarised the irony: “English statesmanship had in the first instance no other purpose than that of securing the Canal and the whole highway to India… Down to the very latest moment, the English Government was altogether opposed to an English occupation of Egypt.”

Gordon and Khartoum (1884–1885)

The Mahdi Rising

After the British occupation of Egypt, the Sudan — under Egyptian administration — fell into chaos. In 1881, a religious movement of extraordinary force erupted under Muhammad Ahmad, who proclaimed himself the Mahdi (“the Guided One”). The Mahdist revolt swept through the Sudan, annihilating an Egyptian army under General Hicks in 1883. By 1884, the Mahdi’s forces controlled much of the country.

Gordon’s Mission

The Gladstone government decided to abandon the Sudan entirely, resolving to withdraw the Egyptian garrisons and restore the territory to some form of native independence. To accomplish this, the government turned to General Charles George Gordon — “Chinese Gordon” — a man of legendary courage, deep religious conviction, and immense public popularity.

Gordon was sent to Khartoum in January 1884 with instructions to evacuate the Egyptian garrisons. He understood the government’s decision to be irrevocable. Gladstone himself would later refer to the Mahdi’s followers as “a people struggling to be free, and rightly struggling to be free.” Gordon, however, soon concluded that evacuation was impossible without first establishing some form of stable government, and he began instead to organise the defence of Khartoum. By March 1884, the Mahdi’s forces had surrounded the city.

Delay and the Relief Expedition

For months, the government hesitated. As McCarthy records: “There was a strange, and to this day an unaccountable, delay about the measures to be taken for Gordon’s rescue. People said that the Government had no hope of being able to do anything to save him; and a Government is in sore straits when people believe that there is something that it ought to do and cannot even try to do.”

At last, a relief expedition was assembled under Sir Garnet Wolseley. It was perhaps a half-hearted enterprise at best. The public had full confidence in Wolseley — the hero of the Red River expedition and the Egyptian campaign against Arabi — but the difficulties were immense. Wolseley had to fight his way step by step up the Nile. Colonel Fred Burnaby, the beloved and chivalrous soldier of gigantic stature, was among the first to fall — a dark omen.

The Fall of Khartoum

On January 26, 1885, after a siege of nearly eleven months, Khartoum fell. Gordon was killed on the palace steps, two days before the relief force arrived. In a cruel irony, Punch had published a cartoon depicting the gladsome meeting of Wolseley and Gordon at Khartoum — on the very day the news of Gordon’s death arrived.

Gordon’s death was a terrible blow to the Liberal Government. “Gordon was an ideal hero; and an Administration of saints and sages could not have been saved from reproach.” Yet the Ministry did not immediately fall. It survived votes of censure until the budget of the Chancellor, Mr. Childers — proposing increased taxes on income, spirits, and beer — was defeated by a majority of twelve on June 8, 1885. Lord Randolph Churchill danced on the benches of the House of Commons in wild excitement as the Gladstone administration fell.

The Reconquest of Sudan (1896–1898)

The Long Interval

For over a decade after Gordon’s death, the Sudan remained under the rule of the Mahdi’s successor, the Khalifa Abdallahi. Egypt — and her British administrators — lacked both the funds and the political will for reconquest. Sir Evelyn Baring (later Lord Cromer), the British Agent and Consul-General in Egypt, wrote in 1888: “There is not now, and since the destruction of General Hicks’ army there never has been, any serious idea of deliberately adopting a policy involving the reconquest of the Soudan by force of arms.”

The fear, however, that another European power might seize the Upper Nile and control Egypt’s water supply — the legendary fear of Nile manipulation dating back to medieval times — gradually drove British policy toward reconquest. As Lord Milner wrote in his influential book England in Egypt (1892): “It is an uncomfortable thought that the regular supply of water by the great river, which is to Egypt not a question of convenience and prosperity but actually of life, must always be exposed to some risk, as long as the upper reaches of that river are not under Egyptian control.”

The Decision to Advance

The immediate catalyst came in early 1896. The Italians had suffered a catastrophic defeat at Adwa in Abyssinia, and the Dervish forces were concentrating near Kassala. Lord Salisbury’s cabinet, concerned both to assist the Italians and to plant “the foot of Egypt farther up the Nile,” authorised an advance from Wadi Halfa toward Dongola. Salisbury wrote to Cromer that the decision was “inspired specially by a desire to help the Italians at Kassala, and to prevent the dervishes from winning a conspicuous success which might have far-reaching results. In addition, we desired to kill two birds with one stone, and to use the same military effort to plant the foot of Egypt farther up the Nile.”

Kitchener’s Campaign

The advance was placed under the command of Sir Herbert Kitchener, the Sirdar (Commander-in-Chief) of the Egyptian army. Kitchener — a man of remarkable organisational ability, foresight, and patience — methodically built up his forces and extended the railway across the desert. In the spring of 1898, he attacked and destroyed the entrenched Dervish camp at the Atbara River, killing at least 3,000 men and capturing the Dervish leader Mahmoud.

The Battle of Omdurman (2 September 1898)

Kitchener’s final objective was Khartoum itself. The Khalifa had assembled a force of over 60,000 men, including the most warlike tribes of the region, well supplied with artillery. The British and Egyptian troops numbered some 23,000.

On September 2, 1898, the two forces met at Omdurman, just north of Khartoum. The Dervishes attacked with desperate bravery, charging again and again into the concentrated fire of Kitchener’s army — armed with modern rifles, machine guns, and artillery. Maxim guns and Lee-Metford rifles mowed down the advancing Mahdist forces. By the end of the day, some 11,000 Dervishes lay dead and 16,000 wounded, against fewer than 50 British and Egyptian killed.

Kitchener entered Omdurman in triumph. On September 4, a thanksgiving service was held in Khartoum, and the British and Egyptian flags flew side by side over the shattered citadel where Gordon had died. As McCarthy observed: “The feeling was that Gordon had at last been avenged, and that the place which had seen his murder had now become the scene of the vengeance inflicted for that murder.”

Kitchener’s Reward

Kitchener returned to England to a hero’s welcome. The Queen created him Lord Kitchener of Khartoum. At a banquet at the Mansion House, he invited the British public to raise £100,000 for a college at Khartoum to be built as a memorial to Gordon — a task he completed before returning to Egypt.

Fashoda Crisis

The Marchand Expedition

Even before the battle of Omdurman, Kitchener received intelligence that a European force was in occupation of Fashoda, a point on the White Nile far south of Khartoum, and that the French flag was flying there. This was the expedition of Major Jean-Baptiste Marchand, who had crossed Africa from the French Congo with a small force of Senegalese soldiers, reaching Fashoda in July 1898 after an epic two-year journey.

Kitchener’s Confrontation with Marchand

Kitchener acted with characteristic promptness. He steamed up the Nile with a flotilla of gunboats and a military force. Finding Marchand in occupation and the French flag flying, he informed the French officer that Fashoda was Egyptian territory under British protection, and firmly — but politely — invited him to withdraw.

Marchand replied that he was there on the orders of the French Government and could not leave without instructions from Paris. The two commanders exchanged courtesies: Kitchener is said to have offered whisky and water; Marchand responded with champagne of the best brand. Kitchener established a Sudanese garrison at Fashoda under an English officer and left the diplomatic settlement to London and Paris.

The Crisis Deepens

The British Government published the despatches on October 10, 1898, revealing a serious international crisis. It emerged that as early as December 1897, Sir Edmund Monson, the British Ambassador in Paris, had warned the French Government that no other power could claim to occupy any part of the Nile Valley. In 1895, Sir Edward Grey, then Under-Secretary for Foreign Affairs, had declared that a French advance into the Nile Valley would be regarded as “an unfriendly action” — diplomatic language for a threat of war.

For weeks, the two nations stood on the brink of conflict. The French Foreign Minister, Delcassé, argued that France was only following England’s example of expansion. Lord Salisbury directed the French Government to be told that the Fashoda region was now subject, by right of conquest, to British and Egyptian authority.

The French Back Down

A remarkable display of national unity emerged in Britain. Lord Rosebery and the Liberal leaders joined the Conservatives in declaring that England’s claims must be maintained — by war if necessary. “The strong feeling thus manifested through England had its effect on the hesitating action of the French Government.”

In early November 1899, the Marchand expedition was recalled. Fashoda was evacuated in December. A profound sense of relief was felt in both countries. The crisis had tested the new alignment of European powers and demonstrated Britain’s determination to control the entire Nile Valley from source to sea.

The Veiled Protectorate

An Occupation Without a Name

From 1882 onward, Britain exercised effective control over Egypt, yet the country remained nominally part of the Ottoman Empire, with the Khedive as the Sultan’s vassal. This arrangement — neither outright annexation nor genuine independence — came to be known as the “veiled protectorate.” The term was popularised by Alfred Milner in his 1892 book England in Egypt, which made a deep impression on the British public.

The British ruled through a British Agent and Consul-General — Sir Evelyn Baring (created Lord Cromer in 1892) held this post from 1883 to 1907. Cromer was effectively the ruler of Egypt, though he operated behind the facade of the Khedivial government. As McCarthy noted, writing in 1897: “Since then, of course, the Dual Control has vanished, and England has occupied Egypt on her own account. There can be no doubt that the British occupation has brought great material benefit to the people of the country. Taxation has been reduced and equalised, forced labour has been abolished, and something like justice has been done in the courts of law.”

The Paradox of the Occupation

Yet the occupation remained paradoxical. As McCarthy observed: “No English statesman could possibly pretend, or ever did pretend, that British diplomacy had any such purpose when it planted a more or less firm foot on the banks of the Nile… English statesmanship had in the first instance no other purpose than that of securing the Canal and the whole highway to India.”

After 1887, when France and Russia blocked a convention between England and Turkey for evacuation, the chances of withdrawal rapidly diminished. By 1892 it was clear the occupation would last indefinitely, and Cromer settled down with renewed confidence to the work of reconstruction.

The Results of the Protectorate

Cromer’s regime brought considerable material progress: irrigation works expanded, the tax burden was rationalised, and the judicial system reformed. But the veiled nature of British control created constant tension. France remained unreconciled to British dominance in Egypt. The occupation drew Britain deeper into African affairs, leading to the reconquest of the Sudan and nearly to war with France at Fashoda. As McCarthy presciently concluded: “The problem is, how to keep what we have already got, and thus far the idea of statesmanship, whether Liberal or Tory, seems to be that of an extension of territory and frontier southwards… Expedition after expedition has been sent out to extend the Egyptian frontier, nominally for the Khedive, but really for the English occupation, and some of the expeditions have been ghastly failures, while others have been at least temporary successes.”

The veiled protectorate lasted until 1914, when Britain declared Egypt a formal protectorate at the outbreak of the First World War.
