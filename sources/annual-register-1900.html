The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1900

The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1900

Sep 15, 202620 min read

The Annual Register 1900

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Century Parallel, Roberts, Liberal Imperialism, Church

The Register opens by likening Britain’s position in 1900 to that of 1801 after Lunéville: again in a war whose difficulty and duration rulers had misjudged, and again without a continental ally, while Europe from Calais to Constantinople was hostile though not in arms. The decisive difference was colonial: Canada, Australia, the West Indies, and Indian rulers offered men and money unbound by conditions; without that spontaneous rally, the Register suggests, European and South African events might have run differently. At home, as in 1801, there was a stern resolve to see the matter through despite generals’ incapacity, administrative blunders, and early campaign blindness. “Pro-Boers” could barely get a hearing; parliamentary critics met “music-hall militarism” with judicious silence.

Just before the year closed, the Government had awakened to the military danger: Field-Marshal Lord Roberts of Kandahar and Lord Kitchener were summoned in haste (rumour said without consulting Commander-in-Chief Lord Wolseley); Roberts took supreme command in South Africa, Kitchener as chief of staff; large reinforcements were granted; Roberts reached Cape Town on 10 January and at once raised the standing of colonial contingents hitherto used on subordinate duties.

Mr. Balfour at Manchester and Ardwick defended the war as not a grab for gold but a civilising necessity after Boer attack, repudiated War Office concealment, claimed field guns compared favourably with those of any great nation, and argued that Britain’s army organisation was uniquely hard because each war demanded different armament and transport. Critics found his optimism thin. Sir Charles Dilke at Cinderford (10 January) — named by Lord Charles Beresford among three Commons men who understood imperial defence — denied that three complete army corps were in South Africa: infantry might be there, but cavalry and artillery to complete them were wanting, and guns barely exceeded what France or Germany allotted to one corps. Home Secretary Sir M. White Ridley at Blackpool fell back on the plea that asking for ten millions and an army corps the previous summer would only have precipitated war. Herbert Gladstone at Holbeck (19 January) urged that even a change of Government would not stop the war, which must still be prosecuted; he would never regret Majuba restoration of Transvaal independence and held that South Africa must be governed with the Dutch. A South African Conciliation Committee formed to push goodwill and accurate information, and soon quarrelled with Cape loyalist spokesmen.

Lord Rosebery at Chatham (23 January) tried non-partisanship: the war was among Britain’s most formidable in numbers and in near-unanimous European ill-will; Austria’s Bosnian struggle was recalled for perspective; never had another nation sent 120,000 men 7,000 miles by sea; prestige lost since Waterloo-high 1898 must be recovered; Navy, capital, and national character were assets, and colonial aid had proved the empire “based on a rock.” Mr. L. Courtney’s independent criticism tore at Rhodes–Colonial Office relations. Mr. John Morley at Forfar (24 January) — with Harcourt a doubtful colleague and no followers — lectured rather than led: he disliked “empire” as rule over the unwilling, preferred a confederacy centred on Britain, and mocked Liberal Imperialism as “Chamberlain wine with a Rosebery label”; grudgingly the war must go on, but Outlander grievances (votes, dynamite, native labour) were not worth British lives; the Government’s summer language had made war inevitable while Rhodes and Milner were trusted over Schreiner’s warnings. The Duke of Bedford, recently chosen to move the Lords’ Address, told Bedford Liberal Unionists (25 January) that Government policy rested on four misapprehensions — that the Transvaal would never declare war; that Free State offensive alliance was unlikely; that British territory would not be invaded; that Boer forces were about 30,000 with negligible artillery — and demanded Milner, Conyngham Greene, Butler, Intelligence, and Wolseley–Lansdowne papers before inquiry could mean anything. War Office scapegoating of Lansdowne continued. The English Church Union, led toward medieval continuity by Lord Halifax, protested the Archbishops’ “opinion” on incense and processional lights; a lay communicants’ memorial (claimed 13,794 signatures) was later repudiated by the Union as a body; withdrawals from extreme ritualism marked strain between High Church militants and the laity.

Chapter II — Parliament: Queen’s Speech, Address, Estimates, Budget

Parliament met a week early (30 January). A “Unity” meeting at Campbell-Bannerman’s house framed an Opposition line charging lack of foresight and asking who was responsible for the policy that led to war and for political interference with military plans. Irish Nationalists reconstructed a united party under J. Redmond as sessional chairman, separating from Liberals toward more militant policy. The Queen did not open Parliament in person. The Speech stressed unbroken South African war, colonial loyalty, a Samoa/Tonga Pacific treaty with Germany (partly with the United States), and an early Australian federation bill; its length suggested an approaching electoral appeal.

Lord E. Fitzmaurice’s amendment produced a prolonged debate; Balfour wound up by declaring the War Office in perfect order and its chiefs beyond reproach — a line that cheered the House but found no favour outside. Division: 352 to 139 against the amendment (two Liberals with Government; many Liberals abstaining). The same day York returned Unionist G. Denison Faber 6,248 to Hon. A. Murray’s 4,818 after Lord Charles Beresford’s resignation for active service — read as a Chamberlainite war vote. Plymouth writ followed Sir Edward Clarke’s resignation after Conservatives told him his Majuba-minded war criticism no longer represented them.

Lansdowne’s proposals for increasing the Army, Lord Wemyss on the militia ballot, Navy and Army Estimates, Militia and Volunteers, and Civil Service Estimates filled the House. Sir Michael Hicks-Beach’s preliminary Budget and war finance raised taxation (including 4d. on the income tax, tea, tobacco, beer and spirits) against a deficit framed around expenditure of some £149 millions; a £30 millions war loan (stock/bonds redeemable in ten years or Treasury bills) was authorised (6 March). Campbell-Bannerman called the Budget statesmanlike for putting war burden on taxpayers; Harcourt approved principles but doubted easy Transvaal contribution and noted borrowing exceeding Crimean practice. Youthful Offenders Bill; Presidents’ peace proposals; War Office and campaign conduct debates; Eight Hours Bill rejected; the Queen’s shamrock order for Irish soldiers and her Irish visit (see Ch. VI); Government and pro-Boer meetings; Army contracts; Irish Catholic University talk; Old Age Pensions; Agricultural Holdings Bill (second reading before Easter, Long answering Scottish and Welsh critics); British interests in China; Delagoa Bay Railway award; National Liberal Federation at Nottingham; attempt on the Prince of Wales — all marked a session living on South African news as Spion Kop context and early-session legislation intertwined.

Chapter III — West Africa, Spion Kop Despatches, Commonwealth, Mafeking

Pause after Kimberley and Ladysmith reliefs revived charges that War Office supplies and remounts were exhausted. West Africa showed Colonial Office recklessness: the Gold Coast Governor’s attempt to seize the Ashanti “gold stool” (a tribal fetish), after Coomassie occupation had left it concealed, provoked resistance, a beaten detachment, and country-wide rising. Uganda Railway debate exposed Foreign Office reliance on employees’ hearsay: a £3 millions vote already squandered with the line not half done.

Crowning War Office blunder, in the Register’s view, was publishing Buller’s Spion Kop report with Roberts’s scathing remarks — failure partly due to ground and enemy positions, “probably also to errors of judgment and want of administrative capacity” on Warren’s part, but also to the supreme commander’s disinclination to assert authority and to “unwarrantable and needless assumption of responsibility by a subordinate” — while Methuen’s Modder River report appeared without Commander-in-Chief comment. Publication six weeks after receipt, officers censured yet kept in command, and the slight on Colonel Thorneycroft (thought to shield British officers at colonials’ expense) were called inexplicable and indiscreet if no action followed.

Recess speeches; Australian Commonwealth Bill through the imperial Parliament, with fight over the Privy Council appeal clause (clause 74); Marriage with Deceased Wife’s Sister vote; Lords on War Office proposals; Charity Commissioners and Birmingham Schools Bill. Salisbury at the Albert Hall on foreign opinion, rifle clubs, and imperial unity. Chamberlain at Birmingham stated settlement terms: no restored independence for the two republics; territories to be fully incorporated under the British flag; an interval of Crown-colony / military administration, then self-governing colonial status when safe — less would lose colonial allies’ respect. Episcopal revolt against Salisbury’s Gallio-like temperance stance: Bishop of Winchester moved for legislation on the seventy-seven recommendations common to Licensing Commission majority and minority reports; Salisbury demurred that cellar-owning legislators made law for the cellarless. Housing of the working classes; Women and Local Government bill second reading 248–119 then dropped. Bye-elections fed dissolution cries. Culmination: relief of Mafeking (17 May) after 200-plus days’ defence by Baden-Powell’s improvised garrison against some 4,000 with modern artillery — ecstatic national reception; Chamberlain and Balfour interpreting the political weather.

Chapter IV — City, China, Commonwealth Compromise, Hospitals, Liberal Split, Prorogation

Prolonged Whitsuntide recess let members sound constituents on an autumn “khaki” election while Roberts entered Pretoria (6 June) without evident Boer exhaustion. Salisbury to the City of London Conservative Association (29 May) corrected peace-party misuse of “We desire no territory, we desire no gold”: he had been repudiating European calumnies, not binding Britain never to annex; war had followed Outlander oppression and an insulting ultimatum; responsibilities included protecting natives and seeking reconciliation subject to extinguishing hostility to Queen and empire.

China disturbances rose toward the Boxer crisis. Campbell-Bannerman at Glasgow and Morley at Oxford sharpened Liberal Imperialist versus “pro-Boer”/Little Englander divisions. Australian Commonwealth Bill reached final imperial–colonial compromise on appeals. Money-lending Bill; Companies Bill; new education Code; shifted Government views on education and militia ballot. Mr. Burdett-Coutts’s Times letter (27 June) on South African hospitals — typhoid cases on hard ground near Bloemfontein with almost no nurses, milk, or medicines after weeks of occupation and rail supply for everyone but the sick; ox-waggon torture of wounded — recalled Crimean revelations and contradicted MacCormac/Treves dinner praise of completeness. Accommodation for ~5,000 versus needed ~20,000; by end of May some 20,000 sick and wounded. Government appointed a commission; later Balfour–Burdett-Coutts exchanges grew bitter over oath powers and ex parte debate.

Agricultural Holdings Bill; Tithe Rent Charge (Ireland) Bill; military measures; West Africa and China debates. Liberal split dramatised (including the July 25 division on reducing Chamberlain’s salary). Indian Budget; supplementary Army Estimate and revised Budget. Commander-in-Chief versus War Office controversy; Chamberlain and “pro-Boer” members; War Office contracts. On settlement, Chamberlain rejected Lawson’s amendment restoring republican independence; Campbell-Bannerman would neither support nor oppose the extreme amendment, adhering to Asquith’s four points (imperial supremacy; no recurrence; equality of civil rights; no ascendency) while fearing martial-law harshness and delayed equality. Session ended with parties fixed on war and dissolution.

Chapter V — Pekin Relief, Khaki Election, Reconstruction, Winter Session, Century’s Close

Deep apprehension for Pekin lifted when, four days after despatch, news came that on 14 August the capital was reached and the legations relieved. The Queen telegraphed thanks to the Royal Marine Guard. Relief was only the first chapter: Dowager-Empress and officials escaped toward Si-ngan-fu; German Field Marshal Count von Waldersee was accepted as allied generalissimo (coolly outside Germany); Brodrick (15 August) spoke of advancing as “good comrades” with Germany.

Dissolution timing filled the press; Opposition charged Chamberlain with pushing a “khaki” election. South African letters published; “Protestantism before Politics”; railway labour troubles; Trade Union Congress rule changes. Dissolution announced; Opposition complained of practical disfranchisement; party manifestoes; war the one dominating issue; personal attacks on Chamberlain (including company/contract insinuations Rosebery later called groundless but symptomatic of weak supervision). Church Discipline Bill; Imperial Liberal Council (Rosebery/Grey/Fowler/Asquith line). Chamberlain’s Heywood telegram — every seat lost to Government “a seat gained by the Boers” — gained venom when wires substituted “sold to” for “gained by”; Balfour at Bingley (9 October) explained that Radical votes raised Boer hopes and depressed Natal and Cape loyalists, not that every Liberal desired Boer victory. Herbert Gladstone denounced “vote for Liberals = vote for Boers” placards as fool or liar or both.

Polls: Unionist urban landslides unprecedented in strength of majorities more than in net seat transfers — e.g. Hackney Central majority 312 to 1,504; North Lambeth more than doubled; Kennington 265 to 1,886; St. Pancras and Deptford rises; Shoreditch divisions split one each way. County returns less conspicuous but equally decisive. Final House: Conservatives and Liberal Unionists 402; Liberals and Labour 186; Nationalists 82; total 670; Unionist majority 134. Liberals remained divided; Imperial Liberal Council could not yet control the party.

Anglo-German Agreement on China affirmed integrity of China and the “open door” at treaty ports; clause 3 reserved preliminary understanding if another Power used complications for territorial advantage; other Powers were to be invited to accept the principles — widely read as a warning especially to Russia, which irritated the Russian press. Guerrilla war continued in South Africa after conventional collapse. Lord Wolseley, retiring as Commander-in-Chief, spoke optimistically of 234,000 men and 170,000 animals sent 7,000 miles, credited Cardwell short service, then at Sheffield (1 November) complained that expert necessities were met with “no money” and that Cabinet should publish expert advice. Salisbury shortly reconstructed a twenty-member Cabinet: himself still Prime Minister and Lord Privy Seal; Lansdowne to Foreign Office; Brodrick to War; Ritchie Home; Selborne Admiralty; Gerald Balfour Board of Trade; Londonderry Postmaster-General; other offices redistributed (Halsbury, Devonshire, Hicks-Beach, Chamberlain, Hamilton, Balfour of Burleigh, Balfour, Cadogan, Ashbourne, James of Hereford, Long, Hanbury, Akers-Douglas). Junior posts rearranged. Ministers at the Mansion House; Hicks-Beach at Bristol; Liberal speeches.

Winter session (from 3 December; Queen’s Speech 6 December by commission) existed only to vote further South Africa and China war money. Debates covered Unionist electioneering, military measures, South African policy (farm-burning, women and children, need of a conciliatory proclamation), ministers’ company directorships (Rosebery’s family-genius jab at Salisbury; Devonshire’s tart reply), and war expenditure. Front Opposition bench differences persisted. The Register closes the English narrative on the century’s end under unfinished guerrilla war and an unfinished China settlement.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Politically and ecclesiastically memorable. Imperial Liberals (Rosebery, Asquith) versus Morley, Bryce, Reid, and Campbell-Bannerman’s uncertain lead: the July 25 Chamberlain-salary division was taken to heart; many lifelong Liberal electors judged the party unfit for imperial affairs. At the polls Liberals lost eight Scottish seats (East Aberdeenshire, Edinburgh South, Dumfriesshire, Glasgow Blackfriars and Bridgeton, Sutherlandshire, Orkney and Shetland, Wick Burghs) and gained only Inverness-shire — first failure of a Liberal majority in North Britain since 1832; Liberal majority of ten became Unionist majority of four. Glasgow returned no Liberal in seven divisions. Munro-Ferguson (resigning Liberal whip) at Leith (19 November) said appearance of cohesion was futile and independent groups might eventually form a Government; Campbell-Bannerman at Dundee claimed four-fifths Liberal agreement and condemned the Imperial Liberal Council’s exclusiveness. Dr. Clark lost Caithness after Bloemfontein letters; Orkney and Shetland rejected a Liberal for the first time in sixty-three years. War decided the elections; Scottish Private Bill procedure and Agricultural Holdings amendment also helped Unionists. Free Church and United Presbyterian Churches united (31 October) as the United Free Church of Scotland in Waverley Market (~6,000 present); Principal Rainy first Moderator; Highland/Island dissentients prepared litigation as the true Free Church of 1843. Trade and shipbuilding mostly prosperous.

Ireland. Three prominent incidents: Queen Victoria’s unexpected visit; Unionist schisms; Nationalist consolidation under William O’Brien. The Queen ordered Irish soldiers to wear shamrock on St. Patrick’s Day in honour of South African gallantry, then sojourned three weeks at the Viceregal Lodge (April) instead of the Riviera — personal, not political, initiative. Dublin Corporation carried a welcome address 30–22 despite Redmond’s “insult or a lie” letter and irreconcilable pressure; popular reception along the Kingstown–Dublin route and at Phoenix Park review of ~6,000 under the Duke of Connaught was cordial to enthusiastic. Tithe and agrarian legislation and Nationalist responses to the war filled the parliamentary year already traced above.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. The Paris International Exhibition year was meant as a political truce; passions did not fully calm, yet the year ended more peacefully than the early session promised. High Court (4 January) condemned Déroulède and Buffet to ten years’ banishment, Guérin to ten years’ imprisonment (Fort Chabrol). Deschanel beat Brisson for Chamber presidency (308–221). Assumptionist Fathers’ trial (24 January) brought mild fine and suspended dissolution pending Associations legislation; Cardinal Richard’s blessing visit inflamed Church–State dispute and exposed the old Catholic–Socialist Pacte de Bordeaux. Strikes, senatorial elections, Nationalist revenge politics, and Exhibition ceremonies filled the calendar while South African Anglophobia coloured much of the press.

Italy. Pelloux ministry, procedure-rule crisis, and June elections: Upper Italy defected to Opposition; extreme Left doubled mainly at Constitutional Opposition expense; King opened Parliament (16 June) in melancholy defence of institutions while Opposition absented itself; Gallo narrowly beat Biancheri for Chamber presidency (242–214); committee on procedure including Socialists. Triple Alliance continuity amid domestic turmoil.

Chapter II — Germany and Austria-Hungary

Germany. Busy at home and abroad; second half absorbed by China. The Emperor, rededicating Guards’ flags, compared Germany to early-nineteenth-century Prussia and insisted on a stronger Navy; at a banquet for Prince Henry’s return from China he spoke of forging an arm for the black-white-red flag. New Navy Bill and exposé des motifs argued that even the prior Navy Act’s fleet could not prevent blockade by a superior naval adversary and that German shipping would be at such an adversary’s mercy — Germany need not match the greatest navy but must be strong enough that beating her would weaken the victor. China expedition and Waldersee command; Anglo-German Agreement thread (English History Ch. V); Anglophobe press versus imperial friendliness (e.g. coolness toward Krugerite gestures).

Austria-Hungary. Dualist politics and Balkan vigilance continued beside participation in the China expedition and European concert routines.

Chapter III — Russia, Turkey, and Eastern Europe

Russia. Witte’s Budget report stressed industrial growth under protection (coal, pig iron, steel surges), railway consolidation and Siberian line cost (~¾ milliard roubles), Manchurian branch from Port Arthur, and gold-reserve drain partly blamed on the South African war. May ukase finally discharged the remaining 50 million rouble Exchequer debt to the State Bank from Crimean/Turkish paper inflation — henceforth no financing by note issue. Far Eastern military posture and Boxer intervention dominated the second half-year; Russian press bristled at the Anglo-German Agreement.

Turkey and Eastern states. Background surveys while world attention fixed on Africa and China; Balkan and Ottoman notices in the Register’s usual compass.

Chapter IV — Minor States of Europe

Belgium, the Netherlands, and Switzerland — the Register notes — had especial cause for prudence amid great-power storms; Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, and Norway receive their customary political and legislative notices (army, franchise, Iberian recovery, Scandinavian defence and constitutional questions).

Chapter V — Asia

India, Afghanistan, etc. Rumours of Russian aggression continued on the Afghan frontier; administrative and famine-aftermath notices; Indian Budget politics at Westminster (English History).

China. After Japan’s 1895 victory exposed Chinese weakness, Europe abandoned Britain’s “open door” preference for German-style spheres: Shantung, Port Arthur, Wei-hai-wei, French southern concessions, Italian coaling ambitions — convincing Chinese conservatives that partition impended. Dowager-Empress and Prince Tuan channelled antiforeign hatred; arms imports; Brooks missionary murder (news 4 January); Yu Hsien’s I-Ho-Chuan (“Righteous Harmony Fists” — “Boxers”) with invulnerability cults; Yüan-Shih-Kai ordered to suppress without force. Legation siege, von Ketteler’s murder, allied relief (14 August), Waldersee command, and the settlement problem — the year’s second imperial crisis beside South Africa. Hong Kong, Korea, Japan, Siam in supporting survey roles.

Chapter VI — Africa

Egypt and the Soudan. Singularly barren of drama. Khedive–British relations friendlier; Cromer sworn of the Privy Council; cautious Budget with irrigation outlays; revenue justifying prior ten-to-eleven-million estimates; Caisse de la Dette still skimming surpluses; Egyptian debt about £95.5 millions, no floating debt; gradual native-court and fellaheen-debt reforms.

South Africa — the War. Full campaign narrative complementary to English History. Year opened gloomily after Black Week; City Imperial Volunteers enrolled; French around Colesberg; Pilcher’s colonial success at Sunnyside; Kuruman surrender; Buller’s Natal efforts and Spion Kop; Roberts/Kitchener reorganisation; relief of Kimberley and Ladysmith; Cronje’s capture and shipment to St. Helena (~1,000 prisoners from 3 April); Bloemfontein and advance to Pretoria (6 June); annexation agitation at Cape loyalist meetings; rebel movement subsiding after Free State withdrawal. Cape ministerial crisis (8–13 June): Schreiner could not hold Bond and Cabinet (Sauer, Merriman, Te Water versus Schreiner/Solomon); Sprigg Progressive ministry with Rose Innes Attorney-General; Treason Bill (special commissions; five-year disfranchisement of rank and file) carried Assembly 48–38. Guerrilla war after conventional collapse — De Wet and others — carried into the winter Westminster debates.

East, West, Central Africa. Ashanti gold-stool rising (English History Ch. III); regional partition and administration notices.

Chapter VII — America

United States. 56th Congress party balance (Senate Republicans 52, Democrats 27, etc.; House Republicans 185, Democrats 164). Gold Standard Act (signed mid-March) fixed the gold dollar and $150 millions gold redemption reserve without extinguishing silver legal tender or precluding future international bimetallism. Alaska civil code (Juneau capital); Hawaii organic act; Porto Rico temporary civil government and tariff (President first urged free trade then a two-year tariff). Nicaragua Canal and Anti-Trust bills failed despite House passage. Presidential year: Republicans at Philadelphia (19–21 June) nominated McKinley and Roosevelt by acclamation; Democrats renominated Bryan; imperialism, gold standard, and prosperity the fight. Samoa/Pacific and China policies intersected British interests.

Canada, Newfoundland, West Indies, Central and South America. Canadian and other contingents to South Africa; colonial loyalty; routine Latin American and West Indian surveys (including natural disasters affecting British interests).

Chapter VIII — Australasia

Two supreme topics almost excluded lesser local politics: the South African War — extraordinary patriotic contingents and feeling — and Confederation. Premiers’ Sydney meeting (24 January; Forrest of Western Australia excepted) on Lieutenant-Governors, Governor-General residence, and London delegates for the Commonwealth Bill. Imperial Parliament passed the bill virtually unamended except the struggle over clause 74 (Privy Council appeals): Chamberlain pressed amendment; colonial sentiment proved divided and often uninterested in losing the imperial appeal; final compromise enabled the Act. Federation plus war contingents defined the Australasian year.

Chronicle of Events in 1900

Daybook companion to Parts I–II. Opens with the Queen’s New Year greetings to South African commands (1 January); Paris High Court sentences on Déroulède, Buffet, Guérin; Royal yacht Victoria and Albert docking mishap; Royal Niger Company flag lowered at Lagos; Caucasus earthquake (~1,000 dead); Ladysmith eleven-hour defence (6 January); Roberts at Cape Town (10 January); Bank rate 6 to 5 per cent; Indian famine relief rolls above three millions — and so month by month through Spion Kop, Cronje, reliefs, Mafeking night, Pekin, dissolution, khaki polls, guerrilla telegrams, and century’s end.

Retrospect of Literature, Science, and Art

Publishing output slightly restricted: South African anxiety made publishers cautious on general books while war books proliferated for future historians. Fiction and poetry dipped slightly under war taxes and newspaper distraction; history, biography, and travel increased, with lasting additions. Poetry: little judged likely to live in book form from the highest names; exception Stephen Phillips’s Herod (after Paolo and Francesca), with Yeats’s The Shadowy Waters and Symons’s Images of Good and Evil noted. Science and art sections follow the Register’s usual specialist notes for the year (Sullivan’s death marked as music’s greatest personal loss in the chronicle/obituary apparatus).
