The Servile State (1912–1913)

Hilaire Belloc’s argument that industrial society, with the means of production owned by a few, is in unstable equilibrium and tends toward stable equilibrium by legally enforceable compulsory labour of non-owners for the advantage of owners.

Source

Belloc, Hilaire. The Servile State. Boston: Le Roy Phillips; London and Edinburgh: T. N. Foulis. First published October 1912; reprinted February 1913 and October 1913. Printed by Neill and Co., Ltd., Edinburgh (one impression) and Morrison & Gibb Limited, Edinburgh (another). Dedicated to E. S. P. Haynes.

The title-page epigraph: “If we do not restore the Institution of Property we cannot escape restoring the Institution of Slavery; there is no third course.”

Contents

Preface to the Second Edition

Belloc writes after comparing reviews that parts of the argument are “liable to misconception,” and will not alter the completed book. First, the book “bears no relation to the common accusation levelled against Socialists (that is, Collectivists) that life in a Socialist State would be so subject to regulation and order as to be unduly oppressive.” He does “not discuss the Socialist State.” “It is the very heart of my thesis that we are not, as a fact, approaching Socialism at all, but a very different state of society; to wit, a society in which the Capitalist class shall be even more powerful and far more secure than it is at present: a society in which the proletarian mass shall not suffer from particular regulations, oppressive or beneficent, but shall change their status, lose their present legal freedom, and be subject to compulsory labour.”

Second, he has not set up the thesis “as a warning or as a piece of gloom.” He says nowhere that “the re-establishment of slavery would be a bad thing as compared with our present insecurity.” “The tendency towards the re-establishment of slavery is due to the very fact that the new conditions may be found more tolerable than those obtaining under Capitalism.” Which arrangement might be preferred “would make an ample subject for another book.”

Third, he answers the charge (from “the most sincere and the best writer among the English Socialists”) that he has mistaken professional politicians’ “Social Reform” for Socialism. What he has said is that the Socialist object — “the putting of the means of production into the hands of politicians to hold in trust for the community” — “is not in practice being approached”; “we are rapidly coming nearer to the establishment of compulsory labour among an unfree majority of non-owners for the benefit of a free minority of owners.” The Socialist ideal, “in conflict with and yet informing the body of Capitalism, produces a third thing very different from the Socialist ideal — to wit, the Servile State.”

He offers a metaphor: a traveller wishing to escape mountain cold goes south by a river; the mountains will “divert the course of this River northward again”; “the big bend has already begun.” The traveller is the Socialist; the South is the Collectivist State; the River is modern “Organised Reform”; the northern bed is “a society reposing upon compulsory labour.” The only discrepancy is that the traveller could leave the river and go south by land — corresponding to “a bold policy of Confiscation.” He nowhere denies this is “ideally possible,” as it is ideally possible that “to-morrow all Englishmen shall take and preserve for twenty-four hours a vow of silence.” “Nothing like it or approaching it has ever been done or is now being done.” With every step along existing lines of change “we are making it more and more difficult to retrace such steps.” “The path of Confiscation, the only way by which Socialists can reach their goal, gets more and more remote with every new and positive economic reform, undertaken, remember, with the aid and under the advice of Socialists themselves.”

He recasts three misconceptions: (1) that he used “servile” rhetorically for “irksome” or “oppressive,” whereas labour is “servile” which is undertaken “not in fulfilment of contract but under the compulsion of positive law and which attaches to the status of the labourer, and is performed for the benefit of others who are under no such compulsion”; (2) that the Servile State is put forward as a “warning or a danger-sign” — he is concerned “to say how and why we are approaching it; not whether we should approach it”; (3) that he has mis-stated Socialist aims, which “are simple enough”; “we are not heading towards them” and Socialist doctrine upon Capitalist society produces “a third thing different from either of its two begetters.”

The argument that slavery was transformed and the Pagan Servile State approached a Distributive State “under the influence of the Catholic Church is not a piece of special pleading put forward to please my co-religionists.” “It is a plain piece of historical fact.” “Whether the servile institution be a good or a bad thing, it did, as a matter of fact, slowly disappear as Catholic civilisation developed; and it has, as a matter of fact, slowly begun to return where Catholic civilisation has receded.” He has not said that a “completely free Distributive State” was ever reached: it was “in process of formation when the disruption of our united European civilisation in the sixteenth century arrested its development and slowly produced, in this country especially, Capitalism in its stead.”

State regulation and municipal economic enterprise “obviously do not affect my argument.” Until based on Confiscation they are “no more an example of Socialism than the explosion of gunpowder is an example of warfare,” or Crystal Palace fireworks “experiments in militarism.” Socialism’s “essence consists in vesting in trust with the politicians what is now private property.” Municipal and State enterprise based on loans “devised to avoid Confiscation” is “a negation of Socialism”; attempts to mask the capitalist character by sinking funds “are logically worthless. You cannot ‘buy out’ Capitalism.”

Since first publication: “We already have Wages Boards in one great industry; we shall shortly have them in more.” Registration of the proletariat — name, address, movement, illness, supposed or real “malingering,” drink, domestic habits, employment — is “very nearly complete,” imposed by “the wealthier classes who are the actual gatherers of the Poll Tax upon which this registration is based.” Labour Exchanges will register every proletarian as a worker, “his tendencies to rebellion against Capital,” whether and when he has “refused service.” The concluding pages mean that “no change in European society arrives at completion unless it is universal throughout Europe.” Capitalism is not thus universal; “the advent of servitude is therefore a probability differing in degree.” “The example of economic freedom elsewhere may in the future transform, and will certainly limit,” sections drifting toward slavery. “But the tendency to the re-establishment of slavery as a necessary development of Capitalism is patent wherever Capitalism has power, and nowhere more than in this country.”

Introduction — The Subject of This Book

The book is written to maintain “the following truth”: “That our free modern society in which the means of production are owned by a few being necessarily in unstable equilibrium, it is tending to reach a condition of stable equilibrium BY THE ESTABLISHMENT OF COMPULSORY LABOUR LEGALLY ENFORCEABLE UPON THOSE WHO DO NOT OWN THE MEANS OF PRODUCTION FOR THE ADVANTAGE OF THOSE WHO DO.” With compulsion there must come a difference of status: men divided into two sets, the first “economically free and politically free, possessed of the means of production,” the second “economically unfree and politically unfree, but at first secured by their very lack of freedom in certain necessaries of life and in a minimum of well-being beneath which they shall not fall.” Such a society would be “released from its present internal strains” and “capable of being indefinitely prolonged without change.” To that society he gives the title of The Servile State.

He will not judge whether the approaching organisation be “good or evil,” only show “the necessary tendency towards it” and “the recent social provisions which show that it has actually begun.” The new state will be acceptable to those who desire “the re-establishment among us of a difference of status between possessor and non-possessor”; distasteful to those who regard that distinction “with ill favour or with dread.” His business is “to point out to each and to both that that which the one favours and the other would fly is upon them.”

He will prove the thesis “in particular from the case of the industrial society of Great Britain, including that small, alien, and exceptional corner of Ireland, which suffers or enjoys industrial conditions to-day.” The scheme: definitions; ancient slavery and the Servile State; dissolution of slavery in the Christian centuries and the mediaeval system of highly divided property; wreck of that system in certain areas, especially England, and substitution of Capitalism; instability of Capitalism (conflict of social realities with law; denial of sufficiency and security); the only three solutions — Collectivism, the Distributive State, or the Servile State; why Collectivism appeals most easily to a society already Capitalist; how pursuit of Collectivism leads not toward Collectivism “but to that third utterly different thing — the Servile State,” with an appendix that public purchase is “based upon an illusion”; concluding examples from “modern English legislation” that “the Servile State is actually upon us.”

Section I — Definitions

Man lives by transforming his environment. That “special, conscious, and intelligent transformation” is the Production of Wealth. Wealth is “matter which has been consciously and intelligently transformed from a condition in which it is less to a condition in which it is more serviceable to a human need.” “Without Wealth man cannot exist.” In England certain prepared food, clothing, fuel, and habitation are necessary. “To control the production of wealth is to control human life itself.”

Human energy applied to nature is Labour; material and natural forces he conventionally calls Land. A third factor arises from tools and from stores that maintain labour during production. Wealth reserved for future production, not immediate consumption, is Capital. The three factors are Land, Capital, and Labour. The Means of Production are “land and capital combined.” A man “master only of his labour” and without useful control of capital or land is proletarian; a considerable class of such men is a proletariat.

Property is the arrangement vesting control of land and wealth made from land in some person or corporation, guaranteed by law “to use it or withhold it from use.” Private property is distinguished not by the owner’s being less than the State, but because “the owner may exercise his control over it to his own advantage, and not as a trustee for society.” Mr Jones owns as Mr Jones, not as a citizen of Manchester; the municipality owns only as a political body.

An ideal society with the means of production in the hands of political officers is Collectivist, or more generally Socialist (save in this special sense “Socialist” “has either no clear meaning, or is used synonymously with other older and better-known words”). A society in which private ownership of the means of production is confined to some number of free citizens “not large enough to determine the social mass,” the rest proletarian, is Capitalist. The two marks of the Capitalist State: citizens are politically free, yet divided into capitalist and proletarian so that the State is characterised “by the restriction of ownership to a section markedly less than the whole.”

The Servile State: “That arrangement of society in which so considerable a number of the families and individuals are constrained by positive law to labour for the advantage of other families and individuals as to stamp the whole community with the mark of such labour we call The Servile State.” A society is not servile where men labour from enthusiasm, religion, fear of destitution, love of gain, or common sense. “Where there is compulsion applicable by positive law to men of a certain status, and such compulsion enforced in the last resort by the powers at the disposal of the State, there is the institution of Slavery.” The free man “can refuse his labour and use that refusal as an instrument wherewith to bargain; while the slave has no such instrument.” The mere presence of some slavery does not make a State servile; nor is a State servile in which all citizens must labour at official discretion. “A servile condition only exists by contrast with a free condition”: slaves work under positive law “for the benefit” of free citizens.

“Servile” “in no way connotes the worst, nor even necessarily a bad, arrangement of society.” Whether slavery is good or bad “has nothing whatever to do with the exact definition.” A man guaranteed against insecurity and insufficiency, with subsistence in old age, “would be a great deal better off than a free man lacking all these things. But the argument does not affect the definition.” Ownership of the slave by a particular master is not essential; one may conceive a class compelled to labour for a free class “without any direct act of possession.” A man compelled only part of the time, or a serf “regardant” rather than “in gross,” may still be a slave; if hours are many and the class numerous, the State is Servile. Fluctuating composition (income tests, manual-labour definitions, entry and exit) still leaves a permanent institution.

Contract of service for a week is not slavery; a life-contract binding a man and his minor children for “bare subsistence” is. “The enforcing of a contract of service certainly or probably leaving a choice after its expiration is consonant with freedom. The enforcement of a contract probably binding one’s whole life is not.” A life-contract at a wage that soon makes a man wealthy is not slavery, because “it is in the essence of slavery that subsistence or little more than subsistence should be guaranteed to the slave.” The test of the Servile Condition is “the withdrawal from a man of his free choice to labour or not to labour, here or there, for such and such an object; and the compelling of him by positive law to labour for the advantage of others who do not fall under the same compulsion.”

Europeans “sprang” from a “purely Servile conception of production.” The Church slowly released Europe from that conception; industrial or Capitalist society is “now upon its return. We are re-establishing the slave.”

Section II — Our Civilisation Was Originally Servile

From two thousand years ago upward, “one fundamental institution whereupon the whole of society reposes; that fundamental institution is Slavery.” Mediterranean city-states, Celtic tribes, and German hordes “all indifferently reposed upon slavery. It was a fundamental conception of society. It was everywhere present, nowhere disputed.” European ancestry “took slavery for granted, made of it the economic pivot upon which the production of wealth should turn.” It was not a matter of conquering races enslaving subject races: “The Greek had a Greek slave, the Latin a Latin slave.” “The most striking feature of that Servile Basis upon which Paganism reposed was the human equality recognised between master and slave.” Emancipation coexisted with slavery; great men were “little troubled by the memory of a servile ancestry.” Recruitment was perpetual: “It was poverty that made the slave,” with war and piracy as other modes; “the slave bred the slave.”

Pagan society was divided into the citizen with a voice, often labouring freely and normally possessing property, and a mass “dispossessed of the means of production and compelled by positive law to labour at command.” Urban proletarians in late Paganism “never existed in a sufficient proportion to stamp the whole State.” Men accepted slavery “freely as an alternative to indigence.” There was “no organised effort” and “no complaint of conscience against the institution.” “You have no martyrs for the case of ‘freedom’ as against ‘slavery.‘” Servile wars were resistance to recapture, not affirmation that servitude is intolerable. “Slavery is irksome, undignified, woeful; but it is, to them, of the nature of things.” “Men were happy in that arrangement, or, at least, as happy as men ever are.” Slavery was “of the very stuff of Europe” until “that considerable moral experiment called The Faith.” Among the by-products of the Christian Church was “the exceedingly gradual transformation of the Servile State into something other: a society of owners.”

Section III — How the Servile Institution Was for a Time Dissolved

The disappearance of slavery among Christian men covered “close upon a thousand years.” The Growth of the Church “included no attack upon the Servile Institution.” “No dogma of the Church pronounced Slavery to be immoral.” Emancipation was a good work, as among Pagans; sale of Christians to Pagan masters was abhorrent as “treason to civilisation,” not because slavery was condemned.

The unit of production became the Villa: land owned by a dominus in absolute proprietorship, cultivated by slaves “to whom he owed nothing in return.” Custom then limited the slave’s dues; slaves rooted to a spot; surplus left to them as an “implied bargain.” By the ninth century the estate divided into domain (lord’s land, worked at fixed hours), land in villenage (almost in the slaves’ possession, dues to the bailiff), and common waste with minutely remembered rights (for example, of three hundred swine on beech pasture, the lord might put in fifty). Sale of men became exceptional, then almost unknown. The Latin servus must now be translated “serf,” not “slave.”

The early mediaeval serf is “already nearly a peasant”: bound in theory to the soil, but in practice his family tills its quota and dues do not fail. Members enter professions and the Church or the towns. Compulsory labour is commuted for money. By the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century squire and peasant both say of the land “These are my lands” / “This is my land.” The peasant cannot be evicted; dues are a fraction of produce; the holding is inheritable; he buys, sells, saves, invests, builds, drains “to his own profit.”

Town industry is organised in Guilds: mainly private owners of capital, self-governing, designed “to check competition,” “safeguard the division of property,” and prevent a proletariat and a monopolising capitalist. Apprentices become masters. Freehold increases. Three forms — serf with fractional dues, freeholder paying what is “more of a tax than a rent,” and Guild — make for a society based on property: “All, or most, — the normal family — should own.” Restraints on purchase, sale, mortgage, and inheritance are “restraints designed for the preservation of liberty.” Collectivism has not replaced slavery: common land is “jealously guarded by men who were also personal proprietors”; guild common property is halls, relief funds, endowments; tools are owned by members. “The Middle Ages had instinctively conceived and brought into existence the Distributive State.”

That consummation was destroyed, “more particularly in Britain.” The usual explanation — the Industrial Revolution, expensive machinery enslaving men “apart from the human will” — “is wholly false.” “It was the deliberate action of men, evil will in a few and apathy of will among the many.” “The Industrial System was a growth proceeding from Capitalism, not its cause.” “It was not machinery that lost us our freedom; it was the loss of a free mind.”

Section IV — How the Distributive State Failed

At the close of the Middle Ages Western Christendom, England included, was “economically free.” In England the arrangement was ruined. Seeds were sown in the sixteenth century; effects appeared in the seventeenth; in the eighteenth England was “finally, though insecurely, established upon a proletarian basis”; in the nineteenth it became “a purely Capitalist State, the type and model of Capitalism for the whole world,” with “nineteen-twentieths” dispossessed.

The first step was mishandling of the sixteenth-century economic revolution. Monastic lands were taken intending to vest them in the Crown, but passed to “an already wealthy section” who became “the governing power of England.” Early Tudor England still had a mass owning land, houses, and implements, but unequally. Land and fixtures then formed “very much more than all other forms of wealth combined.” A wealthy land-owning class already held “rather more than a quarter, though less than a third” of land and fixtures; the squire owned perhaps a quarter to a third of village land, received dues, and for a century had been the local Justice.

What happened in France — peasants buying out lords — “might perfectly well have happened here,” but was interrupted by “an artificial revolution”: seizure of monastic lands. The Church was lord of something over 25 per cent, “say 28 per cent., or perhaps nearly 30 per cent.,” of agricultural communities, and owned about 30 per cent. of demesne. After 1535 something “well over 20 per cent.” was confiscated — “by far the most complete, the most sudden, and the most momentous” economic revolution in any European people. Had the Crown kept the land, “the English State and its government would have been the most powerful in Europe,” using economic power “for the weakening of the wealthier classes, and to the indirect advantage of the mass of the people.” “The King failed to keep the lands he had seized.” Large owners obtained grants “sometimes freely, sometimes for ridiculously small sums.” They added “a new and extra fifth” to their third and “became at a blow the owners of half the land.” Clerical landlords had been “customary — leaving much to the tenant”; the new owners were “strictly competitive.” Great country houses date from this revolution. Under Edward VI “the loot went on at an appalling rate”; under Mary it was “nearly completed.” Howards, Cavendishes, Cecils, Russells, “and fifty other new families thus rose upon the ruins of religion.”

By 1630–40 “a powerful oligarchy of large owners” overshadowed “an impoverished and dwindled monarchy.” Fall in the purchasing power of money (to about a third) hit the Crown, whose receipts were customary and expenses competitive. The Crown lost the Civil Wars; after 1660 the King was “in practice a salaried puppet.” By 1700 “more than half of the English were dispossessed.” “By that date England had already become CAPITALIST.” “It is this and not the so-called ‘Industrial Revolution,’ a later thing, which accounts for the terrible social condition in which we find ourselves to-day.”

Industrial development coming upon a people “already largely lost its economic freedom… took at its very origin a Capitalist form.” Industrialism spread from England “upon the Capitalist model.” Newcomen (1705), Watt, Hargreaves, Darby of Coalbrookdale, Kay’s flying shuttle, and the lifetime of Dr Johnson cover the transformation. The textbook claim that new machinery “fatally and of themselves” produced Capitalism is “unhistorical.” Capitalisation of new industry had to come from the class that already monopolised accumulations; cheap labour came from the existing proletariat created “when, in cornering the wealth of the country after the Reformation, they had thrust out the mass of Englishmen from the possession of implements, of houses, and of land.” In a thirteenth-century society the same discoveries “would have blest and enriched mankind.” Common rights over common land were destroyed “deliberately and of set purpose.” There is not an example of so-called “Social Reform” to-day which is not demonstrably “directed to the further entrenchment” of a society in which “a few shall own” and the majority “live at a wage under them… but not by property; not by freedom.”

Section V — The Capitalist State in Proportion as It Grows Perfect Grows Unstable

Capitalism is “more properly a transitory phase lying between two permanent and stable states of society.” Recalling the definition: ownership confined to a body of free citizens not large enough to characterise society, the rest proletarian. There will be “conscious, direct, and planned exploitation” of the majority by the minority, because “wealth must be produced” and possessors can make terms that take a portion of what non-possessors produce.

Two strains increase as society becomes more thoroughly Capitalist. First, divergence between moral theories and social facts. Law still presupposes free citizens: it defends property as normal, punishes theft and fraud as abnormal, enforces contract on the moral base of freedom not to contract, allows wills, casts damages assuming ability to pay. The sanction of social life is legal punishment; the basis of security is possession of goods. Contrast England today: property as experience “is unknown to nineteen out of twenty.” Many frauds of unrestrained competition “are not or cannot be punished.” Legal machinery has become “little more than an engine for protecting the few owners.” The vast bulk of so-called free contracts are “leonine contracts”: one party free to take or leave, the other not, “because the second had for his alternative starvation.” Livelihood “is at the will of the possessors.” “Most men now fear the loss of employment more than they fear legal punishment.” “The true master of the Englishman to-day is not the Sovereign nor the officers of State, nor, save indirectly, the laws; his true master is the Capitalist.” Things came to a head late because even England “did not itself become a completely Capitalist State until the present generation”; within living memory half England was agricultural, “relations domestic rather than competitive.”

Second, Capitalism destroys security. Labour of the non-owner fetches what it is worth as leaving a surplus to the Capitalist: “nothing when the labourer cannot work… nothing in sickness; nothing in despair.” Capital purchases “a cross-section of his life, his moments of activity.” “A perfect Capitalist State cannot exist”; left completely free there would be “so heavy a mortality from starvation as would dry up the sources of labour.” Capitalism “must keep alive, by non-Capitalist methods, great masses of the population”: Elizabeth’s Poor Law, the Poor Law of 1834, and “to-day a hundred others.” Competitive anarchy — underselling, overproduction, gluts, miscalculation, waste in commissions and advertisements — makes employment precarious (commercial travellers, advertising and insurance agents, touts). Freedom suffers: competition is restricted by understandings, ruin of smaller competitors, “secret conspiracies” supported by “secret political forces” (telephones, South Wales coal, the defeated soap trust, soda, fish, fruit; first step is to “interest” a politician). Trusts exist because “political freedom is not, as a fact, protected here by the Courts in commercial affairs.” England is “no longer possessed of that absolute political freedom which true Capitalism essentially demands.” Novel features already include permission of conspiracy among possessors and non-possessors and “compulsory provision of security through State action.”

Section VI — The Stable Solutions of This Instability

Unstable equilibrium seeks stable equilibrium (pyramid on its apex; explosives). There are “but three social arrangements which can replace Capitalism: Slavery, Socialism, and Property.” Mixtures are imaginable; no fourth arrangement can be devised. The problem turns on control of the means of production. To solve Capitalism you must get rid of restricted ownership, or of freedom, or of both. The only alternative to freedom is legal compulsion to work: “in the second he is by definition a slave.” Direct re-establishment of slavery “would provide a true solution” and “is the probable goal which our society will in fact approach,” but “the surviving Christian tradition of our civilisation compels men to reject” open advocacy.

Leaving freedom untouched, two courses remain: put property into the hands of many, or of none. “None” means vesting it as a trust in political officers. “The only alternative to private property is public property. Somebody must see to the ploughing and must control the ploughs.” The first model is Socialism or the Collectivist State; the second the Proprietary or Distributive State. The kernel of the thesis: “The Capitalist State breeds a Collectivist Theory which in action produces something utterly different from Collectivism: to wit, the SERVILE STATE.”

Section VII — Socialism Is the Easiest Apparent Solution of the Capitalist Crux

The line of least resistance leads a Capitalist State to transform itself into a Servile State, because Collectivism is easier to aim at than Distribution, yet the attempt produces “the servitude of the many, and the confirmation in their present privilege of the few.”

Those who would restore the Distributive State are “Conservatives or Traditionalists,” respecting “the old forms of Christian European life,” dealing with “known realities” tested by experience. They are less practical in another sense: the stage of the disease “does not readily lend itself to such a reaction.” Collectivists deal with something “hitherto imaginary”; one cannot examine Socialism “in actual working.” Yet buying out a railway or harbour, or municipalising water, gas, tramways, “does not disturb any fundamental thing.” Creating many small shareholders would arouse opposition and leave owners free “to sell again to a few.” The distributor of property “is working against the grain of our existing Capitalist society”; the Collectivist “with the grain.” The first physician tells atrophied limbs to exercise; the second, to be “wheeled about” consonant to the disease. “The Physician is the Reformer, his Patient the Proletariat.”

Confiscation and redistribution at a blow raise insoluble questions of choosing new owners and “enormous and innumerable separate acts of injustice.” Building small property slowly means reversing the economy of interest on deposits: it is far easier to save £100 from £1000 than £10 from £100. One must “deliberately subsidise small savings.” Penalising few owners leaves a majority who “cannot even bid for the smallest share.” Strongest is the moral force: “Will men want to own?” Can one count on workmen not squandering gifted shares, or on managers serving poor owners as they serve rich? The proletarian mass “thinks in terms not of property but of ‘employment.‘” Restoration of property has “a note of oddity… like recommending elasticity to the aged.”

Collectivism works with existing machinery, “appeals to just those appetites which Capitalism has aroused,” and ridicules memory Capitalism has killed. Stupider Collectivists welcome a trust as furnishing “a mode of transition from private to public ownership.” Collectivism promises employment, pensions, promotion, security “vastly increased” because the State guarantees it. If Coal and Railways were handed to the State tomorrow, armies of men would find “no change in the character of their lives, save in some increase of security and possibly in a very slight increase of earnings.” To owners Collectivism is an enemy “with whom they can treat”: taking over a trust paying 4 per cent. if the State can make 5 per cent. “takes the form of a business proposition.” The State’s greater credit can seemingly “buy out” (an illusion reserved for later). Discipline would be “the same rules as those by which the Capitalist imposes discipline.”

A magical England of small owners would be “an enormous revolution”: insolence of the poor, laziness of the contented, “rebellious, vigorous personalities.” Slow Collectivism would not be conspicuous: the hopeless margin would disappear into “isolated work-places of a penal kind”; large incomes would become salaries; shopkeepers absorbed at salaries; remaining small owners would notice “inspection and… onerous petty taxation.” The last generation of Collectivists thought only “unintelligence” stood in the way. Today “the practical effect of their propaganda is not an approach towards the Collectivist State at all,” but toward a State in which “the mass of men shall be constrained by law to labour to the profit of a minority, but, as the price of such constraint, shall enjoy a security which the old Capitalism did not give them.”

Section VIII — The Reformers and the Reformed Are Alike Making for the Servile State

Three interests account for nearly all forces making for social change: the Socialist (theoretical reformer on the line of least resistance); the “Practical Man”; and the proletarian mass.

The Socialist movement comprises (a) the man who regards public ownership (and “consequent compulsion of all citizens to work under the direction of the State”) as the only feasible solution, and (b) the man who loves the Collectivist ideal in itself: officials who “shall order other men about and so preserve them from the consequences of their vice, ignorance, and folly.”

Type (a) demands confiscation, but owners have “a stony moral barrier” — “the moral basis of property” — and “innumerable complexities of modern ownership.” Even reverting enclosures since 1760 would wreck “thousands of exchanges” and “twenty times the wealth involved.” He is led to “buying out,” based on “an economic error.” Meanwhile he can cure destitution and insecurity without confiscation by modifying freedom. The Capitalist replies: “I refuse to be dispossessed… But if you will define the relation between my employees and myself, I will undertake particular responsibilities… Subject the proletarian, as a proletarian… to special laws. Clothe me, the Capitalist, as a Capitalist… with special converse duties… Nay… such a novel arrangement will make my own profits perhaps larger and certainly more secure.” Confiscation is dammed; “securing human conditions for the proletariat” is the open sluice. When transformation is complete “there will be no ground left… for public ownership. The reformer only asked for it in order to secure security and sufficiency: he has obtained his demand.” Owners remain owners; the mass still works for a few who enjoy surplus values; insecurity and insufficiency, “in the main the product of freedom, have been eliminated by the destruction of freedom.”

Type (b) is not indignant at exploitation. He loves “tables, statistics, an exact framework,” the “running” of men “as a machine is run.” Complexity offends him; he wants “a vast bureaucracy” scheduled by clerks and department heads. He too cannot confiscate; he cares more for tabulation and elimination of private power to react against his Department. He has but to register the proletariat and ensure that neither their freedom nor the employer’s produces insufficiency or insecurity. “Let laws exist which make the proper housing, feeding, clothing, and recreation of the proletarian mass be incumbent upon the possessing class,” imposed “by inspection and punishment.” For him the Servile State is “a tolerable alternative” he “regards favourably.” Such reformers are now “less concerned with any scheme for socialising Capital and Land than with innumerable schemes… for regulating, ‘running,’ and drilling the proletariat without trenching by an inch upon the privilege” of the small Capitalist class. This type “has not fallen into the Servile State by a miscalculation. He has fathered it.”

The Practical Man prides himself on not being a socialist. His disabilities are “an inability to define his own first principles and an inability to follow the consequences proceeding from his own action,” from “the inability to think.” His first principles are the same as others’: insufficiency and insecurity are intolerable. Unemployment, destitution, slums “often unemployable,” “the ravages of drink,” breeding children, and the fact that “such men do not work unless you make them” occupy him. He knows nothing of a society of free owners. “He ‘takes the world as he finds it’” and “positively gloats on every new detail in the building up” of the Servile State. “The destruction of freedom by inches (though he does not see it to be the destruction of freedom) is the one panacea.” Modern exchange gives him wealth and political power; of history, philosophy, religion, and “human nature itself he is blank.” Left to himself he would produce “a welter of anarchic restrictions” and revolt; he is “the ally or flanking party of great forces” used “with gratitude and contempt.” The Servile State, needing “lucid thought in those who govern, will certainly eliminate him.”

The mass is proletarian — in England about “nineteen-twentieths,” excluding Ireland, where reaction against Capitalism “is already successful.” Three factors: memory of ancestral property and freedom; the gambling chance of entering the Capitalist class; appetite for security and sufficiency. Traditional rights of property remain strong — theft, legacy, exchange, gift, contract — but actual experience has gone. Belloc spoke as a boy to labourers near Oxford who had risked their skins against enclosure, and in Lancashire to men who remembered small ownership of cottage looms. “The generation brought up under the Education Acts of the last forty years… has grown up definitely and hopelessly proletarian.” They do not seek property; possessors are “a class apart” whose position they accept as “immemorial.” To increase the weekly stipend is vivid; “to make him cease to be a wage-earner” seems “outside the realities of life.” The chance of rising, “usually by various forms of villainy,” has “very greatly lost in force”; in transport and mines men have “quite given up such an expectation.”

If one offered millions of wage-families a life-contract of service guaranteeing usual full wage, “how many would refuse?” Such a contract “is the negation of contract and the acceptation of status,” forced labour “coterminous… with his power to labour,” “permanent renunciation” of surplus values. “Very few would refuse it.” “That is the key to the whole matter.” What the mass most fears is not the Courts but “the sack.” Truck-Act fines, silence, insults are accepted because of unemployment. “Private law has for the second time in our long European story overcome public law.” In the seventeenth century a man feared to go to Mass lest judges punish him; today he fears to speak a social theory “lest his master should punish him.” Benefits requiring inquisition by officials and administration by the Capitalist the man serves do not prevent preference of the benefit to freedom. “After the long terrors imposed upon them through a freedom unaccompanied by property, they see, at the expense of losing a mere legal freedom, the very real prospect of having enough and not losing it.” Substitute “serf” for “employee” or “master” for “employer” and blunt words “might breed revolt”; sudden full Servile conditions would; first great steps meet “acquiescence and for the most part gratitude.”

“The Servile State is, in industrial England to-day, no longer a menace but something in actual existence… The corner-stone of it is already laid.”

Appendix on “Buying-Out”

Those who would expropriate without direct confiscation imagine the State could “buy-out” the Capitalist class “without their knowing it.” “It is impossible by any jugglery to ‘buy-out’ the universality of the means of production without confiscation.”

A parable: twenty-two families, two capitalist farms, twenty proletarian; 300 measures of wheat, 200 to labour (10 each), 100 surplus (50 to each capitalist family). The State wants the surplus distributed and itself unembarrassed owner. Capital is accumulated for a minimum yearly return; below that men spend. The Law of Diminishing Returns checks accumulation at the “Effective Desire of Accumulation” (E.D.A.). In England today E.D.A. is “a little over 3 per cent.,” about one-thirtieth. Capitalists sell at “so many years’ purchase” (they even price land, which “need no accumulation,” on this analogy — the illusion that makes Irish land purchase work for the dispossessed). Two capitalists at one-thirtieth want 3000 measures; the State has no such store.

The State might promise more than 100 measures a year for thirty years, paying extras from taxes on the capitalists themselves — “ringing of the changes” they would resist as they resist confiscation. Masking by buying small areas seriatim, or taxing unpopular trades (brewers in a half-Puritan society) then others, will rouse suspicion (railway stock is another matter). The check: the Capitalist takes extra revenue to reinvest. Exchange of Brighton railway stock yielding £30 for State paper paying £50 for thirty-eight years delights him because he reinvests the extra £20 at 3 per cent. and ends richer. On a small scale, subsidised from general taxation, the trick works briefly; beyond a narrow limit “the market for investment” restricts, Capital takes alarm, guarantees cost more, “confiscatory” rates meet the same forces as open expropriation. “If you desire to confiscate, you must confiscate.”

Two ways without that resistance: (1) promise more than present revenue if the State can manage better — as it could have socialised railways fifty years ago, but socialising the hansom cab in the nineties would endow “that worthy but extinct type the cab-owner (and his children for ever)”; (2) annuities to capitalists without heirs, a “very small” area. Actual “Socialist” measures tax so as to lessen capital, but “not putting the proceeds into the means of production”: they fund “new official salaries” or hand them to another set of Capitalists. Death duties represented by “locomotives in the Argentine” become palings for officials under the Inebriates Bill, or pass to Prudential shareholders under the Insurance Act, or their equivalent to “the Sassoons.”

Section IX — The Servile State Has Begun

Proof is of two sorts: laws and proposals subjecting the proletariat to servile conditions; and confirmation, not expropriation, of the Capitalist by so-called Socialist experiments.

Factory laws are not an origin. Interference that forbids an action to “the citizen as a citizen” (scissors 122 millimetres long for rose-cutting; mill cubic space; fencing machinery; riparian fences) does not distinguish two classes “by a criterion of manual labour or of income.” The Capitalist is not responsible quâ Capitalist, nor the proletarian protected quâ proletarian. Compulsory education applies in legal theory to every citizen in Great Britain (not Ireland), though plutocracy exempts the rich in practice.

“The first example of servile legislation to be discovered upon the Statute Book is that which establishes the present form of Employers Liability.” Its motive was “merely humane,” but it admitted anomaly. Common-sense doctrine: if B, fulfilling a contract with A, damages third party C, C recovers from A, the author of the plan — no distinction of status. If damage is to B himself, who “willingly undertook” known risks (sprained wrist, a kick), compelling A to pay more than the contracted sack of wheat recognises status. There is no converse right of A against B unless B is culpable. “You are a worker, and therefore an inferior.” The principle is pushed when A is liable for injury by one employee to another (B lets slip the rope lowering D): D recovers not from B but from innocent A. “They can claim protection from A, as inferiors of a superior in a State admitting such distinctions and patronage.” That the workman is grateful because a fellow workman cannot pay “is not the point… It remains servile legislation none the less.” A parallel: a publisher is not liable if a historian of Rutland falls down a pit; if the same man, in mean clothes, is wounded by a fish cleaning the publisher’s ornamental water, the publisher is mulcted.

Later measures, “already law” or “upon the threshold,” “definitely recognise the Servile State.” They fall into three categories: (1) relief of proletarian insecurity through the employing class or the proletariat under compulsion; (2) compelling the employer to give not less than a certain minimum; (3) compelling a man lacking the means of production to labour though he has made no contract. The last two are complementary.

Of (1) the Insurance Act “follows in every particular the lines of a Servile State.” Its criterion is employment: compulsion if exchanging services for goods and obtaining less than a certain amount, or being “a vulgar fellow working with his hands.” It excludes labour of “the educated and therefore powerful classes” and those earning enough to be “economically free.” A writer of books in distress would not be compelled as a citizen via income tax. “Still more striking”: the duty of seeing the law obeyed is put “not upon the proletariat itself, but upon the Capitalist class.” The future historian “will most certainly fix upon that one point as the cardinal landmark of our times”: two categories, the lower man compelled to registration and a tax, the upper man “the instrument in enforcing that registration and in collecting that tax.” Comparison is to substitution of tenure for Roman proprietary right, or mediaeval peasant for Dark Age serf.

Minimum wage as a statutory sum had not entered the laws as of September 1912, but mining had legal sanction for a hypothetical minimum arrived at by local boards, claimable “by force of law.” The next step, a sliding scale according to prices and profits, is “easy and natural”: capital gets guarantee against disturbance; labour sufficiency and security. The proletarian produces a total, retains a portion, leaves surplus value; he loses “the power to refuse his labour and thus to aim at putting himself in possession of the means of production.” Parliamentary debate on the miners did not turn on their owning the mines or the State owning them, but on “the minimum wage… at a certain satisfactory level.” Both parties “were agreed upon that fundamental change.” Experiments will extend piecemeal as combinations in trades “immediately necessary to the life of the nation” arise. Two and a half millions guaranteed against unemployment at a weekly sum related to estimated earnings: “a short step” to statutory sums in employment, lest a man get more unemployed than employed or the difference fail to tempt work. State statistics and constraint throw “the whole weight of its influence… in favour of regulation.” Enforceable contracts between groups of labour and capital work from the other side.

A minimum wage involves compulsory labour in two forms. First, Courts must constrain either party to a ratified bargain (ten measures produced, six to labour, four surplus). Extend the system “over a long period of years,” make it habit, and it becomes compulsory labour: agricultural labourers who have long taken fifteen shillings, officials having put their hands to it, must “return within the limits of that contract or suffer the consequences.” Slight threat already controls men living “from week to week under peril of discharge.” Insurance sums are “not even in the hands of some society which he can really control” but of a Government official: refuse work at twenty-five shillings and “you certainly shall not have a right to the money you have been compelled to put aside.” Labour Exchanges accumulate dossiers: “No man, once so registered and known, can escape.” Compulsory arbitration is “the obvious bludgeon… a frank admission of servitude at one step”; “no civilised European state” has succumbed, but it “has twice been brought forward… as a Bill in Parliament.”

Second: what of the man who cannot produce even the six measures? Capital will not be constrained to pay more than he produces; reforms leave profit “a necessity” and aim at “reconciling… the interests of capital and labour.” Gratuitous support “is to put a premium upon incapacity and sloth.” “The man must be made to work.” “Hence he is necessarily a subject for forced labour.” The “Labour Colony” — “a prison so called because euphemism is necessary to every transition” — will absorb this surplus and “crown the edifice.”

Complementary truth: translation of the means of production from private owners to public officials “is nowhere being attempted.” Municipalisation and nationalisation proceed by loan. Capitalists lend cars and rails; they require their old profit plus a sinking fund representing original value. “Interest” is continued surplus values; “sinking fund” the instalments. In theory a section might be socialised from general taxation; “as a fact things are never so favourable.” Implements are sold above true value; purchase includes “commissions… champagne luncheons… lawyers’ fees… bribes” and non-productive pleasure gardens, wash-houses, baths, libraries, monuments, town halls treated as if reproductive; many purchases fail just before a new invention; borrowing outruns repayment. European experiments have produced indebtedness increasing “rather more than twice, but not three times, as fast as the rate of repayment.” Interest exceeds produce by “rather more than 1½ per cent.,” even counting successful state railways and municipal enterprises. “Capitalism has seen to it that it shall be a winner and not a loser by this form of sham Socialism.”

The future of industrial society left to itself: subsistence and security guaranteed for the proletariat “at the expense of the old political freedom” and establishment “in a status really, though not nominally, servile,” owners guaranteed in profits, machinery smooth, stability restored. Internal strains relax. The community settles “upon that Servile basis which was its foundation before the advent of the Christian faith, from which that faith slowly weaned it, and to which in the decay of that faith it naturally returns.”

Conclusion

The past can be portrayed if one has time for research and power to integrate detail. Of the future “no one can say even in its largest aspect… He can only present the main tendencies of his time… determin[e] the equation of the curve.” Societies that “broke with the continuity of Christian civilisation in the sixteenth century — which means, roughly, North Germany and Great Britain — tend at present to the re-establishment of a Servile Status… diversified by local accident… hidden under many forms. But it will come.”

“Mere Capitalist anarchy cannot endure.” Only a very few solutions exist; Belloc does not believe there are more than two: “a reaction towards well-divided property, or the re-establishment of servitude.” He cannot believe “theoretical Collectivism, now so plainly failing, will ever inform a real and living society.”

That conviction does not yield “meagre and mechanical prophecy.” “There is a complex knot of forces underlying any nation once Christian; a smouldering of the old fires.” Some European societies will reject the Servile solution as they rejected or suspected Capitalism and industrial “progress”: “in the main the same as those which, in that great storm of the sixteenth century… held fast to tradition” — “chief among them… the French and the Irish.” He records as “an impression (and no more)” that the Servile State, the tide making for it “in Prussia and in England,” “will be modified, checked, perhaps defeated in war, certainly halted… by the strong reaction which these freer societies upon its flank will perpetually exercise.” “Ireland has decided for a free peasantry.” In France experiments that elsewhere introduced the Servile State “have been contemptuously rejected”; “a recent attempt to register and to ‘insure’ the artisans as a separate category of citizens has broken down in the face of an universal and a virile contempt.” Free societies will modify the tendency “by example and perhaps by direct attack.” “As I am upon the whole hopeful that the Faith will recover its intimate and guiding place in the heart of Europe, so I believe that this sinking back into our original Paganism (for the tendency to the Servile State is nothing less) will in due time be halted and reversed.”

Videat Deus.
