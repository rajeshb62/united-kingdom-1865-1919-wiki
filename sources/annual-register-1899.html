The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1899

The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1899

Sep 15, 202619 min read

The Annual Register 1899

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Affairs Abroad and at Home; Little Englander Debate; Church; Madagascar; Soudan; Peace Proposals

The new year opened outwardly peaceful yet under undiminished European tension. The Czar’s invitation to make peace the primary aim of policy was widely treated as delusive or chimerical; hope of better understanding with the United States seemed the brighter counterweight to Britain’s patent isolation in the European Concert. Fashoda and the Nile campaign had stirred French ill-will; Germany’s high quarters counselled support for British South African policy while public sentiment and trade rivalry stayed hostile; Russia signed peace invitations with one hand while threatening China with the other. Crete at last gained real autonomy under a Christian ruler (Prince George). South Africa remained the empire’s most unsettled quarter: racial feeling hardened, and the murder of a British subject with the subsequent acquittal of the murderer further embittered Transvaal relations.

At home Harcourt’s and Morley’s withdrawal from Liberal front-bench counsels left leadership in abeyance until Parliament. The Cobden Club’s “open door” manifesto — if necessary to be “blown open by artillery” in derelict territories where English interests already existed — was read as imperialist rather than Cobdenite; Sir Edward Clarke at Plymouth (5 January) mocked the fear that European annexation killed British trade. Bye-elections (Mid-Bucks Rothschild unopposed; Newton; Mid-Surrey) showed practical confidence in Salisbury. The Miners’ Federation at Edinburgh (11 January) welcomed South Wales delegates and treated the Compensation Act more kindly than parliamentary Labour critics had, while noting how many fatal accidents fell outside its scope.

Morley at Brechin (17 January) explained his and Harcourt’s withdrawal: Liberal “cross-currents,” imperialism as militarism and waste, objections to Soudan conquest, Fashoda methods, and Omdurman wounded treatment; he denounced war for markets, quoting Clarke against Maxim-and-lyddite commerce. Chamberlain at Wolverhampton (18 January) defined a “little Englander” as one who holds expansion’s obligations out of proportion to its advantages, cited trade-follows-the-flag contrasts (Mauritius/Burmah vs Madagascar/Tonquin), claimed firm gains from France in West Africa, and flagged Madagascar and Newfoundland as still needing settlement. Hicks-Beach at Bristol counselled digesting existing territory rather than Jingo enlargement. Asquith at Louth (16 January) praised yet politically buried Morley, dissented on Fashoda and Soudan, redefined true imperialism as expansion whose advantages are not out of proportion to obligations, and saw no need for a new Liberal programme beyond completing political freedom and national education. Morley at Montrose (19 January) stuck to peace, economy, reform, temperance, old-age pensions caution, and Irish Home Rule as unfinished Liberal debt. Sir Edward Grey at Liverpool (20 January) showed Morley out of step with Commons Liberals: Liberals might co-operate with Irish MPs but should not take office dependent on them; Home Rule was suspended not abandoned; China needed understanding with Russia more than spheres; Opposition criticism was of Government drift, not of holding Egypt/Soudan. National Liberal Club confusion over policy and leadership underlined the party’s reconstitution problem. Devonshire at Birmingham (23 January) defended the Government, denied Cabinet Jingoism, and endorsed free trade (Chamberlain agreeing); Kimberley, interim titular Liberal head, at Wymondham (24 January) soft-pedalled Jingo/little-Englander labels, welcomed Fashoda settlement, worried about Soudan responsibility, and kept Home Rule and Lords reform alive.

Balfour at Manchester (30–31 January) urged Liberal revival for national good, expected Liberals to press Disestablishment more than Lords reform, personally favoured an endowed Roman Catholic university for Ireland against party feeling, and argued bishops should restore Church order before Parliament intervened. A packed Albert Hall Protestant demonstration (31 January) telegraphed the Queen to suppress “Romish practices.” Harcourt’s “Crisis in the Church” agitation, the Register judged, won little Nonconformist or lay-notable support; High and Low views of the Reformation remained irreconcilable.

The Madagascar blue-book, published after Marchand’s Nile withdrawal, showed Salisbury’s July 1898 protest: France’s 1890 assurance that the protectorate would not affect British most-favoured-nation rights and the 10% ad valorem duty ceiling had been flouted by June 1898 tariff increases and local boycotts of English goods — unanswered amid Fashoda. The Anglo-Egyptian Soudan convention defined reconquered territories south of 22° N., dual flags (except Suakin), a Governor-General appointed/removable only with British consent, proclamation rule, no Egyptian laws or Mixed Tribunals, no foreign consuls without British consent, and slavery abolished — signalling Britain’s practical protectorate of Egypt. French press irritation later cooled; Delcassé even complimented Kitchener over Marchand. Count Muravieff’s thirteen Hague proposals (armaments pause, war-budget freeze, Geneva rules at sea, halt to naval/matériel “improvements,” mediation/arbitration machinery, no discussion of treaties’ political status, meeting outside great-Power capitals) were coolly received in Western Europe.

Chapter II — Campbell-Bannerman; Parliament; China; Church; London Government; Transvaal; France Convention

Liberals at the Reform Club (6 February) chose Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman as Commons leader after Harcourt’s final resignation; Fowler and Asquith withdrew, and wording expressing “continued confidence” in Harcourt was added after Atherley-Jones pressure. Campbell-Bannerman pledged a watchful, not reckless, Opposition. Parliament opened by commission (7 February) with a long, dull Queen’s Speech: Omdurman and Soudan order; Cretan government under Prince George; Russian peace conference; anarchist conference after Empress Elizabeth’s murder; West Indian hurricane; Cape naval contribution; Indian plague amid recovering trade; and a legislative menu headed by London government, a Board of Education, Scotch private-bill procedure, small-dwellings purchase aid, Irish agriculture/technical instruction and tithe-rent relief, metropolitan water emergencies, company regulation, food adulteration, money-lenders, Factory Acts, and agricultural holdings — with the Register noting Ministers keen only on London government and unwilling to push social reforms that might split their majority.

Address debates ranged over China/Russia, Soudan status after the Anglo-Egyptian agreement, Church and Parliament, land and Lords reform, Scottish crofters, ministers as company directors, Irish Home Rule and congested districts, bishops’ seats, Egypt, East African slavery, Morley on the Soudan campaign, Muscat, children’s education, and the Army, Navy, and Civil Service Estimates. Outlander grievances and Transvaal restlessness threaded foreign and colonial questions; China policy and Russian railway/sphere pressure filled repeated sittings. The London Government Bill (introduced 23 February under Balfour) left the City’s charters and the 1888 County Council’s metropolitan functions intact while turning vestries/district boards into municipalities with elections and transferred powers — Opposition fearing political motive and incomplete central London government. Second reading followed; peers and Church questions, a Secondary Education Bill, money-lending control, old-age pension and other social bills, Telephone Company–Post Office friction, and Scotch private-bill legislation occupied time. Bye-elections and the National Liberal Federation marked party temperature; Balfour’s published scheme for parallel Catholic Dublin and Protestant Belfast universities stirred Protestant Unionist protest. An Anglo-French convention and Central African settlement eased West African friction after Fashoda-year strain; Rhodes moved in European capitals; Transvaal restlessness grew. Campbell-Bannerman defended his leadership; the Railways Regulation Bill (railway servants’ safety) was withdrawn late.

Chapter III — Socialists at Leeds; Budget; Small Houses; Board of Education; London Bill; Church Discipline; Licensing; Rosebery; Queen’s Birthday

The Easter recess was thin in home speeches while Samoa’s three-Power quarrel produced commissioners with near-absolute powers (British–American co-operation against German officials notable). A Socialist conference at Leeds (31 March) discussed municipal propaganda versus practical local work. Courtney in Cornwall and the Harrow election filled early political notes.

Hicks-Beach’s Budget proposed cutting the fixed debt charge from £25m to £23m, prolonging Savings Bank annuities, cancelling book debt and Consols held for Savings Banks in favour of new terminable annuities, and raising about £870,000 by stamp duties (foreign/colonial securities documents; loan capital/debenture mortgage duty; companies’ capital duty from 2s. to 5s. per £100; allotment/renunciation stamps) plus wine-duty increases — yielding a 1899–1900 balance of revenue £111,157,000 against expenditure £110,927,000 (margin £230,000). Reception was cool even among Conservatives. The Small Houses Acquisition Bill advanced enabling local authorities to help occupiers buy dwellings; St. Paul’s decoration, the Board of Education Bill (primary, secondary, technical administration), London Government in committee, and the Finance Bill filled the middle session. Primrose League Albert Hall and Salvation Army Mansion House gatherings, Education Estimates and the Vice-President’s protest, the Church Discipline Bill (royal supremacy, end of episcopal veto, deprivation machinery — coinciding with archbishops’ sittings), and Irish technical education marked Church and social fronts. Anglo-Russian China agreement papers (tabled after Salisbury’s promise; explained 1 May) barred British railway concessions north of the Great Wall and Russian ones in the Yangtze basin. Transvaal and China questions stayed intertwined with home politics. The Licensing Commission reported; Rosebery spoke on the Liberal party’s condition; the Queen’s eightieth birthday closed the chapter’s domestic calendar.

Chapter IV — Old Age Pensions; Bloemfontein; South African Crisis; Kitchener; London Government; Niger; Peace Congress; Prorogation

Chamberlain on old-age pensions and Liberal speeches by Morley, Spencer, and Harcourt framed Whitsuntide politics. Kruger and Milner met at Bloemfontein (31 May) amid Hague peace hopes; Kruger would discuss all but independence, yet the conference failed — Kruger pressing foreign arbitration of Anglo-Transvaal differences, Milner refusing arbitration between independent and dependent status. Chamberlain told the Commons (8 June) Kruger had rejected Milner’s proposals and the alternative was unacceptable, creating a “new situation.” The 14 June South African blue-book printed Milner’s “helots” telegram (case for intervention “overwhelming” after the Edgar killing) and Chamberlain’s Outlanders’ petition despatch: Britain as paramount Power could not permanently ignore arbitrary treatment, yet wished to avoid intervention and preserve republican independence if legitimate demands were met.

Robson’s Half-Timers Bill raising the factory child-labour age toward twelve survived Lancashire obstruction and Berlin Conference pledges (Gorst). A £30,000 grant and vote of thanks to Kitchener (5 June) exposed Liberal cleavage: Campbell-Bannerman supported the vote while criticising the Mahdi’s remains episode; Morley’s Radical opposition found little party-press sympathy. London Government, Illegal Commissions, Telephone, Lord Charles Beresford on China, Indian tariff, Youthful Offenders, London Government in the Lords, and Tithe Rent-Charge advanced. Bye-elections and Balfour–Chamberlain South African speeches hardened crisis language; Campbell-Bannerman addressed Liberal organisation. The Royal Niger Company bill (Hicks-Beach, 3 July) ended the charter’s administrative role after Franco-British West African friction and the new frontier force: Government took treaty/land/mineral rights and needed plant, paid to redeem Customs-backed debt and “unexhausted improvements,” and left the company as a trader. Transvaal debates, Irish agriculture and technical instruction, Colonial Loans, Board of Education, Indian Budget, and the Old Age Pensions Committee report closed legislative business. Convocation and clergy questions ran beside the Hague Peace Conference, which adjourned (29 July) having failed on armaments reduction but accepted arbitration machinery (Pauncefote) and humanitarian restraints (including Dum Dum bullet limits aimed at Britain). Parliament prorogued under gathering South African danger.

Chapter V — Dreyfus; Transvaal Crisis to War; Methuen; Black Week; Kaiser Visit; Khalifa; Colonies; Venezuela

Through late August into September the British public, the Register notes, fixed less on dwindling Transvaal peace odds than on the Rennes court-martial of Captain Dreyfus — newspapers filling columns with testimony, demeanour, and free comment, near-universally treating Dreyfus as victim of staff conspiracy or covering blunders. Church troubles continued: the archbishops’ Lambeth opinion against ceremonial incense and processional lights drew Harcourt’s triumphant Times reading as cutting the roots of the “Catholic revival,” Lord Hugh Cecil’s corrective that doctrine was not decided, Lord Halifax’s “stand by your priests” counsel (Dean Hole quitting the English Church Union in protest), Sanday’s Broad Church historical critique, and gradual episcopal pressure toward obedience amid Creighton’s Church Congress chairmanship.

Transvaal blue-books and colonial telegrams (Canadian Mills; Australian squadron offers; Jamaican militia volunteers) showed empire opinion hardening. Chamberlain at Highbury (26 August) said Kruger “dribbles out reforms like water from a squeezed sponge,” that Milner’s Bloemfontein terms were so moderate they seemed near weakness, that Britain could ask and take no less, and that “the sands are running down in the glass.” Boer conditional franchise offers, British qualified acceptance, Boer withdrawal of conditions, the 8 September British despatch, negative Boer reply, interim despatch of 23 September, and Balfour–Devonshire speeches filled September; joint-inquiry proposals and suzerainty dispute (1884 Convention; Faure testimony; Boer green book) darkened the record. Military preparations: Free State Volksraad warlike resolutions; field force of nearly 50,000 authorised; Army Reserve proclamation 7 October; Parliament summoned for 17 October. After Steyn–Milner telegrams, the Boer ultimatum precipitated war; autumn session majorities backed Ministers. Early public confidence met disappointments; Rosebery’s stimulating speeches (“see this thing through”), Mansion House ministerial appearances, and Bryce / Campbell-Bannerman / Asquith interventions marked party lines. Methuen’s Belmont, Graspan, and Modder River actions pushed toward Kimberley (flashlight link with Kekewich by 27 November); Natal saw Boer detachments forced back to the Tugela with Buller at Frere near Colenso — hopes of dual fires and Cape clearance under Gatacre and French.

The German Emperor’s visit (with Bülow), despite German press protest, brought long interviews with Balfour and Chamberlain and a £300 gift to Scots Greys widows; French press insults and the Delagoa Bay search incident angered Paris even as Delcassé (24 November) discouraged mediation fantasies. Chamberlain’s Leicester speeches mixed loftier war patriotism and colonial tribute with “mend their manners” and a Germany–United States–England “alliance”/understanding phrase that chilled German ministers, American journals, and McKinley’s 5 December Message affirming non-entangling alliances and South African neutrality. Samoa’s joint control ended (early November): Germany and the United States divided the archipelago; Britain took compensation especially in Tonga. Wingate’s Egyptian force killed the Khalifa and principal emirs (Osman Digna briefly escaping again) — Soudan military completion almost coinciding with South African disaster.

“Black Week”: Gatacre’s Stormberg night attack trap (10 December) — two guns, ~170 killed/wounded, 500+ prisoners; Methuen’s Highland Brigade surprise shattered at Magersfontein (~1,000 casualties; Wauchope killed; withdrawal to Modder); Buller’s Colenso Tugela failure (15 December) — ~1,100 casualties and eleven guns lost. National temper, Devonshire at York, colonial and home volunteering, and fresh military measures answered the reverses. Venezuelan arbitration in Paris (American and English judges; Martens presiding), within a week of the Boer ultimatum’s early October timing in the Register’s later summary, awarded Britain substantially the Schomburgk-line Guiana claim, leaving Venezuela Barima Point on the Orinoco delta; Anglo-American feeling remained broadly improved despite Chamberlain’s indiscretion and Transvaal misunderstandings (American ladies’ Maine hospital ship received by the Queen). Party autumn resolutions, lingering Church difficulties, and trade prosperity closed the English year.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Bye-elections suggested sober-but-advanced Liberalism progressing (Edinburgh and others), aided by land-values taxation propaganda and a large Glasgow conference (October; 112 local authorities). Scottish Liberal Association at Aberdeen (December) put land-values fiscal policy, with or after Lords abolition, at the programme’s front — yet imperial pride in Scots heroes Hunter and Macdonald (Omdurman) and Rosebery’s war watchword found sure echo. Campbell-Bannerman’s leadership pleased Scots Liberals; Asquith’s imperial war speeches often outran his cautious criticism with popular feeling. Dr. Clark’s “pro-Boer” Caithness stance drew Wick Town Council protest and Thurso effigy-burning. Glasgow Corporation’s telephone and tram successes (tram profit over £50,000 to 31 May) illustrated municipal energy. Clyde shipbuilding beat 1898’s record by nearly 25,000 tons; iron, steel, engines, Dundee textiles, coal conciliation, and whaling prospered. Free–United Presbyterian union prospects brightened.

Ireland. Two markers: the 1898 Local Government Act’s elected County and District Councils, and a drawing-together of jealous Nationalist sections. Landed proprietors who stood were largely rejected (few country gentlemen in Connaught and Munster); small farmers, country-town shopkeepers, and publicans dominated; only six north-eastern counties lacked great Nationalist majorities. Co-opted Grand Jury remnants and retained surveyors/clerks gave limited continuity; many councils showed public spirit, others parsimony, jobbery, and political/disloyal demonstration (Westmeath green-flag quarrel forcing Home Ruler Lord Greville’s resignation as chairman). As war approached, Limerick Corporation’s pro-Boer sympathy resolution was followed by Cashel, Tullamore, Monaghan, Mullingar, Nenagh, Kilrush, and Cork Corporation; Cork District Council (7 October) substituted language charging the English nation with “rapine, murder, pillage.” Session Irish measures (agriculture/technical instruction, tithe-rent) and Catholic university controversy (Balfour) remain the Westminster counterparts already traced above.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. The republic seemed at stake: army–nation tension, revision talk (de Marcère), Nationalist and Assumptionist clerical propaganda, Dupuy’s despised Cabinet, and Faure’s distrusted presidency. Street and press violence, Beaurepaire’s campaign against the Court of Cassation’s Criminal Chamber over Dreyfus revision, Algiers anti-Semitic tumult under Max Régis, and Nationalist pressure for transferring revision marked early months. Deschanel re-elected Chamber president; Loubet the Senate. The Rennes retrial and Dreyfus drama dominated French — and, as English History notes, British — attention; ministerial crises and the later Waldeck-Rousseau consolidation belong to the year’s French narrative of civil power versus army and League.

Italy. Coalition politics, Triple Alliance continuity, and Mediterranean interests receive the Register’s usual survey beside French turmoil.

Chapter II — Germany and Austria-Hungary

Germany. An army bill raised peace strength by 26,576 men between October 1899 and end-1902, holding that level to 1904 (quinquennate); the War Minister cited the Czar’s Eirenicon as lessening near-term Russian attack risk yet pointed to other Powers’ continued preparations, new corps organisation (Bavaria, Saxony, Hesse-Darmstadt), cavalry and howitzer needs, and two-years’ service problems. Naval/world-policy undercurrents and press feeling toward Britain remained sensitive even as official tones during the Kaiser’s autumn British visit were carefully managed; Samoa partition later removed a friction point.

Austria-Hungary. Dualist strain, Banffy–Opposition duel, tax-by-rescript episodes, and Balkan vigilance continue the Register’s Habsburg notice; anarchist-conference aftermath after the Empress’s 1898 murder echoed in British legislative hints.

Chapter III — Russia, Turkey, and Eastern Europe

Russia. Peace-congress sponsorship sat beside Far Eastern/Chinese pressure already stressed in English History; Muravieff’s Hague programme and great-Power diplomacy dominate the year’s Russian pages.

Turkey and smaller Eastern states. Cretan autonomy under Prince George (Queen’s Speech) closed a concert chapter; Macedonian and Armenian questions stayed secondary to Crete’s settlement and European finance in Turkey.

Chapter IV — Minor States of Europe

Belgium, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, and Norway receive routine political, military, and colonial notices — post-Spanish-war Iberian adjustment among them — without a single dominant crisis comparable to France or the Transvaal.

Chapter V — Asia

India. Plague continued to spread to fresh southern and northern places despite local diminution elsewhere; harvests and trade/revenue recovery were strong (Queen’s Speech; Elgin farewell; Curzon’s assumption of the viceroyalty, 6 January). Gold-standard moves and fiscal surpluses feature in later Indian Budget debate at Westminster.

China, Hong Kong, Korea, Japan, Siam. Scramble politics, railway spheres, and the Anglo-Russian railway self-denying arrangement north of the Wall / Yangtze basin structure the Far Eastern survey; open-door rhetoric versus sphere practice remains the British policy tension noted in Chapter II.

Chapter VI — Africa

Egypt and the Soudan. Uneventful Egyptian progress under better Khedive–British working and less French irritation; land reassessment assent; cotton prosperity despite a record-low Nile flood; 1900 Budget equilibrium despite land-tax and railway shortfalls; judicial experiments; Mixed Tribunals arrears. Upper Nile rights with France settled toward British river predominance and French “compact” territory west/south. Khalifa’s final defeat under Wingate removed the remaining military obstacle after Omdurman.

South Africa. The diplomatic and military narrative of Bloemfontein, blue-books, ultimatum, Natal and Cape invasions, sieges (Ladysmith, Kimberley, Mafeking), Methuen’s advances, and Black Week is told at length in English History Chapters IV–V and amplified here for colonial detail.

East, West, Central Africa. Niger Company imperial takeover; Anglo-French settlement aftermath; continuing partition administration.

Chapter VII — America

United States. Fifty-fifth Congress party numbers; Army reorganisation toward a large regular establishment; admiral-of-the-Navy office; failed Hawaii territorial bill; Miles “embalmed beef” inquiry censuring Miles and Eagan, Alger resigning for Root; Windsor Hotel fire; Spanish dead reinterred at Arlington; Philippine war after delayed treaty ratification (Senate 6 February; ratifications 11 April) as Aguinaldo fought on; McKinley neutrality Message on South Africa. Venezuela arbitration award (above) eased a former Anglo-American flashpoint; Maine hospital ship symbolised private American sympathy.

Canada, Newfoundland, Latin America, West Indies. Colonial loyalty offers in the Transvaal crisis; Newfoundland French-shore friction still listed among Anglo-French leftovers; routine Latin American and West Indian notices (hurricane relief already in the Queen’s Speech).

Chapter VIII — Australasia

Five colonies (NSW, Victoria, South Australia, Queensland, Tasmania) passed a federal Commonwealth Bill for imperial enactment; Western Australia delayed popular vote though mining opinion favoured federation. Premiers at Melbourne (2 February) settled double-dissolution-style joint sittings, Braddon Clause for ten years, and federal capital in NSW territory 100 miles from Sydney — congratulatory imperial telegrams following. South African war produced extraordinary loyalty: contingents from all colonies (Queensland first to tender), popular enthusiasm drowning small Labour opposition, initial imperial preference for unmounted men notwithstanding. Protest against British wine-duty increases and Pacific cable route/cost disputes were the chief fiscal frictions with London.

Chronicle of Events in 1899

Daybook from New Year: U.S. flag over Cuba (1 January); Cromer viscountcy and other honours; Samoan Malietoa–Mataafa fighting; Elgin farewell and Curzon’s viceroyalty; European gale damage; Barking boiler explosion; Omdurman reception and Gordon College foundation-stone; through plague and China telegrams, Hague sittings, Bloemfontein, Rennes, war mobilisation, Black Week, and colonial contingent sailings — a chronological companion to Parts I–II.

Retrospect of Literature, Science, and Art

Literary output held up versus 1898 though late-year Boer-war anxiety held back some important books; biography, letters, criticism, and art books were strong; reprints of classics continued. Poetry was barren of major new voices (Laureate, Meredith, Kipling, Watson silent in book form); Swinburne’s Rosamund; Stephen Phillips’s Paolo and Francesca; Yeats’s The Wind among the Reeds and Poems. Science and art sections follow the Register’s usual specialist year-notes.

Obituary of Eminent Persons Deceased in 1899

Standard Register necrology — opening with the Duke of Northumberland (d. 2 January) and Nubar Pasha among many British and foreign public figures — to be mined by name when building entity pages. Khalifa Abdullahi appears among those whose deaths close the year’s African military story.

State Papers

Transvaal. Extended documentary companion to Chapters IV–V: Milner–Chamberlain correspondence, franchise and suzerainty papers, green/blue-book material.

Venezuela. Arbitration award materials paralleling the Paris tribunal judgment summarised in English History Chapter V.
