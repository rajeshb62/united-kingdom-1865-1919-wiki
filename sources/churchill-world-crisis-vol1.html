The World Crisis, Vol. I (1911–1914)

Churchill’s account of the origins and outbreak of the Great War, the naval preparations and mobilisation, the Battle of the Marne, the war at sea, the Antwerp expedition, and the Turkish entry into the war.

Sources

Winston S. Churchill, The World Crisis, Part I 1911–1914. First published 1923–31. Electronic edition 2013, RosettaBooks, LLC, New York. ISBN 9780795331305.

This edition includes a Foreword to the New Edition, a Preface, a comprehensive appendix (five sections: A–E), and the full text of the 1923 first edition revised with additional material on Lord Fisher’s resignation and the Battle of the Marne.

Contents

  • Foreword to the New Edition — Churchill notes that this edition includes revisions incorporating new knowledge, particularly a more correct account of Lord Fisher’s resignation and a fuller narrative of the opening battles in France, including the Battle of the Marne — the first appearance of this new material in the complete work.

  • Preface — Churchill explains his method: the events described are those that passed before his own eyes or in which he played a part; the story of the Battle of the Marne is told by a soldier (unnamed but credited) to whom he listened.

Chapter I — The Vials of Wrath (1870–1904)

Churchill surveys the long diplomatic background: the Franco-German feud after 1870, Bismarck’s alliance system and his apprehension of a war of revenge, the young Kaiser’s dismissal of Bismarck and the Caprivi era, the Franco-Russian Alliance of 1892, the Anglo-German estrangement fueled by Germany’s sympathy for the Boers, the beginnings of the German Navy and “the birth of a challenge,” the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, the Russo-Japanese War, the Anglo-French Agreement of 1904, the formation of the Triple Entente, and the degeneration of Turkey and Austria. The chapter ends with “the long descent” and “the sinister hypothesis” of a general European war.

Chapter II — Milestones to Armageddon (1905–1910)

The domestic and diplomatic stage narrows to Britain. Churchill covers: Lord Salisbury’s retirement, Balfour and the end of an epoch, the fall of the Conservative government in the General Election of 1906, the Algeciras Conference, the Anglo-French military conversations, Asquith’s administration, the Austrian annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina, the German threat to Russia, the Admiralty programme of 1909 with its “We want eight and we won’t wait” naval scare, the growth of the German Navy, and the implications of German finance. Churchill includes personal recollections: a lunch with Sir William Harcourt in 1895 (“nothing ever happens”), and his first Cabinet appointment as President of the Board of Trade.

Chapter III — The Crisis of Agadir (1911)

The Agadir crisis in full detail: the Panther’s arrival at Agadir, the alarm bells of Europe, Sir Edward Grey’s warning, the period of silence, divisions within the Cabinet, Lloyd George’s Mansion House speech and its effect on German policy, British naval precautions, vulnerable points and the Naval Magazines, the military situation and Sir Henry Wilson’s forecasts, the meeting of the Committee of Imperial Defence on August 23, the divergences between the generals and admirals, Churchill’s own memorandum of August 13 (the “Twentieth Day” and “Fortieth Day” plans), his letter to Sir Edward Grey of August 30, and the ultimate invitation from the Prime Minister to go to the Admiralty.

Chapter IV — Admirals All

Churchill’s arrival at the Admiralty: the state of the Board of Admiralty, his first impressions of the naval leadership, the character and methods of Sir John Fisher (who had retired as First Sea Lord in 1910), the personality of Sir Arthur Wilson (the current First Sea Lord), the War Staff and its shortcomings, the relationship between the Admiralty and the Committee of Imperial Defence, and Churchill’s introduction to the great naval personalities who would command the fleets in the coming war.

Chapter V — The German Navy Law

The naval race in detail: the history of German naval legislation from the 1898 and 1900 Navy Laws through the Novelle of 1912, Tirpitz’s “risk theory,” the comparative strength of the British and German navies, the new Anglo-German negotiations of 1912 (the Haldane Mission), Churchill’s offer of a “naval holiday,” the German response, and the continued acceleration of German shipbuilding. Churchill includes an analysis of German naval finance and the political pressures driving the expansion.

Chapter VI — The Romance of Design

The technical evolution of the modern battleship: from the Dreadnought to the Queen Elizabeth class, the development of the all-big-gun ship, armour and gunnery design, the shift to oil fuel, the invention of the fire-control system, the increase in speed and gun calibre, and Churchill’s own role in pushing the 15-inch gun forward. The chapter includes detailed descriptions of the design debates between Fisher, Jellicoe, and the naval constructors, and the race to incorporate the lessons of Tsushima.

Chapter VII — The North Sea Front

The strategic geography of the North Sea: the German naval bases, the British dispositions at Scapa Flow, Cromarty, and Rosyth, the problem of blockade, the organisation of the Grand Fleet and the Harwich Force, destroyer and submarine flotillas, minefields and coastal defences, the Dover Patrol, Churchill’s inspection tours of the North Sea bases, and the evolving operational plans for the war at sea.

Chapter VIII — Ireland and the European Balance

The intersection of the Irish Home Rule crisis with the European diplomatic situation in 1913–1914: the Curragh incident, the Larne gun-running, the Buckingham Palace Conference, and the way the Irish crisis divided the Cabinet and the Army while Europe moved toward war. Churchill describes how the threat of civil war in Ireland distracted the Cabinet from the continental crisis and delayed decisions on the continental commitment.

Chapter IX — The Crisis (July 24–30, 1914)

The July Crisis day by day from Churchill’s perspective at the Admiralty: the Cabinet of Friday July 24 when the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia was read out (with the Irish boundary question still on the table), the seventeen points of the ultimatum, the naval position, the mission of Herr Ballin (a German shipping magnate attempting mediation), the decision on Sunday July 26 to hold the Fleet together after its practice mobilisation, the Cabinet divisions over British intervention, and the gathering sense of inevitability.

Chapter X — The Mobilization of the Navy

Churchill’s narrative of the naval mobilisation: the decision not to disperse the Fleet after the test mobilisation, the night of August 1–2 when Churchill waited for news from Germany, the German ultimatum to Belgium, the British ultimatum to Germany, the Admiralty war orders, the disposition of the Grand Fleet under Jellicoe, the Harwich Force under Tyrwhitt, and the submarine flotillas under Keyes. Churchill describes the mood at the Admiralty and the moment when the clock struck eleven on August 4 — the expiration of the British ultimatum.

Chapter XI — War: The Passage of the Army (August 4–22, 1914)

The transport of the British Expeditionary Force to France: the War Council of August 5, the debate over four divisions or six, changes in the Cabinet, Lord Kitchener’s appointment as Secretary of State for War, the organization of the British Armies, Kitchener’s vast recruiting task, the formation of the Royal Naval Division, the departure of the German and Austrian ambassadors, the Board of Admiralty in war, the responsibilities of the First Lord, the German method of war planning, relative naval strength, and the prospects of battle for command of the sea.

Chapter XII — The Battle in France

The opening of the land campaign: the British advance to Mons, the Battle of Mons, the retreat, the Battle of the Marne, the crisis at the Admiralty during the retreat, the “race to the sea,” the Battle of the Aisne, and the stabilisation of the trench line. This chapter draws on the soldier’s account that Churchill credits in the Preface, and includes new material in this edition.

Chapter XIII — On the Oceans

The war at sea beyond the North Sea: the German raiders, the escape of the Goeben and Breslau to Constantinople, the pursuit by Admiral Troubridge, the political consequences of the escape (the Turkish entry into the war), the threat to British trade routes, the hunt for von Spee’s squadron, the sinking of the Monmouth and Good Hope at Coronel, and the Admiralty’s response to the first defeats at sea.

Chapter XIV — In the Narrow Seas

The war in the English Channel and the southern North Sea: the sinking of the Amphion by a mine, the U-9’s destruction of the Aboukir, Cressy, and Hogue (the “live bait” squadron), the first British submarine successes, the Harwich Force’s operations, and the gradual evolution of the defensive and offensive minelaying campaigns in the narrow waters.

Chapter XV — Antwerp

Churchill’s personal account of the Antwerp expedition: the Battle of the Marne, the race for the sea, the identification of Antwerp as the true flank, the Admiralty’s concern about Antwerp, Churchill’s own mission to Antwerp (he took personal command of the defence), the Belgian government’s evacuation, the siege of Antwerp by the Germans, the Royal Naval Division’s part in the defence, the fall of Antwerp, and Churchill’s controversial decision to go to Antwerp in person.

Chapter XVI — The Channel Ports

The struggle for the Channel ports after the fall of Antwerp: the German drive for Calais, the defence of Dunkirk, the Belgian Army’s stand on the Yser, the First Battle of Ypres, the role of the Royal Naval Division and naval artillery in the land fighting, the formation of Churchill’s “Dunkirk Circus” (armoured cars and naval air squadrons operating ashore), and the final stabilization of the front from the sea to the Swiss border.

Chapter XVII — The Grand Fleet and the Submarine Alarm

The submarine threat in the autumn of 1914: reports of German submarines in the North Sea, the Grand Fleet’s temporary withdrawal from Scapa Flow to safer anchorages, the controversy over the fleet’s security, the loss of the Audacious to a mine, Churchill’s attempts to devise defensive measures against submarine attack, the development of decoy vessels, and the Admiralty’s evolving understanding of the U-boat as a strategic weapon.

Chapter XVIII — Coronel and the Falklands

The destruction of Admiral von Spee’s East Asiatic Squadron: the Battle of Coronel (November 1, 1914) where the British cruisers Good Hope and Monmouth were sunk off Chile, the Admiralty’s reaction, Churchill’s decision to despatch the battle-cruisers Invincible and Inflexible to the South Atlantic, the Battle of the Falkland Islands (December 8, 1914) where von Spee’s squadron was annihilated, and the aftermath for British naval strategy in the Atlantic and Pacific.

Chapter XIX — With Fisher at the Admiralty

Lord Fisher’s return as First Sea Lord (October 1914) and the Churchill-Fisher partnership at the Admiralty: the strengths and tensions of their collaboration, Fisher’s energy and creative strategic thinking, his advocacy of Baltic amphibious projects, the friction with other admirals, the growing divergence with Churchill over the Dardanelles, and the early signs of the rupture that would come in 1915 (covered in Vol. II).

Chapter XX — The Bombardment of Scarborough and Hartlepool

The German battle-cruiser raid on the east coast (December 16, 1914): the bombardment of Scarborough, Hartlepool, and Whitby, the civilian casualties and the public outcry, the British naval response, the near-interception of the German squadron by Beatty’s battle-cruisers, the breakdown of communications that allowed the Germans to escape, and the political fallout including charges of Admiralty negligence.

Chapter XXI — Turkey and the Balkans

The entry of the Ottoman Empire into the war: the Goeben and Breslau reaching Constantinople, the German influence on the Young Turk government, Enver Pasha’s decision for war, the Turkish attack on Russia’s Black Sea ports, the British declaration of war on Turkey, the opening of the Dardanelles campaign, the Russian appeal for a diversion, and the beginnings of the Gallipoli strategic concept.

Appendices

Appendix A — Memorandum by the First Lord on Naval Staff Training and Development

Churchill’s official memorandum on military education and war staff training for the Royal Navy. Covers the distinction between military education (general diffusion of knowledge) and war staff training (specialist preparation), the curriculum for young officers, the establishment of continuous courses at Greenwich, the training of staff officers for war planning, the organisation of the War Staff divisions (Intelligence, Mobilisation, Operations), and the need for a permanent Naval War College.

Appendix B — Tables of Fleet Strength

Comparative tables of British and German naval strength, including capital ships, battle-cruisers, cruisers, destroyers, and submarines, with progressive figures showing the construction race from 1905 to 1914.

Appendix C — Trade Protection

Churchill’s memorandum on the protection of British merchant shipping in wartime, including the organisation of convoys, the role of cruisers and destroyers in trade defence, the system of routes and reporting, and the allocation of vessels to the trade protection squadrons.

Appendix D — Mining

The state of British and German mining: mine stocks, minelaying vessels, mine types, and the strategic use of minefields in the North Sea, including the proposed Northern Barrage concept and the defensive mining of the Dover Strait.

Appendix E — First Lord’s Minutes

A collection of Churchill’s official minutes from his time at the Admiralty, covering naval policy, shipbuilding programmes, personnel questions, strategic appreciations, and administrative reforms. Includes his minutes on the naval holiday proposal, the 15-inch gun development, the Queen Elizabeth class design, and the organisation of the War Staff.
