The Annual Register 1908

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Before the Session

The fact that Parliament was to meet on 29 January deprived the New Year of much of its customary interest. Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, in an interview published by the Manchester Guardian, anticipated two or three years of effective work before another general election; yet it was generally felt that the coming session must be formidable, and that grave difficulties would attend Ministerial measures dealing with licensing, education, and finance. The conflict with the Upper House seemed to be slackening, and the economic outlook was becoming overcast. The high tide of prosperity had begun to ebb. Revenue returns for the October quarter showed a decrease in all departments except the Post Office, amounting altogether to 1,150,000l.; Board of Trade returns indicated that, though coal exports remained heavy, manufactured goods were decreasing; and financial stress in the United States and Germany was ominous of depression in Great Britain. In Lancashire a serious cotton dispute was impending, unemployment was becoming more acute, and the Mining Association of Great Britain, waiting on the Home Secretary on 8 January to deprecate the miners’ Eight Hours Bill, estimated that the measure would reduce output by nearly 34,500,000 tons annually.

In Church matters the outlook was more hopeful. The Bishop of Carlisle, in his New Year pastoral, favoured celebration in church of marriages under the Deceased Wife’s Sister Act and intimated that he would not debar the parties from Communion. The Primate recognised the significance of the coming Pan-Anglican Conference as enabling English Churchmen, with the help of observers from other lands, to view educational, social, and liturgical questions differently.

Political speeches in January were mainly echoes of 1907 controversies, though Opposition speakers brought into prominence the treatment of British Indians in the Transvaal. Women’s suffrage continued to diversify party politics: suffragists interrupted London police courts with protests against “man-made law,” and Mr. Haldane’s warning to feminine agitators at Glasgow on 8 January—that their vexatious methods had injured their cause in Parliament—proved ineffectual, though he stated that ultimately the suffrage could not be withheld from women. The Earl of Crewe, at Watford, promised an Education Bill that would satisfy moderate men, a Licensing Bill accelerating the reduction of public-houses, and a continuance of Mr. Birrell’s Irish policy.

Lord Cromer, defining the position of the small “Centre party” of Unionist Free Traders before the Unionist Free-Trade Club of Glasgow on 10 January, declared Free Trade a safeguard against huge trusts, a special benefit to the poorer classes, and the soundest basis for a healthy Imperialism. He strongly protested against an old-age pension scheme as developing demoralising dependence on the State, urged economy and reduction of taxation, and recommended reform of the House of Lords and opposition to hasty reforms. Lord Balfour of Burleigh pointed out that Tariff Reformers virtually admitted new taxes would raise prices by disclaiming any intention of taxing raw materials. A section of ardent Tariff Reformers known as the “Confederates”—described by Lord Robert Cecil in The Times of 23 January as “political moonlighters”—intended to run Tariff Reform candidates against all Free-Trade Unionists and were said to have twenty such candidates ready.

Mr. Asquith, speaking at Lancaster on 15 January, remarked that less satisfactory trade years were coming but that Free Trade would prove the sheet-anchor for British industry; he promised a short, simple, and drastic Education Bill and indicated that an old-age pension scheme would be begun. Sir Edward Grey at Alnwick the same evening noted that foreign nations were increasing their navies and that, although the British navy was adequate against any probable combination, if foreign programmes were carried out Britain’s own navy must be further increased. Mr. Balfour spoke in Glasgow on 17 January with an enthusiastic reception, declaring that the autumn campaign in Scotland against the Lords had failed and that the Government’s Scottish land Bills were not meant to pass but to promote agitation; he closed with an earnest appeal to Free-Food and Tariff-Reform wings to co-operate with the main Unionist body.

On the same day about a dozen women suffragists attempted to surround the Prime Minister as he entered Downing Street for a Cabinet Council; some chained and padlocked themselves to the area railings, and Mrs. Drummond, Miss New, and three others, refusing recognisances, were sent to prison for three weeks. Next morning the Ministerial position received a severe shock: in Mid-Devon, strongly Liberal since 1885, Captain Morrison Bell converted a Liberal majority of 1,289 in 1906 into a Unionist majority of 559. Tariff Reformers were specially energetic; one cogent argument was that food had risen in price under a Liberal Government; and the suffragists claimed the largest share in the Liberal defeat. Another Liberal disaster was the death from pneumonia on 17 January, after two days’ illness, of the Attorney-General, Sir J. Lawson Walton, K.C.

Mr. Winston Churchill, just returned from his African tour, made light of Mid-Devon at the National Liberal Club on 18 January as a storm in a teacup, predicted taxation of land values in 1909 and a measure dealing with the Lords’ veto the following year, and gave an account of East Africa and Uganda, acknowledging missionary enterprise and holding that Transvaal action on British Indians, however regrettable, was within colonial powers. Mr. Birrell at Reading on 21 January devoted most of his speech to Ireland and the problem of breaking up and distributing the grass lands. Sir Henry Fowler at Wolverhampton energetically denied rumours of Cabinet conflicts and denounced Socialism in so far as it meant nationalisation of the means of production. On the same day a manifesto from a hundred ministers of the Established and other Churches declared that their “Christian Socialism” involved public ownership of production, distribution, and exchange. Two days later the Labour Conference at Hull, after rejecting by ten to one a motion identifying the Labour party with Socialist aims, adopted another Socialist formula moved by the Amalgamated Society of Engineers, declaring socialisation of the means of production, distribution, and exchange and social and economic equality between the sexes, by 514,000 to 469,000 votes.

Chapter II — The Session Until Easter

Parliament was opened with the customary ceremonial by the King in person, accompanied by the Queen, on Wednesday, 29 January. Brilliant weather favoured an outdoor pageant more successful than on any previous occasion since the accession; militant women suffragists nearly succeeded in reaching the state coach with a petition, and a candidate to the dormant peerage of De Morley presented himself in the Lords but was removed before Their Majesties’ arrival.

The King’s Speech characterised the Anglo-Russian Convention as enabling the two Governments to maintain a peaceful policy notwithstanding the situation in Persia; touched on the joint treaty for preserving the integrity of Norway, the Hague Conference Final Act instruments, Macedonia, and the treatment of natives in the Congo State under the Berlin Act; mentioned negotiations for arbitration on the Newfoundland fishery dispute and the settlement of the Japanese immigration difference with Canada; and noted the threatened famine in India. A London maritime-law conference was contemplated for the autumn. The Estimates paragraph omitted the customary reference to “efficiency and economy” but promised proposals “for making better provision for old age,” together with Bills to amend licensing and education in England and Wales, regulate hours of labour in coal mines, deal with housing and town planning, amend valuation for rates and taxes, improve and extend Irish University Education, amend the Irish Land Purchase Act of 1903 (especially compulsory acquisition of untenanted land for relief of congestion), establish an authority for the Port of London, and consolidate the law relating to the protection of children and treatment of juvenile offenders. The Scottish Land and Valuation Bills of 1907 would also be submitted afresh.

The business of the Lords practically began with the admission of Lord Curzon of Kedleston, elected an Irish Representative Peer on 20 January. Though his name was not on the roll of Peers of Ireland entitled to vote, the Lord Chancellor, with the concurrence of the Earl of Halsbury, held that Lord Curzon was entitled to take his seat.

The Marquess of Lansdowne, commenting on the Speech, laid most stress on the absence of any reference to Ireland, charged the Government with preparing the destruction of the grazing industry, denounced refusal to resort to the Crimes Act, and condemned the assumption of the colossal liability of old-age pensions. After touching on licensing and education, he challenged the Government on its attitude toward the House of Lords and asked for proper opportunity for the Upper House to discharge its functions. The Lords’ debate on the Address, covering 30 January and 3 February, dealt wholly with Ireland: great stress was laid by the Marquess of Londonderry, Lord Clonbrock, the Earl of Donoughmore, Lord Ashbourne and others on cattle-driving and agrarian crime; on the Ministerial side Lord Castletown, Earl Beauchamp and Lord Denman contended that evils were greatly exaggerated, and the Earl of Dudley expressed belief that cattle-driving was due to national demands for a change in the system of government.

In the Commons the proceedings began with formal announcement of the committal of Mr. Ginnell for contempt of court. Mr. Balfour, after sympathetic references to the late Attorney-General and the Prime Minister’s absence through weak health, criticised the Anglo-Russian Agreement as affecting English trade in Persia and containing no textual reference to the Persian Gulf, denounced Mr. McKenna’s administration of the Education Department as cynically partisan, and laid special stress on disorder in Ireland.

Mr. Asquith, after heartily endorsing Mr. Balfour’s tribute to the late Attorney-General, replied that the Persian Gulf was not mentioned because it was largely Turkish, whereas England and Russia were the only two Powers with substantial interests in Persia; he deprecated discussion of British Indians in the Transvaal; defended Mr. McKenna; and declared that Mr. Balfour’s picture of Ireland was a caricature—the Government did not put the Crimes Act in force because they had protested against it for twenty years, and apart from cattle-driving there had been little crime. Mr. John Redmond regarded the Speech as disappointing in its omission of national self-government, but congratulated the Government on the Irish University Bill and the announcement that compulsory land purchase would not be confined to congested districts.

Mr. A. Henderson, newly elected chairman of the Labour party in succession to Mr. Keir Hardie, pressed for attention to unemployment. On 30 January Mr. Ramsay Macdonald moved an amendment regretting that no legislation had been recommended on that subject: unemployment, he said, now amounted to 6.1 per cent. of the population, yet this was after a boom in trade. Mr. John Burns, President of the Local Government Board, wound up the debate: some 50,000 to 70,000 less per million of unskilled labourers were unemployed than twenty years earlier; pauperism was decreasing in the country generally; and Free-Trade London workers were better off than those in Berlin. He intended to continue the Unemployed Workmen Act pending the Poor Law Commission Report. The Labour amendment was negatived by 195 to 146 after a closure of 315 to 80; twenty-four Liberals and twenty-four Unionists were found in the minority.

Chapter III — From Easter to Whitsuntide

The abridged Easter recess was a period of unusual interest, due to the change of Government and a miniature general election. Ministerial changes and elevations to peerages necessitated fresh elections in North-West Manchester, Dewsbury, West Wolverhampton, Dundee and Montrose; West Derbyshire and Central Sheffield fell vacant through the succession of Mr. Victor Cavendish to the Dukedom of Devonshire and the deaths of Sir Howard Vincent (7 April) and Mr. J. W. Crombie (22 March). Of the contested seats, only North-West Manchester and West Wolverhampton presented great electoral risk.

Mr. Churchill’s success in a normally Unionist constituency had been one of the earlier surprises of 1906; his spirited address of 13 April, as President of the Board of Trade, appealed to Manchester’s special interests and Free Trade, defended the Licensing Bill, and condemned the practice of opposing the re-election of Ministers. He was strongly opposed by women suffragists—who had already, in an “open letter” to the women of Great Britain, made Mr. Asquith a principal target—and by a Socialist, Mr. Dan Irving; the Unionist candidate was again Mr. Joynson Hicks. Free-Trade Unionists and Irish electors were recognised as swing factors.

Mr. John Redmond, addressing the United Irish League at Dublin on 15 April, deprecated disappointment at the Home Rule debate: the Liberal party had supported Home Rule by an enormous majority including Mr. Asquith, Mr. Birrell and Sir Edward Grey, but whereas the late Premier had been an enthusiastic Home Ruler, Mr. Asquith, though intellectually a Home Ruler, was not enthusiastic, and would probably try to remain in office for some years by a conciliatory course rather than a direct challenge to the Lords. Redmond did not at first think the Irish electors at Manchester could be asked to support Mr. Churchill—though after Churchill’s Easter Monday profession of Home Rule faith and his statement, with the Prime Minister’s concurrence, that after the close of the existing Parliament the Liberal party should claim a free hand to deal with Irish self-government, the Irish leaders in Manchester advised electors to support him.

On 24 April Mr. Joynson Hicks was returned with 5,517 votes against 4,988 for Mr. Churchill and only 276 for Mr. Irving. The result was claimed as a victory for Tariff Reform, though Joynson Hicks ascribed it also to the Education, Licensing and Miners’ Eight Hours Bills and Home Rule; Churchill described it as a heavy blow to Liberalism and Free Trade and was at once invited to stand for Dundee. At Dewsbury on 23 April Mr. Runciman held the seat despite diversion of 2,446 votes to a Labour candidate; in Kincardineshire the Liberal victory bitterly disappointed the Tariff Reformers. Churchill also promised that old-age pensions would be in existence before the year was out.

Parliament reassembled after Easter on 27 April only to adjourn till the next day as a mark of respect to the late Prime Minister. Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman’s death, expected for some time, came somewhat suddenly on 22 April and drew forth from all parties at home, as well as from Continental statesmen and leading newspapers, displays of respect and affection in striking contrast to the savage attacks of some Unionists a few years before. It was generally admitted that he had not reached the first rank of statesmen, but he had been an excellent administrator, had reconciled conflicting Liberal sections, conciliated the Irish, and linked Free Trade with Social Reform. The King’s personal regard was exhibited in the farewell visit paid on 5 March before His Majesty left for Biarritz.

The funeral service in Westminster Abbey on 27 April was attended by the Prince of Wales—the King and Queen being absent at Copenhagen—by the French Prime Minister M. Clemenceau, who came specially from Paris, and by representatives of State departments, the Diplomatic Corps, the Universities and leading Liberal organisations. By his own wish he was buried at Meigle, Perthshire, beside his wife. Impressive tributes were paid in the Commons by Mr. Asquith, Mr. Balfour, Mr. T. P. O’Connor (in the absence of Mr. Redmond), and by spokesmen of the Labour members. Mr. Asquith laid stress on the acuteness of his chief’s sympathies, his reliance on moral force, his passionate love of peace, and his calm, persistent courage, describing him in Sir Henry Wotton’s words as an example of the happy man “Whose armour is his honest thought, And simple truth his utmost skill.” Similar tributes were paid in the House of Lords on its reassembly.

The debate on the second reading of the Licensing Bill began on 28 April in a crowded House. The Primate had that day endorsed the measure at the Church of England Temperance Society, declaring its principles right and just and the time-limit right in principle. Mr. Asquith, loudly cheered on rising as Prime Minister, restated the fundamental aims of the Government as improvement of the liquor traffic and recovery, with due but not undue regard for existing interests, of State control of the monopoly.

Under Mr. Balfour’s Act of 1904 reduction would become slower, uneven and very costly; this Bill would make it cheaper for the trade and more effective. A licence was granted for a year only; the holder had defeasible expectations of renewal, too fragile to be property, but deserving attention in equitable form. The reckless competition between brewery companies for tied houses, “fratricidal in intention,” had been “suicidal in effect.” Controversies over the time-limit, local veto, clubs, and the employment of barmaids ran through the debate; opponents charged “Socialist confiscation.” The Opposition amendment, moved by Mr. Cave (Kingston) and seconded by Mr. Evelyn Cecil, called on the House to refuse a measure failing to promote temperance and violating equity. Mr. Leif Jones, President of the United Kingdom Alliance, congratulated the Government on their courage; Mr. Barnard, a Liberal, thought consideration to trade interests insufficient; Sir J. Dickson-Poynder urged considerable extension of the time-limit; Herbert Samuel defended the public-house provisions.

Chapter IV — From Whitsuntide to the Summer Recess

The chief event of the Whitsuntide recess was the much-canvassed State visit of King Edward VII. to the Tsar at Reval—the first visit ever paid to Russia by any British Sovereign. The King and Queen, with Princess Victoria, left on the evening of Friday, 5 June, and arrived at Reval on board the Victoria and Albert, escorted by warships, on Tuesday morning, 9 June. Speeches at the State banquet laid stress on the effect of the Anglo-Russian Agreement of 1907 in drawing the two countries together and consolidating the peace of the world; Sir Charles Hardinge and M. Isvolsky, accompanying their respective Sovereigns, came near to complete agreement on projected Macedonian reforms. The Russian progressive Press regarded the meeting as encouraging hopes of domestic reform in Russia as well as peace in Europe; in Germany and Vienna there was some apprehension as to the effects of an Anglo-Russian understanding on German diplomatic interests. An unfortunate after-effect of the Commons debate on the visit was the omission of Mr. Keir Hardie, Mr. Victor Grayson and Mr. Ponsonby from invitations to the King’s garden party on 20 June; the Labour party formally resented the omission of Mr. Keir Hardie as “an attempt by the Court to influence members of Parliament.”

At home the United Irish League Convention at Leeds (8 June) emphasised the breach between English Catholics and Irish Nationalists and advised acceptance of the Irish University Bill. Friendly Societies expressed dissatisfaction with the Old Age Pensions Bill as giving no encouragement to thrift; other labour organisations treated it as only an instalment. Lord Morley of Blackburn, at a dinner of the Indian Civil Service Dinner Club on 11 June, set forth the Ministerial attitude towards unrest in India: the first duty was to keep order, but pure repression was impossible; he defended the Press Act and Explosives Act against Radical criticisms and predicted that “we should get through the dark and ugly moment before us” only with self-command and without quackery or cant.

The House of Commons reassembled on 10 June. On the Board of Agriculture vote Sir Edward Strachey stated that no necessity had yet arisen to acquire land compulsorily under the Small Holdings Act; some 16,000 people had applied and 250,000 acres were required. The Prevention of Crime Bill, introduced by the Home Secretary on 27 May, established Borstal-system reformatories for offenders between sixteen and twenty-one and empowered courts to sentence habitual criminals (a man convicted of four offences) to detention until sufficient assurance of honest life; on second reading (12 June) the Bill passed by 133 to 11, Mr. Akers-Douglas approving the principle while Mr. Atherley-Jones and Mr. Belloc protested against indeterminate sentences.

At a National Liberal Club banquet on 12 June Mr. Asquith reviewed the outlook: the party and Ministry were thoroughly united, but future work needed greater concentration on fewer subjects; uncompleted work should be resumable in the following session; they were not pressing the Education Bill because forces were working for a compromise consistent with Liberal principles; on Licensing there was a reaction in favour of the Ministry; and he defended Old Age Pensions against both advocates of a contributory scheme and Liberal and Labour critics, noting that exclusion of persons in receipt of poor relief was only for the current year pending the Poor Law Commission Report. The united women’s suffrage procession next day marched from the Embankment to the Albert Hall, where Mrs. Fawcett presided; an independent Lancashire working women’s demonstration was held the same day in Trafalgar Square against the displacement of barmaids under the Licensing Bill.

The Old Age Pensions Bill second reading was debated on 16 and 17 June. The Bill as printed (2 June) differed from the Budget forecast in imposing a qualified character test, disqualifying recipients of poor relief (other than medical relief) since 1 January 1907, and extending the married-persons limitation to other relatives living in the same house.

Mr. Lloyd-George, moving the second reading, said so-called contributory schemes had been given up by most reformers including Mr. Chamberlain; they would exclude women, and the failure of friendly societies’ superannuation schemes showed the contributory plan impracticable. The number pensionable was estimated at 572,000 (out of 1,262,632 persons over seventy) and the annual cost at 7,500,000l., with 6,000,000l. for the first year. It was the most liberal scheme yet proposed in any old country. Mr. Harold Cox moved an amendment condemning expenditure of taxpayers’ money in subsidising persons selected by arbitrary standards of age, income and character, and commended the German contributory scheme. Lord Robert Cecil saw Socialistic elements and cited United States pension lists as a warning; Mr. O’Grady for the Labour party advocated universal pensions as a right and declared the Bill hopelessly inadequate. Mr. Haldane declared that the Bill embodied the principle of the British poor law since Elizabeth—that the prosperous must relieve the necessitous—and was part of a much larger class of reforms after the Poor Law review.

Chapter V — The Summer Recess and the Autumn Session

Echoes of the session were heard in Bank Holiday speeches by Mr. Long at Huntingdon and Mr. Wyndham in the Duke of Westminster’s park at Eaton near Chester, dealing with the Licensing Bill, Tariff Reform, and the need of increased naval strength. The naval alarmists were partly answered by the remarkable voyage of H.M.S. Indomitable, bringing the Prince of Wales from Quebec at an average speed of twenty-six knots; The Times pointed out that this new class of cruiser-battleship possessed more armament than an armoured cruiser and more speed than a battleship, and was likely to be of the highest value both for reconnaissance and for actual warfare.

A more comprehensive answer to Tariff Reformers was offered by the International Free-Trade Congress, the first of its kind, organised by the Cobden Club and meeting in London from 4 to 7 August with some 500 delegates from various countries, among them Professor Sumner of Yale, Mr. Franklin Pierce and Mr. Joseph Fels from the United States, MM. Yves Guyot, Paul Leroy-Beaulieu and Emile Ollivier from France, Signor E. E. Moneta from Italy, and Professor Brentano, Herr Georg Gothein and Dr. Theodor Barth from Germany.

Mr. Winston Churchill, President of the Board of Trade, welcoming the delegates, laid stress on Free Trade’s advantages for Britain and insisted that international peace was preserved mainly by the increase of international commerce. The Prime Minister, at a dinner at the Hotel Cecil the same evening, ridiculed the delusions that English Free Traders were slaves of an economic formula or of the Cobden tradition alone; Free Trade had been adopted as an economic necessity to give necessary food and raw materials; in fifty years imports of raw wool had nearly quadrupled and raw cotton had just doubled. Britain was entering a period of comparative depression and might have to suspend debt reduction and impose fresh taxation, but Free-Trade finance would stand the strain of social reform; none of the yield of taxation would be diverted to privileged interests. Effective speeches were also made by M. Yves Guyot, Herr Barth, and Mr. Martin, K.C., a Canadian delegate. At the close a committee was appointed to arrange another congress for 1910 at The Hague, Brussels or Antwerp.

Of all congresses held in this year of international assemblages in London, the first in interest was the Pan-Anglican, with its sequel the Episcopal Conference, which closed on 6 August with a solemn service at St. Paul’s at which some 200 Bishops walked in procession through a thronged nave and heard a sermon by Bishop Tuttle of Missouri, the presiding Bishop of the Protestant Episcopal Church of the United States. The seventy-eight resolutions, published on 7 August with an Encyclical Letter signed by the Archbishop of Canterbury on behalf of 243 Archbishops and Bishops, reaffirmed the historic facts of the Creeds, insisted on more and better trained candidates for Orders and on religious education, condemned separate organisation of Churches on a basis of race or colour, recommended a native episcopate, laid down lines for Prayer-book revision, and contemplated intercommunion in various degrees with Eastern, Old Catholic, Swedish and Moravian bodies. Of the thankoffering of about 345,000l., some 125,000l. was marked for specific purposes by donors; the rest would mainly be spent on educational needs in the Colonies, China, India and Japan.

Other assemblies of the season included an International Constitution Congress (7–8 August), an International Art Congress, the History of Religions Congress at Oxford (15–18 September), and the Moral Education Congress at South Kensington (25–30 September), at which Protestant, Roman Catholic and Agnostic educational experts discussed ethical training with or without religious instruction.

The week following the Free-Trade Congress saw King Edward’s annual visit to the German and Austrian Emperors: a day at Cronberg with Wilhelm II. (11 August) and the next day at Ischl with Francis Joseph, at which Sir Charles Hardinge interviewed Baron von Aehrenthal. Both Governments agreed in adopting an attitude of benevolent expectancy towards the Turkish revolution.

Mr. Churchill, at a miners’ demonstration at Swansea on 15 August—receiving a specially hearty welcome on the announcement that morning of his impending marriage—utterly repudiated the notion that an Anglo-German war was inevitable; there was nowhere any collision of German and British interests; the war party in Germany did not number 10,000, and in England it existed only in lunatic asylums and on the staff of the National Review. Mr. Lloyd-George, in Germany to study workmen’s insurance, had allowed himself to be interviewed by the Neue Freie Presse of Vienna advocating an Anglo-German understanding; the Standard treated his and Churchill’s utterances as undermining Sir Edward Grey—a misrepresentation vigorously denounced by Churchill in the Daily Chronicle.

A Parliamentary paper of 28 August showed that in battleships less than twenty-five years old, armoured cruisers less than twenty years old, and destroyers, Great Britain had more than any two of the other leading naval Powers together; expenditure and new-construction returns, however, were less reassuring. Training of the territorial forces in August camps gave encouraging results. At a Primrose League demonstration at Stockton-on-Tees on 29 August the Marquess of Londonderry denounced the Education and Licensing Bills as robbery and the latter as Socialism; Lord Hugh Cecil and Sir Alexander Acland-Hood also attacked the Licensing measure. Opposition to the Licensing Bill caused Mr. Cameron Corbett (Tradeston, Glasgow), a Free-Trade Unionist, to secede from his party connection.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. In Scotland more than in the other divisions of the United Kingdom the dominant interest of the year was economic. The bye-elections of April and May showed that the electorate, unlike that of England, had not abated the support of the Ministry marked at the general election of 1906. The Scottish Liberal Federation in October passed resolutions in favour of Disestablishment, Women’s Suffrage, and Home Rule for purely Scottish affairs, and deplored excessive outlay on armaments. The second rejection by the Lords of the Small Landholders Bill in March and the alteration and abandonment of the Land Values Bill were not followed by any visible wave of popular indignation. Unemployment was serious, especially in Glasgow; disturbances in September and a demonstration during the visit of Prince Arthur of Connaught indicated Socialist activity. The great legislative feature of the year was an Education Act containing several provisions much in advance of English law; another notable Act consolidated Scottish agricultural law.

One land episode attracted much attention. The Vatersay crofters, whose case had been going on since the spring of 1906, were cited by Mr. Balfour at Glasgow on 17 January as proof that the Crofters Act had utterly failed to check congestion. Lady Gordon Cathcart, landlord of Barra and other Hebridean islands, had multiplied small holdings without permanently relieving congestion; in June 1906 inhabitants, impatient at non-fulfilment of hopes they believed held out by the Congested Districts Board agent, invaded Vatersay (leased with Sandray to one tenant), put cattle on it and built huts. The Scottish Secretary treated settlement as a matter for the landlord and refused to put the Trespass Act in force. Eventually in June ten were sentenced to two months’ imprisonment for breach of interdict; the case was debated in the Lords (31 March) and Commons (25 June); early in July a compromise arranged small holdings, and at Lady Gordon Cathcart’s request the men were released, she paying their fares home. In August the Scottish Secretary had to warn applicants who threatened to seize land, and the situation was further complicated by seizure of Sandray by rival squatters from Mingulay.

The Commission under the Churches (Scotland) Act published in February a comprehensive statement of the allocation of Church funds between the United Free Church and the Free Church, but its labours were still unfinished. In March the provision of 3,000l. a year for a Free Church College was capitalised by allotment of 92,308l.—a heavy deduction from the educational resources of the larger body. The Church of Scotland General Assembly found itself unable to revise the Confession of Faith but decided to confer with other Presbyterian Churches with a view to closer union; the United Free Church responded almost unanimously. A Treasury Committee to inquire into the revenue and financial position of the Scottish Universities was appointed on 31 July. Lord Rector elections at Edinburgh and Glasgow (24 October) were notable Unionist victories for Lord Curzon of Kedleston and Mr. George Wyndham over Mr. Churchill and Mr. Lloyd-George; Mr. Keir Hardie received 122 votes at Glasgow. A Scottish National Exhibition of high merit was opened at Edinburgh by Prince Arthur of Connaught in April.

A housing return at the end of March showed steady decrease in overcrowding since 1861: the percentage living more than two in a room had fallen from 56.57 to 45.68; more than three from 33.83 to 22.91; more than four from 18.67 to 9.56. Vital statistics for 1907 gave an estimated population of 4,776,063. Throughout the year there was grave industrial depression. On the Clyde shipbuilding, which had reached maximum output in 1907, fell to the level of 1894: the Scottish total was 680 vessels of 400,194 tons with engines of 528,702 i.h.p., against 757 vessels of 675,173 tons and 742,299 i.h.p. the previous year. Four Clyde vessels of over 10,000 tons each were built, including the P. & O. liner Malwa and three large Orient liners; the Otaki was the first example of that combination of triple and turbine engines later rendered familiar by the White Star Laurentic. Cotton spinning and weaving, jute, linen, steel and iron, and building all suffered; the harvest season was prolonged, expensive and disappointing.

Ireland. Though legislation for Ireland was not the dominant feature of the lengthy session, the year was marked by measures of high importance. The Irish Universities Act at last disposed of a problem fertile in difficulties to English Ministries for forty years by a settlement satisfactory to Irish Roman Catholics and not imposing undue strain on British Liberal opinion. The two new Universities in Dublin and Belfast were named the National and the Queen’s University; organisation of both was actively in progress by year’s end, though election of the Roman Catholic Archbishop of Dublin as Chancellor of the National University suggested that its denominational character was likely to be more marked than English and Scottish supporters of the Bill had expected. An agitation promoted by the Gaelic League and endorsed by Nationalist leaders pressed for inclusion of Irish as a compulsory matriculation subject. Other important measures were a Housing Act for urban working classes, an Evicted Tenants Act under which compulsory powers were obtained for acquisition of 1,800 acres of the Clanricarde Estate in County Galway, and a Tuberculosis Prevention Act. Two travelling exhibitions against tuberculosis visited sixty towns and were attended by some 700,000 people; the Lord-Lieutenant and the Countess of Aberdeen actively aided the movement. The Old Age Pensions Act was of great benefit to Ireland, though figures early in the new year suggested that many beneficiaries had in fact no claim to pensions.

The success of the Home Rule resolution encouraged Nationalist hopes, strengthened by Mr. Churchill’s avowal at Manchester; in November preparations were made to renew the agitation in Great Britain. Unity of the Nationalist party was restored in January when Mr. William O’Brien and Mr. Healy returned to the main body. In the autumn Mr. Redmond, Mr. Devlin and Mr. J. Fitzgibbon visited the United States to collect funds and addressed a Convention at Boston. The Nationalists, however, were threatened by Sinn Fein: a Sinn Fein candidate was defeated in North Leitrim by nearly three to one on 22 February, but his poll of 1,100 was unexpectedly large; the Sinn Fein Executive report of 26 August stated that there were 115 branches in Ireland.

Unhappily the state of portions of the southern and western counties—eight in all—steadily grew worse through the campaign against the grazing industry. An official return showed that cattle-drives rose from 390 in the nine months ending 31 December 1907 to 517 in the nine months to 30 September 1908. At year’s end the Irish Times recorded nearly 700 cattle-drives, nearly 600 agrarian outrages, nearly 100 cases of firing into dwelling-houses; twenty counties were involved; eight had been officially proclaimed and were maintaining 750 extra police at a cost of nearly 50,000l. The Ministry repeatedly refused the Crimes Act of 1887 as an ineffective and improper weapon; late in the year Mr. J. P. Farrell was sent to prison by the Court of King’s Bench under an Act of Edward III. The Government contended that the evil could only be cured by facilitating land purchase; the Bill for that purpose, introduced in the autumn session, was necessarily postponed till 1909.

The report of the Earl of Dudley’s Commission on Congestion, issued 24 May, made drastic recommendations: the Congested Districts Board area should include Connaught, Donegal, Kerry, Clare and four rural districts in West Cork, with exclusive control over land purchase there; enlargement of small holdings would involve migration and acquisition of untenanted land and parts of large holdings to a value of 450,000l. annually; compulsory powers of acquisition were demanded; and the Board should be reconstructed with nine representatives elected by County Councils. Nothing was done to give effect to the report by legislation. The mystery of the loss of the Regalia of the Order of St. Patrick, including the Irish Crown Jewels, early in July 1907, remained unsolved.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1908

By Cuthbert Maughan

In 1908 trade followed the course forecasted by those who had studied its movements in recent years. After nearly three years of steady expansion a decline apparent in the second half of 1907 continued unchecked throughout the year just closed. The reaction was marked in practically all industrial countries and was disclosed at home by Clearing House returns, traffic earnings, insurance premiums and foreign-trade statistics. In the United States financial conditions steadily righted themselves from the chaos of the previous autumn, and excellent crops in both Americas assisted recovery, though trade remained dull even after Mr. Taft’s election to the Presidency in November. In Germany almost all forms of industry were marked by depression without great disasters. Australia and New Zealand were affected by lower values for their products; South Africa showed progress and ended the year cheerfully; China’s import trade was crippled by currency troubles.

Unfortunately the year’s history was marred by two great labour disputes. The strike in the North of England engineering yards affected all works dependent on shipbuilding and engineering on the north-east coast and lasted seven months before the men yielded. The lock-out in the cotton trade extended for seven weeks and was not unwelcome to spinners who found the stoppage useful for reducing over-supplied stocks of cotton goods; the masters’ point—that as wages were advanced in good years so they should be reduced in bad—was maintained, and the operatives postponed until March the 5 per cent. reduction the masters had insisted should take effect in September.

Direct results were Sir Christopher Furness’s profit-sharing scheme, accepted by workmen in his shipbuilding yards, and the Board of Trade’s formation of a Court of Arbitration for labour disputes. Another important feature was the tendency toward combination among great railways: a far-reaching agreement between the Great Central, Great Northern and Great Eastern was followed by announcement that the London and North-Western and Midland proposed Parliamentary powers for a working agreement. In South Wales the Cambrian Colliery Company acquired a controlling interest enabling it before long to control an output of over 5,000,000 tons. Production of gold in the Transvaal made great progress; the total received from South Africa was 31,814,000l., though the bulk did not stay in this country.

Returns of foreign trade compared with an exceptionally good year and were affected by lower prices, but the total value still exceeded the 1,000,000,000l. first dealt with in 1906. Compared with 1907, imports declined by 52,667,219l. or 8.2 per cent. to 593,140,723l.; exports of United Kingdom produce and manufactures declined by 48,815,504l. or 11.5 per cent. to 377,219,579l.; re-exports declined by 12,276,515l. or 13.4 per cent. to 79,665,569l. Of the import reduction, 72 per cent. was in raw materials; of the export reduction, over 92 per cent. was in manufactured articles, more than half of that in textiles. During the year all manufacturers received Board of Trade schedules under the Census of Production Act of 1906.

January opened with an abnormally high Bank rate: the 7 per cent. imposed in November as a consequence of the American crisis was reduced to 6 per cent. on 2 January and by month’s end to 4 per cent.; further reductions brought it to 3 per cent. on 5 March, then again mid-March, and finally 2½ per cent. at the end of May. The Bank of France received enormous supplies of gold through this country, building its reserve to a record figure—metal which France had lent to Britain and the United States in past years and whose return had been interrupted by the exceptional events of autumn 1907.

On the Stock Exchange, Consols were depressed by the difficulty of providing means for old-age pensions, the probability of a large issue of Irish Land Stock, falling revenue, and competition of other gilt-edged securities. Home railways were generally out of favour with traffics against them, though Central London Deferred rose 7 and Metropolitan District 4 on Franco-British Exhibition traffic and underground facilities. Until the Licensing Bill was rejected nearly all Brewery stocks were greatly depressed. American railroad securities advanced strongly despite bad trade—Union Pacifics by 64, Chicago Milwaukee by 44, New York Central by 32. British insurance companies settled Kingston, Jamaica earthquake claims of 1907 at 85 per cent. after Privy Council judgment, about 1,000,000l. with law costs. Marine underwriters and shipowners experienced a most disastrous year. Commodity prices continued to fall from the high levels of early 1907; Scotch pig-iron warrants opened at 57s. 6d. and closed at 55s. 3d.; English wheat was lower by over 2s. per quarter and Canadian by 4s. At the end of the year depression was general, but some observers expected early signs of revival.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. Though 1908 was much less troubled than its predecessors by religious or social disturbances or attempts at civil war, it was marked by a general strain in the structure of parties. The Radical-Socialist party, having come into power, kept a firm hold and continued, by reciprocal concessions, to rally former antagonists; the extreme wings of the malcontents tended to break up. The Catholics became more clearly distinguished from the Monarchists; the Socialists began to understand where alliance with trade unionism becomes disastrous.

The sudden death of the Minister of Justice, M. Guyot-Dessaigne (31 December 1907), necessitated reconstruction: on 6 January M. Briand was appointed Minister of Justice and of Public Worship (the latter post separated from Public Instruction), and M. Doumergue gave Commerce to M. Cruppi and took Public Instruction and Fine Arts. General d’Amade was appointed to command the expeditionary corps at Casablanca in place of General Drude. The Chambers met on 14 January. News that d’Amade had advanced as far as Settat brought notice of interpellation from M. Jaurès on Moroccan policy; three lengthy sittings (24, 27, 28 January) were devoted to criticism and defence, a profound impression being produced by M. Delcassé’s great speech of 24 January—breaking the long silence he had imposed since his resignation in 1905—advising France to strengthen her friendships and alliance and maintain an Army adequate to her engagements. The debate closed with an order of the day, 436 to 51, repeating assurances regarding the Act of Algeciras.

The Chamber resumed the Income Tax Bill (20 January), interrupted in July 1907. M. Caillaux’s plans had gained nothing by lapse of time; opposition to inquiry into income increased as details were revealed. M. Ribot’s masterly statement of the case against the Bill took up two sittings; examination clause by clause began on 25 February. The Senate attacked the Minister of Marine over irregularities in orders for boilers for Danton-type battleships; the Senate Committee on old-age pensions, under M. Cuvinot, showed that the burden on the State would be more than twice the amount indicated by the Government. The strength of the Cabinet lay mainly in its chief; M. Clemenceau was repeatedly compelled to intervene personally to save a colleague. At the inauguration of the monument to M. Scheurer-Kestner in the Luxembourg garden (11 February) he delivered a magnificent eulogy of Alsace.

General d’Amade received a reinforcement of 4,000 men for organisation of the Shawia territory (2 March); General Lyautey was summoned to Paris regarding the western frontier of Algeria and suppression of disturbances by emissaries of Mulai Hafid. An Amnesty Bill for those convicted in connection with the Southern disturbances of the preceding year was introduced on 16 March, but Clemenceau absolutely refused to extend it to anti-militarists recently condemned by the Assize Court. On 6 April, after Jaurès’s interpellation on general policy, a resolution moved by M. Dubief expressed confidence that the Government would effect without delay the purchase of the Western Railway, establish old-age pensions and a progressive income tax—three only of the seventeen items of Clemenceau’s November 1906 programme. Municipal elections (3 and 10 May) were favourable to the Radical party and specially unfavourable to the Socialists, who lost several seats in Paris and were eliminated from the Councils of Toulouse, Brest and St. Etienne.

M. Caillaux introduced his Budget on 19 May with expenditure of 3,973,000,000 francs (158,920,000l.), to be considerably exceeded; he was constrained to resort to what was virtually a loan. The Bill for purchase of the Western Railway system by the State, begun 26 May, was wound up in the Senate by a remarkable speech from M. Rouvier against its financial consequences; Clemenceau again made the question one of confidence, and the Bill as a whole was accepted on 26 June. On 3 June a strike at the sandpits at Vigneux and Draveil near Corbeil underwent tragic development when gendarmes and strikers exchanged shots. Next day at the Pantheon, during the ceremony of transferring Zola’s remains, a Nationalist journalist, M. Gregori, fired at Major Dreyfus. On 6 June the General Confederation of Labour provoked a sanguinary riot at the St. Paul Riding School; Clemenceau, supported by 429 to 63, declared that responsibility for the disorders lay with the Socialist deputies. The Chamber passed a Secret Voting Bill (with amendments) and a Press-law amendment accelerating procedure for Press offences; a Bill suppressing the death penalty met adverse public feeling stirred by a fresh outbreak of offences against the person. The ordinary session closed by decree on 13 July amid general unsettlement.

At the end of July the building trades’ federation ordered affiliated societies to cease work; the General Confederation of Labour organised a demonstration at Villeneuve St. Georges which became a sanguinary riot (30 July)—three strikers killed and twenty wounded, disabled soldiers thrice that number. Three leading Confederation agitators were sent to prison at Corbeil; the general strike was limited to a stoppage in a few printing offices (2 August) and a two-hour interruption of electric light and power on 6 August. When President Fallières returned to Paris on 3 August after his Northern tour as guest of the Kings of Denmark and Norway, the Tsar and the King of Sweden, order was almost entirely restored, and M. Clemenceau took his usual holiday cure at Carlsbad.
