The Annual Register 1911

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Before the Session

A lull in party politics had naturally succeeded to the excitement of the general election; and, when the year opened, it seemed likely to be broken only by the bye-election in the Horncastle division of Lincolnshire, necessitated by the death of the Earl of Ancaster and the succession of his son, the Unionist member for the division, to the Upper House, and by the efforts of the Unionist party to adjust themselves to the new conditions set up by Mr. Balfour’s acceptance of the Referendum as part of the regular mechanism of the government of the United Kingdom. The acceptance had been sudden, and the process of adjustment revealed unexpected difficulties and some dissension. Sir William Anson, one of the two great living authorities on the British Constitution and the Unionist member for the University of Oxford, argued in The Times (3 and 4 January) that the check on the omnipotence of Parliament, due to the custom that doubtful Bills should be referred by the House of Lords to the electorate, was now endangered by the policy of the Government, and that another check was therefore needed; that there were four groups in the House of Commons, two of which were irresponsible, inasmuch as they had no expectation of taking office, and yet could determine the policy of the Government; and that the Referendum would correct the gross anomalies of the existing representative system. He added that Swiss experience was of slight value, the conditions being too dissimilar, and admitted that the Referendum might not give a clear answer on the issue submitted to it, and on that alone; but he held that it should be obligatory under certain conditions and urged that the Unionist party should work out a scheme for its application. On the other hand, Mr. Jesse Collings followed Mr. Austen Chamberlain in declaring for the withdrawal of the Referendum from the Unionist programme.

The Unionist candidate for Horncastle, Captain Weigall, at first attempted to concentrate his supporters on the constitutional question, declaring himself opposed to food taxes and desirous of postponing Tariff Reform; but he was promptly menaced with the opposition of the Tariff Reform League, and was constrained to make a profession of his faith in its cause. At the poll (17 February) Captain A. G. Weigall (U.) was returned by 4,955 votes, Alderman Linfield (L.) receiving 4,848.

The echoes of the conflict were heard in the New Year messages of some of the Bishops. The Archbishop of Canterbury, after referring to the death of King Edward VII. and the two general elections, said that the issues of these changes and chances might be vaster than the world knew, and hinted at the dangers indicated by the altered views on marriage put forward by some witnesses before the Divorce Commission. He found hope, however, in the wider bases of Christian co-operation exhibited in the Students’ Movement and the World Missionary Conference of 1910, following on the Church assemblages of 1908; and he invoked the Divine blessing on the whole of the nation and the Empire in this year of assembling for “the sacring of our King.” The Bishop of Salisbury dealt directly with the possible consequences to the Church of a reform of the House of Lords. He laid stress on the value to the country of the presence of the Bishops in that House, and desired to see other religious bodies represented there likewise. If the Bishops were excluded, the Church would need a legal representative body in some degree co-ordinate with Parliament, and analogous to the Swedish Kyrkomöte.

However, the public mind was abruptly diverted from these remoter possibilities by one of the most amazing scenes of disorder and bloodshed in English history. On 16 December 1910 a gang of foreign burglars, chiefly Letts, had been disturbed by the City of London police while preparing to break into a jeweller’s shop in Houndsditch, and had opened fire on them, killing one and fatally injuring two. One of the burglars had also been mortally wounded, probably by a companion accidentally, and conveyed to his lodgings, where he died. The City police had been searching for the criminals, one of whom, known as “Peter the Painter,” was believed to be specially dangerous; and in the small hours of 3 January they surrounded a house, No. 100 Sidney Street, Mile End Road, a little way east of the London Hospital, where they had reason to believe that two of the wanted men, known as Fritz Svaars and Josef, were lodging. They cleared the house adroitly of the other inmates, and then aroused the men by throwing pebbles at the window of their room. They were at once received with a continuous fire from Mauser automatic pistols—most formidable weapons, sighted up to 1,000 yards, and far surpassing the police revolvers. One policeman was shot; the police continued an intermittent fire, though ineffectively, and at about 9 A.M. sent for a company of Scots Guards from the Tower, whose rifles would carry farther. The criminals, however, were sheltered by their position in the house, and though firing was kept up by the police and the Guards from adjacent roofs and other points commanding the windows, they could not have been captured, or even hit, without heavy loss of life. The Home Secretary arrived at noon and conferred with the police; Horse Artillery were fetched from St. John’s Wood Barracks to batter down the house. At half-past twelve it was seen to be on fire; but the firemen, though sent for, were not allowed to act for fear of being shot by the criminals. About an hour later one of the latter exposed himself and was probably hit; the house was burnt down, and the bodies of the two men were found in the ruins. One had died of a gunshot wound; the other was suffocated. Of the police, one was badly, four slightly wounded. One Guardsman and four civilians also received bullet wounds; and a police sergeant and four firemen were injured by falling debris. Troops had not been called out against a crowd in London since the Trafalgar Square riots of 1887, nor had they fired in anger in London since the Cato Street Conspiracy in 1820.

This amazing scene, with its seeming disproportion of means to end, raised a fresh outcry against the alien immigrants who filled East London, and who had made their numbers conspicuous by thronging to watch the conflict. The excitement was increased by the mysterious murder of a French Jew at Clapham, which was supposed for a time to be political in character because the victim’s cheeks appeared to have been deliberately marked with S-shaped cuts, and it was strongly suspected that many of the so-called “political refugees” who claimed asylum in England were in fact merely criminals. Later in the year the event led to an effort to strengthen the provision against the return of deported alien criminals; the immediate results were a cry for arming the police—which, however, abated when it was learnt that the force did not desire the change—and a series of attacks on the Home Secretary for his alleged interference with the operations. He was able, however, to show in the Press (12 January) and at the inquest (18 January) that his action had been misconceived; he had not taken charge of the operations or interfered in any way with them, nor had he sent for Royal Artillery or Engineers. He accepted the responsibility, however, for the decision not to put out the fire, as an attempt to do so must have exposed the firemen to the bullets of the criminals. On 12 May, at the Central Criminal Court before Mr. Justice Grantham, the three men Dubof, Peters, and Rosen, concerned in the Houndsditch murder and burglary, were acquitted; the woman Nina Vassilieva was convicted of being accessory after the fact to the murder and sentenced to two years’ imprisonment, but, as a political refugee, not to deportation.

The preparations for the ceremonial incident to the new reign were accompanied by the final destruction of a baseless slander which had been circulated for years in all classes of society, and had persisted in spite of public and authoritative protests and contradictions during 1910. The story was that King George V., when a midshipman in the Mediterranean Fleet, had contracted a secret marriage with a daughter of Admiral Sir Michael Culme-Seymour, its Commander-in-Chief. It had been formally contradicted, and the year opened with an evident desire in public life to meet political questions in a spirit of compromise of good omen for an early adjustment of such vexed questions as South African education and the conditions affecting the British Indians. At a City function Mr. Lewis Harcourt made his first public appearance as Colonial Minister and eulogised the success of the grant of representative institutions to South Africa; Sir Richard Solomon, the High Commissioner, declared that there was no longer room for racial antagonism; and Mr. Asquith, in proposing the health of the Lord Mayor and Corporation, eulogised the Duke of Connaught’s services to the Empire and wished him God speed in his new post.

Chapter II — The Session Until Easter

Parliament was opened in state for the first time in the new reign by the King in person, accompanied by the Queen, on Monday, 6 February. The ceremony was the first public function at which the King had been present since the funeral of King Edward VII., and the Royal procession from Buckingham Palace was witnessed by exceptionally large crowds. The state coach was drawn by a team of eight of the famous cream-coloured horses; the route was lined with troops, and a Royal salute was fired by a battery of Horse Artillery in St. James’s Park as Their Majesties entered the House. The half-mourning still worn by the Court deprived the scene in the House of Lords of some of its accustomed colouring, but it was nevertheless most splendid and impressive. Before delivering his Speech, the King read and signed the Accession Declaration as established by the Act of 1910. One new and significant feature was noted—the presence of the High Commissioners in London of the four great self-governing Dominions among the audience in the House of Lords.

The King read his Speech with great distinctness. The first paragraph ran: “My Lords and Gentlemen,—In opening the first Parliament elected in my reign, the grievous loss which the Empire has sustained by the death of my beloved father is uppermost in my thoughts. When, a year ago, he addressed you from the Throne no one could have foreseen that his life of unceasing and devoted activity in the service of his subjects was so soon to be cut short. Bowing to the inscrutable decree of Providence, I take courage from his example, and I am sustained in my abiding sorrow by the sympathy extended to me by my people in every part of my dominions.” His Majesty went on to allude with satisfaction to the results of the Duke of Connaught’s mission to South Africa. In regard to foreign relations, mention was made of the pending negotiations for a new commercial treaty with Japan, replacing that of 1894, and of the existing disorder on the trade routes of Southern Persia. There had recently been some improvement, and further developments were to be awaited; the British proposals, however, “would have no other object than to see the authority of the Persian Government restored and trade protected.” The King then expressed his interest in the coming Imperial Conference, and announced his approaching visit to India. The paragraph on the Estimates omitted the usual assurance that they were intended to combine efficiency with economy. Proposals would be submitted without delay “for settling the relations between the two Houses of Parliament, with the object of securing the more effective working of the Constitution.” The only Bills specified were those removing the pauper disqualification for old-age pensions and for insurance against sickness, invalidity, and (in special trades) unemployment. “Bills dealing with other measures of importance will be introduced and proceeded with as time and opportunity allow.” The Speech concluded with the customary invocation of the Divine Blessing, and Their Majesties then left the House.

The Suffragists attempted in vain to intercept Ministers on their way to the House, and exhibited their placards in Parliament Square; they protested against the omission of Women’s Suffrage from the King’s Speech and foreshadowed resistance to taxation and the census. The “Conciliation Bill,” however, eventually obtained a place in the private members’ ballot for 5 May.

In the House of Lords the Address was moved by Lord Ilkeston and seconded by Lord Willingdon, better known respectively as Sir Walter Foster and Mr. Freeman Thomas. The Marquess of Lansdowne began by complimentary references to the mover and seconder, and a sympathetic allusion to the position of King George V., declaring that the feeling of allegiance to him was the one bond of union between the peoples of India. Lord Lansdowne went on to describe foreign affairs as in unstable equilibrium, and to speak with some apprehension of the pending treaty negotiations with Japan and of the condition of Macedonia. On the Declaration of London he urged that the Government should take full account of the views of the representatives of the great commercial interests, which he indicated that he shared to some extent. He hoped that the Government would not wait too long before taking action in Southern Persia, and dwelt on the dangers of the pending reciprocity arrangement in Canada. After criticising the financial management of the Government, and some other matters, especially payment of Members, he asked whether the “other measures” mentioned in the Speech included Welsh Disestablishment and Home Rule.

The central legislative business of the early session was the Parliament Bill. After two days’ debate, leave was given on 22 February by 351 to 227 to introduce it. A Referendum Bill was also read a first time; opponents of the Referendum argued that it would be used as an engine of obstruction against representative government. The second reading of the Parliament Bill was followed in the Commons, on 7 March, by that of the Revenue Bill, founded on the resolutions debated 20, 23 and 24 February. It represented the matter left over from the Finance Act of 1910, though with considerable additions, and was attacked by Sir A. Cripps (U.) as disregarding the claims of the local authorities to the proceeds of the land value duties, of which the Chancellor of the Exchequer had promised them half. Mr. Adkins (L., Lancs., S.E., Middleton) urged that the withdrawal of this half should be only temporary, and he was backed by Mr. W. Pearce (L., Limehouse), who insisted that London would get only 52,500l. a year under it, whereas on a population basis it should receive 127,000l., and by Mr. Lough (L., Islington, W.). Mr. W. O’Brien (Ind. Nat., Cork City) complained of the effect of the whisky duty on Ireland, and of the stamp and succession duties, and of the alarm caused by the doctrine of land nationalisation; Ireland was suffering for the sake of a shadowy promise of Home Rule. Mr. Hobhouse, Financial Secretary to the Treasury, who was in charge of the Bill, explained that the proposed alteration in the relations between the local authorities and the Treasury was not permanent, though the Bill might be operative for more than a year or two. As the result of the removal of the pauper disqualification for old-age pensions the total rate relief would amount to 2,000,000l. a year. London would profit under the scheme by 74,000l. annually.

The Navy Estimates for 1911–12 disquieted a large section of Liberals, who had anticipated only four Dreadnoughts, and feared that the German law, which required only two Dreadnoughts in 1912, might be extended as a reply. The addition of 3,000 men was also regarded as ominous; and a series of questions addressed by Mr. R. Harcourt (L., Montrose Burghs) to the First Lord on 8 February had shown that Mr. McKenna had over-estimated the number of German Dreadnoughts which would be ready in 1913. The debate on the Estimates was therefore preceded by a Radical attack on the Government for its increase of naval and military expenditure, led, as in former years, by Mr. Murray Macdonald (L., Falkirk Burghs), who moved a resolution on 13 March to the effect that the House viewed with alarm the enormous increase of expenditure on the Army and Navy in recent years, and was of opinion that it ought to be diminished. He agreed that the Empire must be secured, but asked why the Indian Army should be stronger than it was before the Anglo-Russian Agreement, and why 11,000 troops should be kept in South Africa. Assuming the necessity of a two-Power standard for the Navy, Great Britain was exceeding that standard as compared with Germany and the United States, the next two Powers in naval strength, and they were not likely to combine. A reduction of armaments should be initiated by Great Britain. The resolution was seconded by Mr. Ponsonby (L., Stirling Burghs), who complained that extravagant expenditure was provocative to other nations, condemned the naval scare of 1909, and said that but for the Constitutional crisis the Government would only be saved that night from defeat by the Opposition. As a more practicable alternative, Mr. King (L., Somerset, N.) moved that the House viewed with profound anxiety and regret the continuance of the necessity for the maintenance by Great Britain of large armaments, and would welcome the establishment of international arrangements for the simultaneous restriction of their armaments by the Great Powers. This amendment, moved without official prompting, was seconded by Mr. Adkins as embodying the greatest common measure of agreement among Liberals. Lord Charles Beresford argued that the need of increased expenditure was due to the unsound economy of the Government before 1909; German activity was due to British bombast and insolence over the first Dreadnought; German power of acceleration in shipbuilding had increased enormously; the Fleet margin was far too small.

The shipbuilding and armament Votes together showed an increase of 3,250,000l., caused mainly by the amount required to complete the programme already approved. New construction for the current year would cost 15,063,877l. as compared with 13,279,830l. Of this, 13,325,232l. would be spent in continuing work on ships already begun, and 1,738,645l. for beginning work on ships of the new programme, consisting of five large armoured ships, three protected cruisers, one unarmoured cruiser, twenty destroyers, six submarines, two river gunboats, a depôt ship for destroyers, and a hospital ship. On 1 April 1911 there would be under construction ten battleships, three armoured cruisers, seven protected cruisers, three unarmoured cruisers, thirty-two destroyers, twelve submarines; and of these by the end of the financial year 1911–12 there would be completed six battleships, two armoured cruisers, four protected cruisers, two unarmoured cruisers, twenty destroyers and six submarines. The two floating docks for Portsmouth and the Medway would also be complete by the end of 1911–12. Besides this the Dominion Governments would have under construction two armoured and two protected cruisers and two submarines. Mr. McKenna insisted on the necessity of maintaining naval supremacy, and stated that in the spring of 1914 Great Britain would have thirty Dreadnought battleships and cruisers; seven old ships had been disposed of in 1911, and seven would follow in 1912. The Rosyth works the contractor hoped to finish in September 1914—two years ahead of the contract time; the Admiralty hoped in September 1915 at the outside. Rosyth would ultimately be made a first-class dockyard. He defended the increase in men, as destroyers could not be withdrawn from service to the same extent as battleships, and the new ships carried larger complements.

Mr. Lee (U., Hants, Fareham) contended that in 1914 there would be no British margin in Dreadnoughts against the Triple Alliance, and our Navy was exceptionally liable to risks. He deplored the reduction of the Marines, and declared the provision for new cruisers deficient in view of the probable revival of virtual privateering if the Declaration of London were ratified. We had no recognised standard for the Navy; the two-Power standard was dead, or useless owing to its contradictory interpretations by Ministers. The old standard was two keels to one, which was now tacitly sanctioned by the Admiralty, but the Estimates did not give effect to it. Mr. G. Roberts (Lab., Norwich) moved as an amendment that the increasing expenditure on the Navy was not justified by foreign events and was a menace to peace and national security. Mr. Balfour, whose speech made a great impression, held it necessary to endure what was almost an intolerable burden rather than risk visible dangers. Soft words and diplomacy had hitherto failed to secure disarmament, and the hard facts of the situation in 1914 had to be faced. Mr. G. Lambert, Civil Lord of the Admiralty, declared that there was no evidence that foreign merchantmen carried armaments, but there were 104 possible commerce destroyers among British merchantmen, while the next strongest Power had only seventeen; Great Britain was also far superior in cruisers. By March 1914 the British Empire would have thirty-two Dreadnoughts (including the Dominion Dreadnoughts) against Germany’s twenty-one. Mr. McKenna, winding up, said that in armoured, protected, and unarmoured cruisers no possible combination of Fleets could come near the British; even were Mr. Lee’s forecast realised, Great Britain would have the advantage in unity of command and in pre-Dreadnoughts. The supporters of reduction were like Ethelred the Unready, who believed in financial resources rather than ships. A strong Navy was a good foundation on which to build up credit. After other speeches, the reduction was negatived by 233 to 21. The Archer-Shee case, which was among the subjects touched in the Naval Estimates debates, was also noticed in the session.

Sir Edward Grey’s speech on Anglo-American arbitration was cordially received on both sides of the Atlantic, alike by the general public and by the Churches. Some misgivings were expressed at the possibility that an arbitration treaty might lead to a defensive alliance; in America this seemed to violate the tradition against “entangling alliances,” and in England it was suggested (by Mr. Terrell, M.P., in The Times) that the result would be a series of mutually hostile defensive alliances and an increase of armaments. But Sir Edward Grey’s views received Mr. Balfour’s endorsement; in Germany his reference to the international situation was cordially responded to; in Austria the speech was also well received, though treated as a sign that Great Britain had reached the limit of her ability to expand armament and should husband her resources and reduce the income tax.

Among incidental events of the early months, at the Cambridge University Parliamentary bye-election, due to the death of Mr. S. H. Butcher, Professor Sir J. Larmor was returned by 2,308 votes; Mr. Harold Cox received 1,954 and Mr. T. E. Page 332. At Oxford, Dr. T. H. Warren, President of Magdalen College, was elected Professor of Poetry, vice Mr. J. W. Mackail, by 174 votes against 147 given to Canon H. C. Beeching. In the Upper House of Convocation, resolutions were carried deprecating marriage of divorced persons in church, and any extension of the facilities for divorce. Earl Beauchamp was appointed an Ecclesiastical Commissioner vice Earl Cawdor, deceased. The Very Rev. Robert Gregory, Dean of St. Paul’s since 1868, tendered his resignation to take effect from 1 May. On 12 May the Festival of Empire at the Crystal Palace was opened by the King and Queen, the numbers present being 144,234; and at Hendon Aerodrome a display of military aviation organised by the Parliamentary Aerial Defence Committee took place, Mr. Balfour and Mr. McKenna being taken for short flights, and despatches being sent experimentally to Aldershot. At the South Birmingham bye-election, due to the succession of Lord Morpeth (U.) to the Earldom of Carlisle, Mr. L. S. Amery (U.) was returned unopposed. The Duke of Connaught opened the Scottish National Exhibition in Glasgow. On 3 May the Speaker of the House of Commons celebrated his silver wedding; a pair of silver salvers were presented to him by his colleagues and a diamond pendant to Mrs. Lowther.

Chapter III — From Easter to the Coronation

The usual Easter conferences of the National Union of Teachers and the Independent Labour and Socialist parties dealt only with side issues of the great political struggle. The first-named body met at Aberystwyth under its first woman President, Miss Isabel Cleghorn, whose advent was duly celebrated by the presentation to her of her own portrait and other gifts. She gave an interesting sketch of an ideal national education in her address; but although the union had thus recognised the equality of the sexes, its members defeated two attempts on successive days to suspend the Standing Orders in order to debate women’s suffrage, and there were disorderly scenes. The Holmes circular, and the conduct of the Board of Education generally, received vigorous denunciation. The Independent Labour party at Birmingham complained of the political pressure exercised by Ministers, who treated nearly every motion as a vote of confidence, the rejection of which might entail a dissolution; but a resolution calling on the Labour members to vote on motions on their merits, and a temporising amendment, were both shelved by “the previous question.” The conference declared, inter alia, for the nationalisation of mines, the abolition of the House of Lords, adult suffrage, and wider powers of self-government for India. The Social Democratic party at Coventry showed considerable distrust of the Parliamentary Labour party, and adopted resolutions in favour of an adequate Navy and a citizen Army, in view of the probability (insisted on by Mr. H. M. Hyndman) of a German attack on France by way of Belgium. This led to a further split in their ranks.

The House of Commons resumed its work on Easter Tuesday, 18 April. After the introduction of the Aliens Bill and a demand by the Government for the remainder of private members’ time till Whitsuntide—which was accorded, after debate, by 176 to 128—it spent some thirteen hours in rejecting further amendments to the first clause of the Parliament Bill. Among the more noteworthy of these was an attempt by Lord Hugh Cecil to exempt Bills for payment of members, on which Mr. Asquith pointed out the bearing of the Government’s programme. The Committee stage of the Parliament Bill occupied the greater part of the remaining weeks before the Coronation, the Government pressing the measure as the indispensable preliminary to the effective working of the Constitution as announced in the King’s Speech. Concurrently the National Insurance Bill, foreshadowed in the Speech as providing insurance against sickness, invalidity, and (in special trades) unemployment, was brought forward as the principal social measure of the session, and from the outset its details began to be closely scrutinised by friendly societies, the Labour party, and employers. A Women’s Enfranchisement (Conciliation) Bill, which had obtained a place in the private members’ ballot for 5 May, continued the agitation of the suffrage societies, while the Imperial Conference opened in London and received the King and the Dominion Premiers, whose presence in the capital was also bound up with the approaching Coronation.

The close of the Parliament Bill debates in the Commons was invested with fresh interest by the news which arrived in the interval before the last day that the German Government had sent a cruiser to Agadir. Though carefully guarded, the German intimation was thought to be designed to cover a determination to take a hand in the partition of Morocco already, as it seemed, begun by France and Spain. The debate found an echo, for the same reason, in the discussion of the Shipbuilding Vote on 3 July. This was opened by Mr. Lee (U., Hants, Fareham), who urged that more protected cruisers of the second or third class should be built; the danger to British food supplies would be in the first few weeks of a war, when there might be a panic and famine prices. He had trustworthy information that Germany, Austria, and Italy had arranged that certain of their large liners should at all times carry guns and ammunition, so as to be readily convertible into warships. In 1904 Great Britain had 103 effective cruisers, Germany twenty; in 1911 the numbers were seventy to thirty-five. Cruisers no longer efficient for Fleet action in home waters should be presented to the Dominions, not scrapped. He complained also of a shortage of destroyers. Lord Charles Beresford endorsed these demands. As to Morocco, Mr. Asquith could only say in reply to the Opposition leader (6 July) that discussion was in progress between the Powers; a new situation had arisen, in which future developments might affect British interests more directly than had previously been the case; diplomatic discussion would find a solution, and Great Britain in taking part in it would have regard to those interests, and to her treaty obligations with France. This statement was received with satisfaction in Paris and with calm in Berlin.

Chapter IV — From the Coronation to the Summer Recess

An immense contrast to the disturbed conditions of politics and of industry was presented by the Coronation ceremony and its attendant functions and celebrations throughout the Kingdom and the Empire. For three or four days party strife was suspended, and the people not only of the capital, but of the whole Kingdom, were enabled to participate at least in spirit in that formal acceptance and recognition of their Sovereign which is actually gone through by the privileged few who are present at the Coronation service, and is a survival from the earliest period of the establishment of Kingship. Never in British history has that recognition been more cordial or more loyal, never has the personal participation in it been so widespread or so splendid in its circumstance and pomp. Again the ancient British Monarchy was exhibited as, among all monarchies, the most popular and the most democratic.

In showery and somewhat unpromising weather, the morning of Coronation Day, 22 June, found the London streets on the route of the Royal Procession to and from the Abbey already crowded by sightseers, many of whom had taken up their positions early on the previous evening, while others had come in during the small hours from the provinces and the suburbs. For the three days of the festivities 3,000,000 visitors had been expected in London; more than sixty thousand troops lined the route, and there were twelve thousand police. Great preparations had been made in view of the expected crowds; along the line of the procession barriers had been erected across the side streets, to prevent an inrush from behind the spectators; but the apprehensions of a great crush had kept many people away, and the crowd was at no time greater than that at the Coronation of Edward VII. The frequent extra trains from the suburbs were far from full; it was unexpectedly easy to get away after the processions had passed; and holiday excursions were run from London to various seaside resorts, which acted as a further relief. The first procession, that of the Royal and other guests and representatives of foreign States, left Buckingham Palace (unfortunately in closed carriages) at 9.30 A.M. for the Abbey. It was loudly cheered, the most cordial reception being given to the representatives of Germany, the United States, and France. Next came the procession of the Royal Family, chiefly consisting of the Princesses and their suites; the Royal children in the last carriage were received with enthusiasm all along the route. Then, leaving the Palace at 10.30, the King’s procession came, headed by a military escort, on which followed carriages with officials of the household, military and naval aides-de-camp, and high officers of State.

In the interim an important step was taken in another region of foreign policy by the signature on 13 July of a new Anglo-Japanese Treaty prolonging the existing alliance of 1905 till July 1921, and providing that, should either party make a general arbitration Treaty with a third Power, that party should not be bound by the Treaty of Alliance to go to war with such third Power. This new provision was, of course, due to the Anglo-American arbitration negotiations then in progress, which had resulted on 28 June in an agreement between the two Governments to refer any dispute between them to arbitration. It was semi-officially intimated that, in pursuance of the new policy of taking the Dominions into the full confidence of the Imperial Government, the revision and renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Treaty had only been carried out after full consultation with their Premiers.

But the immediate interest of the moment lay in the Committee Stage of the Parliament Bill in the House of Lords, which had begun on 28 June. After the discussion and withdrawal of two less important amendments, the Earl of Cromer moved his proposal to transfer the decision from the Speaker to a Joint Committee, dwelling on the danger that the Speaker might become a party officer, as in the United States. He paid a cordial tribute, however, to Mr. Lowther’s impartiality. Viscount Haldane pointed out that the amendment would in effect restore to the House of Lords powers it had tacitly resigned; moreover, the Speaker would feel unable to rule out amendments in the Commons, as this would prejudge their admissibility, which would be a question for the Joint Committee. The amendment, of which the supporters included the Marquess of Lansdowne and the Archbishop of Canterbury, was carried by 183 to 44, the Lord Chancellor, however, saying that nothing would induce the Government to accept it. The subsequent stages of the Bill in the Lords became the occasion of a deep division among Unionist Peers between those who counselled surrender and those who counselled resistance. Lord Ribblesdale counselled surrender, remarking that “the game was up,” and that “with dear food round their necks and the House of Lords on their backs” the Opposition were not likely to win a general election. Viscount St. Aldwyn pointed out that if the resistance were persisted in the Bill would become law after a prorogation of Parliament, and the constituencies were absolutely apathetic. He complained strongly that there had been no full disclosure of what had taken place between Ministers and the King, who had been put in a most cruel position, and who should have been allowed to consult the Unionist leaders. His Majesty had been bound in the recent crisis by his hypothetical promise of November 1910. He himself believed that this exercise of the Prerogative had killed its use for the future, and he strongly deprecated resistance and Unionist division. Lord Willoughby de Broke, a leader among the resisters, declared in the course of a vigorous speech that the Government might win a dozen general elections without altering his opposition to the Bill; he warned the Bishops that the Establishment could only be retained through the agency of the Tory party, and declared himself ready to obstruct the Bill by every means. The Dukes of Bedford and Marlborough also supported resistance. Lord Newton, in an amusing speech, described the attitude of the resisters as that of the Chinese who killed themselves on the doorstep of the person who had ill-treated them, and said that, so far from ridicule killing the creation, “they would have the ridicule and the Government the Peers.” The Bishop of Winchester, while admitting “the tremendous case against the Bill,” feared that resistance would bring the House of Lords into indignation and contempt and so discredit it as a Second Chamber. After other speeches, the debate was resumed next day by Lord Midleton, who claimed the right of the House to know, first, whether the Government had received from the King authority to create such a number of Peers as they might consider necessary for the passage of the Bill substantially as it left the Commons; secondly, what was the Government’s reply to the charges made in Lord St. Aldwyn’s speech? The Government had made the crisis and intended to make it; the Conference, and their action, were shams. He then argued in favour of submission, insisting especially on the danger that the Bill might be thrown out by a small majority of obscure Peers. A dramatic scene followed. The Earl of Rosebery rose and backed the demand for a clear statement as to the “guarantees.” The Parliament Bill was eventually read a third time in the House of Lords (20 August chronicle notice), and passed into law after the resistance of the “die-hards” had been overcome by the prospect of a large creation of Peers.

Meanwhile the National Insurance Bill was being brought into prominence both in the House and outside, and certain of its details met with strong objection from the Labour party, backed by some Liberals and Unionists. Broadly, it may be said that the Bill was thought to do too little for the weakest classes of the people, the casuals, the sweated workers, the poor lives, and the strugglers generally. Thus, on 17 July attempts were vainly made to extend full medical benefit to persons entitled to maternity benefit; to extend maternity benefit to unmarried mothers (Mr. Keir Hardie’s proposal, defeated by 207 to 95); to make sick pay run from the first day of illness (as desired by the friendly societies) instead of the fourth as provided in the Bill. Next day indeed the Chancellor of the Exchequer appealed to members not to seek popularity by voting for the removal of what they knew to be proper safeguards for the working of the Bill. But on the day following an amendment was moved by Mr. Lees Smith (L., Northampton) exempting insured persons who had fallen into arrears through unemployment from their obligation to pay up the employers’ arrears as well as their own in order to resume their claim to benefits; and this proposal was only defeated (by 163 to 116) because the Chancellor urged that its success might involve the defeat of the Bill. The whole clause dealing with arrears was carried as amended by 210 to 77. The rejection of the amendments intensified the Labour denunciations of the Bill as a whole; and at the pending bye-elections the Unionists made much of its defects.

During the week, too, the House had disposed of the Corporation of London (Bridges) Bill, which had excited much discussion outside. A new “St. Paul’s” bridge, as planned by the City Corporation, was to be part of a broad thoroughfare extending in a practically straight line from the east side of St. Paul’s Churchyard, which was to be widened, to the junction of Great Guildford Street and Southwark Street on the Surrey side. This thoroughfare would be almost a continuation of Aldersgate Street, and it was arranged that at some future time the County Council tramways might be brought thence through a subway under St. Paul’s Churchyard to the new bridge—a course which raised fears for the stability of the Cathedral, but the Chapter was investigating the foundations and had secured the insertion of protective clauses in the Bill. The total cost, including the rebuilding of Southwark Bridge, would be within 2,250,000l. The Royal Institute of British Architects, however, and other authorities on street architecture, held that a much more effective scheme æsthetically would be to make the new thoroughfare begin exactly opposite the South transept of the Cathedral, so as to secure a full view of the dome. This plan had been rejected by the City Corporation as too costly, as involving a crossing of the river obliquely to the current, which would entail engineering difficulties and æsthetic objections, and, according to some authorities, would necessitate the bridge being of steel instead of stone. The Bill had been read a second time in the Commons on 14 February, and referred to a Select Committee, which had reported in its favour; but, when it came up on 14 June for third reading it had been referred back to the Committee on the motion of Mr. Morrell (L., Burnley) with an instruction (carried by 156 to 90) that that body should not agree to any scheme for the construction of the proposed bridge, unless it were satisfied that the plans had been prepared under the advice and supervision of leading architects and that the scheme, in architectural design and convenience of traffic, was the best adapted to the public needs and the character of the site. The Corporation, however, consulted three prominent architects, who disapproved of the alternative, but recommended certain widenings and removal of buildings all round the east end of the Cathedral. Accordingly the Committee reported in favour of the scheme as modified. When the Bill came up again for third reading on 18 July, Mr. Dickinson (L., St. Pancras, N.) moved its rejection, and another Liberal, Mr. Collins (Kennington), urged that a great improvement should not be spoilt for “a paltry 2,000,000l.”; but Mr. Balfour strongly supported the third reading, remarking that the House was not an artistic body, and the Bill was read a third time by 271 to 104. The Bill became law in August.

The Colonial Office Vote was taken on 20 July. The main subject dealt with was the Imperial Conference. A number of Tariff Reformers, among them Sir Gilbert Parker and Mr. Lyttelton, deplored the scantiness of its results, emphasising, however, the extreme importance of the new departure taken in informing the Dominion Ministers as to the international situation, and desired that the pending Royal Commission on the trade of the Empire should be instructed to report on Imperial Preference. Mr. Harcourt, in reply, said that the decision of the electors at two elections had prevented any allusion to Imperial Preference at the conference. The resolution establishing the Commission on Imperial Trade bound it not to make recommendations as to the fiscal policy of any part of the Empire. It was hardly fair to judge what was accomplished by the resolutions passed. The Secretariate had boiled down and digested the materials for the conference and had given satisfaction to the Dominion Premiers. Passing to other matters raised by various speakers, Mr. Harcourt said that in Nigeria fines were paid in gin in the native courts which were under the jurisdiction, but not the management, of Great Britain, and the practice would now be stopped. Everything was done to encourage the use of coin.

The session hitherto, however, had not been unfruitful in legislation, in spite of the inordinate amount of time devoted to the Parliament Bill and the Declaration of London. Among the minor measures passed the following may be noticed. The Official Secrets Bill strengthened the law against espionage by extending the definition of places prohibited to the public so as to cover, e.g., private shipbuilding yards in which Government work was being done, requiring suspected persons to justify their action when incriminated, and allowing their arrest on the warrant of a magistrate instead of, as hitherto customary, only after leave from the Attorney-General. The Geneva Convention Bill checked the improper use of the Red Cross flag, in accordance with the Geneva Convention of 1906. The Perjury Bill was described by the Lord Chancellor as the first of a series of measures for codifying and consolidating the criminal law. The Labourers’ (Ireland) Bill gave further facilities for building cottages under the Act of 1906 and provided another million pounds to be advanced for the purpose. An Old Age Pensions Bill, brought in by the Government to replace a private member’s measure, made relaxations in the qualifications for pension. Finally, a Protection of Animals Bill, while mainly a measure of consolidation, increased the maximum penalties for cruelty, interfered with the destruction of animals (other than vermin) by poison, and provided for the inspection every twelve hours of hare and rabbit traps.

Chapter V — The Recess and the Autumn Session

Industrial unrest culminated in August in a railway crisis of extreme gravity. The Board of Trade had invited representatives of the railway companies and the railwaymen’s unions to confer with its officials on 16 August; and the two bodies did so separately on that day. The general manager of the Midland (Sir Guy Granet) stated afterwards that the Government had promised the companies protection, and that they were prepared to give “an efficient, if restricted, service” during a strike. Next day (17 August) the executives of the men’s unions, who had come up from Liverpool during the night, after resolving not to settle the dispute unless the Liverpool lock-out were ended, went with the chairman of the Labour party to the Board of Trade, and conferred with the President. He put a series of questions to them relating to their grievances; in reply, they declared substantially that their grievance consisted in the failure of the companies to observe the spirit and letter of the Agreement of 1907, and that the only remedy was a meeting between the companies and the men’s representatives or a strike. The Prime Minister subsequently addressed them and offered them a Royal Commission immediately to investigate the working of the conciliation scheme of 1907, and report as to amendments facilitating the prompt settlement of differences; the Government, he said, were absolutely impartial, but their first consideration must be the public interest, and they could not allow a general paralysis of the railway system. He warned the men’s representatives of the responsibility involved in refusal. The latter, apparently, disliked his tone and thought his offer a device for shelving the dispute. They therefore met subsequently, and, after telephoning to ask if the Prime Minister was doing anything to bring representatives of the companies and the unions together that day, and receiving a negative reply, they passed a resolution declaring the statement “an unwarrantable threat against the railway workers,” and declining the responsibility referred to, and gave the signal for a general strike. The Labour party also drafted a vote of censure on the Government. On being informed of this motion through the chief Liberal whip, Mr. Asquith sent back word to the Labour members that what was meant was a Commission of three, one representing each side, with an independent chairman of high standing, which would sit at once and report forthwith. He himself, being overtired, left London; and the Chancellor of the Exchequer took steps to reopen the negotiations with the union leaders. He stated in the House of Commons on that evening that the companies’ representatives had accepted the Commission, but the men’s had repudiated it; they possibly had not quite understood it; but negotiations had been resumed. He explained the nature of the Commission, and counselled patience and self-restraint. Mr. Ramsay Macdonald, who followed, stated that the offer had been misunderstood, and that the Vote of Censure would not be moved.

It was not known at the time that the peremptory tone taken by the Prime Minister and to some extent by the Home Secretary was due in part to the critical condition of Anglo-German relations, a condition, indeed, of which the public was scarcely aware till November; and, pending negotiations, the strike began. The London and South-Western Railway was not affected at all; the other lines were interfered with in varying degrees. The worst stoppages were in the quadrilateral of which the four extremities are, roughly, Newcastle, Liverpool, Coventry and Hull, and also in South Wales. At Derby there were disturbances, and also at Llanelly. In London, the Great Central, Great Western, and Brighton lines suffered most; St. Paul’s and Cannon Street stations were closed, and Marylebone very nearly so. As compared with the corresponding week of 1910, the Great Western traffic showed a decrease of 40 per cent., that of the North Staffordshire Railway 48 per cent. Troops were moved to London and encamped in the parks, and also to Birmingham, Sheffield and elsewhere, and the stations and strategic points were occupied. An appeal was made in London for special constables, and 6,000 men volunteered for service. However, the Board of Trade continued to negotiate with both parties, and on that evening Mr. Churchill made a statement in the House of Commons. He mentioned that outrages had been committed on railway property, a crime punishable by penal servitude, described the situation at various centres, and said that the Government would take all necessary steps to secure the non-interruption of the supplies of food and fuel and the maintenance of the services vital to the community. They would do this because they were bound at all costs to protect the public against the dangers and miseries which famine and a general arrest of industry would entail, and from which the working classes would be the worst sufferers. Should the means adopted not prove effective, other and larger measures would be taken. It had been intended that the House should adjourn that day till 24 October, but, in view of the situation, it adjourned only till 22 August. In this decision, as was afterwards revealed, an important factor was the danger of a breach with Germany. The railway strike lasted on throughout Saturday, 19 August. Some disturbances were reported from Darlington, Hull, and other points; and in South Wales the general unrest led to serious riots in Ebbw Vale, Tredegar, and the neighbourhood, directed largely against the numerous Jewish shopkeepers and house-owners. A special prayer for use in parish churches and elsewhere during the strikes had been authorised by the Archbishop of Canterbury on 18 August.

When Parliament reassembled, on 22 August, the Prime Minister moved its adjournment till 24 October, and said that the clouds had now risen; thanks were due to all who had contributed in any way to the settlement, and he hoped the Commission would be allowed a fair field in its delicate and responsible task. He then read out the terms of reference and the names of the members. Sir David Harrel, K.C.B., sometime Permanent Under-Secretary for Ireland, was chairman; the other members were Sir Thomas Ratcliffe Ellis, secretary to the Mining Association of Great Britain and the Federated Coal Owners’ Association; Mr. Charles G. Beale, a Birmingham solicitor and bank director and Vice-Chancellor of Birmingham University; Mr. Arthur Henderson, Labour M.P. for the Barnard Castle division of Durham; and Mr. John Burnett, sometime Labour Correspondent of the Board of Trade. Mr. J. J. Wills, of the Board of Trade’s Labour Department, was Secretary. The function of the Commission was to investigate the working of the Conciliation Scheme of 1907 and to report what changes in it, if any, were desirable. Mr. Asquith added that all the Commissioners were men of practical experience, and he felt sure both the House and the country would wish them God speed. Mr. Ramsay Macdonald (Lab., Leicester) thought the settlement might be accepted on both sides as a guarantee of peace if fairly carried out. He pointed out that the grievances differed in different places; and he attacked the Home Office for issuing prejudiced and inaccurate bulletins and for their reckless display of force. He protested against this reversion to medieval ideas of the maintenance of law and order. Mr. Austen Chamberlain, on the other hand, approved the action of the Government; the right to work was not less sacred than the right to strike. Mr. J. H. Thomas (Lab., Derby), who had been engaged in the negotiations as a member of the railwaymen’s executive, praised the way in which the companies had met the men’s representatives, but endorsed Mr. Ramsay Macdonald’s protests regarding the Home Office; and, among later speakers, Mr. Stewart (U., Cheshire, Wirral) gave some account of the intimidation by “peaceful” pickets. Mr. Winston Churchill, after contemptuously dismissing the criticism of the Home Office bulletins, pointed out that, had the general strike continued for a week on the lines contemplated by the unions, the consequences to the community would have been catastrophic. The Home Secretary also stated that had the carmen’s strike in London continued there would have been a regular system of convoy; it would be the duty of the Government, in the event of a paralysis of the railway lines, to give full protection to those working them, and to ensure that no great disaster should happen through failure of the machinery for the supply of food. A statement as to the position in Liverpool and Manchester was also made by Viscount Haldane in the other House.

The first few days after the adjournment of the two Houses saw the restoration of industrial peace. The Railway Commission held its first meeting on 23 August, and began to take evidence six days later, taking the men’s side first; the carters’ disputes at Manchester were also settled on 23 August, the Liverpool dockers’ strike was declared at an end on the evening of the next day. A prolongation of it had seemed imminent, owing to the threats of a fresh outbreak on the part of the tramwaymen, upon the refusal of the municipal authorities to reinstate indiscriminately those who had struck; some 37,000 men were brought out, or kept out, in sympathy; but it was agreed that the tramwaymen should be reinstated as and when required, and subject to the satisfaction of the general manager; and the labour leaders advised the strikers in other trades to return to work. A few days later (31 August) a similar difficulty as to the reinstatement of strikers on the Great Eastern Railway was also compromised after the intervention of the Board of Trade; and a little later the great services of Mr. G. B. Askwith, one of its officials, as a conciliator in the recent disputes, were recognised by a knighthood. But there was a general feeling that the labour war was only suspended, and preparations for its recurrence were visible in the subscriptions raised locally for the railway men who had remained at their posts, still more in the suggestion that a volunteer corps of “strike breakers” should be formed in each locality to secure the services indispensable to the life of the community, such as lighting and railway travel, during times of industrial conflict, and in the suggestion of a volunteer police reserve which took shape in a circular from the Home Office to Chief Constables, issued 15 September. In Wales, too, the anti-Jewish riots which were the outcome of the labour unrest continued at Bargoed on 24 August. They were ascribed to the raising of rents by Jewish houseowners.

The adjournment left the Government stronger than it had been for many months past. If during the strikes it had gone far to estrange the Labour party and some advanced Liberals, its firmness had done something to mitigate the hostility of the mass of Unionists, and it had secured a brief respite for itself and for the public by the appointment of the Special Commission. It had also gained in popularity, apparently, by the Insurance Bill. The Unionist party, on the other hand, was disorganised, torn by dissensions, smarting under severe defeat, and eminently suspicious of its own leadership. In the autumn Mr. Bonar Law, addressing a great meeting at Leeds, attacked the Government’s course since the passage of the Parliament Act. With regard to Home Rule, the Government was under dictation from a small faction in the Commons, which cared nothing for British politics. They were now living under a provisional Constitution; the Government had not reformed the Second Chamber, but only readjusted the relations between the Houses; they might have done both by their felon’s stroke; their Irish allies compelled them to do the second only, so as to prevent the submission of Home Rule to the electorate. So great a change should not be carried while the Constitution was in suspense. He contended for the rights of the minority in Ireland, and declared again that the Ministry was endangering representative Government. Turning to social conditions, with special reference to Tariff Reform, he said that the unrest among the working classes was intensified by the Budget campaign of 1909, of which the Labour disturbances of 1911 were the fruit, and by the increase of wealth and the rise of nearly 10 per cent. in the cost of living, with no corresponding rise in wages. The Government’s method of readjustment was to take from the rich by taxation and give to the poor in doles; capital and population were going abroad in consequence. A fiscal change would tend to raise wages and get rid of that greatest of all evils, chronic unemployment. The combination of a rise in the cost of living with stationary wages was, he declared, peculiar to Great Britain, and he cited a British consular report on Germany in proof. The burdens placed on industry by the Insurance Bill varied from 1s. to 20s. in the pound; the consumer should bear the burden: Prince Bismarck, who introduced national insurance, had begun by a fiscal change. The Canadian elections would be “a landmark in the history of the world.” The Unionist leaders had never attacked Sir Wilfrid Laurier, whose great services in leading the way in the policy of Preference he gratefully acknowledged, but they had a right to rejoice in the Canadian decision. The Radical Press had treated the proposed Treaty as a step towards Free Trade; its rejection was a recoil from it. He denounced the statement that the elections had nothing to do with Preference. They were an emphatic reminder that that was still the policy of the self-governing Dominions. It was now the turn of the Old Country; the Unionists were now the prophets of a larger Unionism. At an overflow meeting Mr. Bonar Law attacked the petrol tax as affecting trade motor vehicles, urging that a tax of 10 per cent. on imported manufactures would be preferable; and denounced the removal, for political reasons, of the duty on imported cocoa. As to Home Rule, he inferred from a comparison of Mr. Asquith’s and Mr. Redmond’s statements that Mr. Redmond held that his Bill, not Mr. Asquith’s, would pass the House of Commons. He cited a recent speech by Mr. Hobhouse to illustrate the relations of the “Radical-Nationalist coalition.”

In the autumn the National Insurance Bill completed its progress. Though its progress through Committee had been described as “kaleidoscopic,” most of the hundreds of amendments made in it were verbal and consequential, and the chief changes and additions may be roughly outlined as follows: Sick pay (10s. per week for men, 7s. 6d. for women) was to continue for twenty-six weeks instead of thirteen. Maternity benefit was extended to the wife of an insured person even if she herself was an insured person. Sanatorium benefit (for consumption) might be extended to the dependants of the insured consumptive. Married women might now come in during their husband’s lifetime as voluntary contributors with reduced payments, receiving sickness and disablement benefit. Soldiers, sailors and other naval men, and mercantile seamen, might come in under special provisions; so might fishermen co-partners under regulations to be made by the Commissioners according to varying local customs. Where it was the custom for the employer to pay full wages during sickness the contributions of both employer and employed might be reduced and the sickness benefit likewise if the employer would agree to pay wages for six weeks of illness. (This affected especially agricultural labourers and clerks.) Persons earning less than 9s. a week paid no contribution, those between 9s. and 12s., 1d. per week less than under the original Bill. (This specially affected women outworkers.) The local Health Committees were to be called Insurance Committees, and their numbers and powers had been considerably increased. The “Post Office” part of the Bill was made distinctly provisional. There were to be separate Commissioners for Ireland, Scotland, and Wales. The provisions for Ireland were considerably altered; inter alia the employer’s contribution was reduced to 2½d. a week; migratory labourers and outworkers whose work was done in leisure time were exempted, medical benefit was withdrawn in view of the existence of the dispensary system, and the constitution of Health Committees adapted to Irish conditions. Part II. (Unemployment) remained an integral feature of the measure. In the Lords, Lord Balfour urged that public Bills, like private, should be carried over from one session to another; the Marquess of Lansdowne said the question must form part of Second Chamber reform, and the Earl of Halsbury called attention to the tendency of Ministerial legislation to get rid of the authority of the law courts. The Committee stage proceeded rapidly. Among the unsuccessful amendments one would have given an appeal to the General Medical Council to a practitioner struck off the insurance panel, another was directed against endowment of research in connection with sanatoria, and was inspired by fear of vivisection, another would have made the Local Government Board responsible for acting on the result of the inquiry into excessive sickness. Other amendments were also rejected, but two or three, proposed by independent Peers, were adopted in substance or altogether. The Bill passed through its further stages without debate.

Militant women’s suffrage agitation also reasserted itself in the autumn. Suffragists broke, with hammers, a number of windows not only of Government offices but of shops, chiefly in the Strand and Piccadilly Circus. Fines were imposed next day, besides payment for the damage, with imprisonment as the alternative (which was accepted by several of the defendants), and some cases were sent for trial. Mrs. Pethick Lawrence, for obstructing a police officer, was sentenced to a month’s imprisonment in the second division; she was, however, eventually released on bail, pending an appeal on a legal objection to the proceedings at the police court. In all there were 223 charges, of which 148 were for wilful damage; the damage to shop windows in the Strand alone was said to amount to about 550l.

Among other incidents of the later months, at the Barnstaple bye-election, due to the resignation of Sir E. Soares, appointed Assistant Comptroller of the Debt Office, Sir George Baring (L.) was returned by 6,239 votes, Mr. C. S. Parker (U.) receiving 5,751. At the Kilmarnock Burghs bye-election, due to the death of Dr. A. R. Rainy (L.), Mr. W. G. C. Gladstone, grandson of W. E. Gladstone, was returned by 6,923 votes; Sir J. D. Rees (U.) received 4,637 and Mr. T. McKerrell (Lab.) 2,761. In the Chancery Division, in the test case of Burghes v. the Attorney-General, it was decided that “Form VIII.,” issued under the Finance Act, 1909, was unauthorised, and compliance with it was not obligatory on the plaintiff; the decision was confirmed by the Court of Appeal on 17 November. The naval airship built for the Admiralty and just accepted for them was wrecked while launching at Barrow (24 September). Announcement was made that the Rev. H. C. Beeching, D.Litt., Canon of Westminster, had been appointed Dean of Norwich, vice the Rev. H. Russell Wakefield, Bishop-elect of Birmingham. The Bank of Egypt suspended payment, the suspension being ascribed mainly to losses due to the partial failure of the Egyptian cotton crop. Opening of the new buildings of Oriel College, Oxford, erected out of the bequest from Mr. Cecil Rhodes, took place on 28 September. Definitive abandonment of the Johnson-Wells prize-fight at Earl’s Court was announced. The Turco-Italian War began on 29 September.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Apart from the ceremonies incident to the Coronation and from labour troubles the year was not specially eventful. The Royal visit (17–21 July) called forth a notable demonstration of loyalty. The Duke of Connaught opened the Scottish National Exhibition in Glasgow. At the Kilmarnock Burghs bye-election Mr. W. G. C. Gladstone was returned as already recorded. The National Insurance Bill, as finally settled, provided separate Commissioners for Scotland; and the labour unrest of the summer, including the railway stoppages and the Welsh disturbances which affected the Border counties, was felt in Scottish industrial centres, though less violently than in the English midland and northern quadrilateral of Newcastle, Liverpool, Coventry and Hull. The Insurance Bill’s special provisions for persons earning less than 9s. and between 9s. and 12s. a week, and for agricultural labourers and clerks where employers paid wages during sickness, were of particular interest to Scottish rural and industrial populations.

Ireland. The King’s Speech had omitted any specific Home Rule Bill, but the passage of the Parliament Act was universally understood to clear the path for an Irish measure in a subsequent session; and Mr. Bonar Law, in his Leeds speeches, insisted that the Government was under dictation from a small Nationalist faction, that Home Rule ought not to be carried while the Constitution was in suspense, and that the rights of the minority in Ireland must be contended for. He inferred from a comparison of Mr. Asquith’s and Mr. Redmond’s statements that Mr. Redmond held that his Bill, not Mr. Asquith’s, would pass the House of Commons, and he cited Mr. Hobhouse to illustrate the relations of the “Radical-Nationalist coalition.” The Labourers’ (Ireland) Bill gave further facilities for building cottages under the Act of 1906 and provided another million pounds to be advanced for the purpose. Under the National Insurance Bill as amended, the provisions for Ireland were considerably altered: the employer’s contribution was reduced to 2½d. a week; migratory labourers and outworkers whose work was done in leisure time were exempted; medical benefit was withdrawn in view of the existence of the dispensary system; and the constitution of Health Committees was adapted to Irish conditions. Separate Commissioners for Ireland were established. Mr. Charles A. O’Connor, Solicitor-General for Ireland, was appointed Attorney-General for Ireland. Mr. W. O’Brien, in the Revenue Bill debates, complained of the effect of the whisky duty on Ireland, and of the stamp and succession duties, and of the alarm caused by the doctrine of land nationalisation, declaring that Ireland was suffering for the sake of a shadowy promise of Home Rule.
