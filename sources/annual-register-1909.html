The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1909

The Annual Register 1909

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Before the Session

The year opened, the Annual Register recorded, amid political lassitude and gloomy prospects in foreign politics and in finance. The Near East remained alarming; the Messina earthquake was relieved only by the courage of the Navy and of British merchant seamen. At home the revenue for the past nine months had run considerably below the Estimates, while claims for old-age pensions greatly exceeded expectation. Mr. Asquith had reckoned on 500,000 pensioners; some 490,000 had already established their claims, and an addition of about 130,000 seemed probable. The Territorial Army numbered only 207,000 all ranks against a full establishment of 315,000. Ireland was becoming more disorderly, and the new Home Rule campaign in England had not proved encouraging. Women suffragists—reinforced by Finnish delegates, one a member of her legislature—continued their agitation; the unemployed held open-air meetings and, on 13 January, demonstrated before houses in Grosvenor Square.

Material for the Government’s social programme arrived early. The Departmental Committee on the Truck Acts (appointed April 1906) reported on 7 January for consolidation of the Acts, extension to outworkers, severe restriction of fines and deductions, and inspection of “living-in” arrangements for shop assistants; Mrs. H. J. Tennant and Mr. Walsh, M.P., in a minority report, would have made fines, deductions and living-in illegal altogether. More daring still, the Royal Commission on Coast Erosion and Afforestation published on 15 January a national planting scheme: some 2,500,000 acres of mountain and heath land in England and Wales, plus 1,000,000 of poor agricultural land, 6,000,000 in Scotland and at least 500,000 in Ireland, were held afforestable. Temporary winter employment for about 18,000 men directly and as many again indirectly was promised; a progressive deficit for forty years rising to some £3,500,000 would be followed, after seventy or eighty years, by a revenue of £3 16s. 6d. per cent. on accumulated excess charges. Commissioners with compulsory purchase powers and an annual free Treasury loan were recommended.

Ministers and Opposition spokesmen marked out the ground. At Liverpool on 8 January Mr. F. E. Smith answered Mr. Lloyd-George’s December speech; Mr. Haldane at East Linton spoke of the Unionist “Confederacy”; Mr. Austen Chamberlain at Birmingham (11 January) was ready to join issue on Tariff Reform and the Lords. Mr. Winston Churchill, also at Birmingham on 13 January, denounced the city’s narrow politicians, insisted that “the platform could always beat the Press,” and declared that reform of the House of Lords must be the issue at a general election—let the Peers dare to force a dissolution on the Budget. Next day at Leicester he noted trade contraction of £114,000,000 in exports, of which £40,000,000 was mere contraction in values, but held that the bottom had been reached and that 1909 would be a year of “important and democratic finance.” Lord Crewe at Brierley Hill (18 January) called the powers of the Lords the dominant issue; Lord Cromer at Leeds the same day thought Lord Rosebery’s Committee report a basis for discussion and advocated the referendum, but chiefly attacked Government finance, predicting that the next Budget would be the turning-point of fiscal policy for many years. He again referred to the flow of British capital abroad and to the Miners’ Eight Hours and Licensing Bills as unlikely to inspire confidence, and described himself and his relative the Earl of Northbrook as absolutely impenitent Free Traders.

A section of Tariff Reformers were taking active steps to end fiscal discussion within the Unionist party by intimidating Free Food members. The National Review for January described a “Confederacy” directed by an autocratic council of twelve, well supplied with money, whose object was to prevent the return of any Free Food Unionist even at the cost of letting in a Liberal. A Unionist majority of 30 to 50 was expected at a general election; fifteen or twenty Free Fooders, if returned, might frustrate its fiscal aims. The Morning Post of 10 January enumerated sixteen such members (five not seeking re-election, six doubtful), and both that paper and Central Office stated that official support would be withheld from candidates who would not accept Balfour’s Birmingham fiscal policy. The most conspicuously decided Free Fooders were Lord Robert Cecil (Marylebone, E.), Mr. Abel Smith (Herts, E.), and Mr. G. Stewart Bowles (Norwood). Lord Robert, on 22 January, refused to leave the party, criticised the anonymous “Confederates” as destructive of parliamentary independence, recalled his 1907 agreement to resign and seek re-election if a Unionist Ministry brought forward fiscal reform, and declared Balfour the only possible Unionist leader; on 28 January one of his meetings was broken up by dissentients. Stewart Bowles was censured by the Norwood Conservative Council for refusing even a duty “on imported doors and window frames.” Alleged Confederates named by the Daily Graphic included Sir Gilbert Parker and Messrs. Bonar Law, Claude Hay, J. W. Hills and Harry Marks. Lord Ridley for the Tariff Reform League (28 January) disclaimed connection with the movement; Austen Chamberlain at Birmingham (22 January) confined himself to Balfour’s policy and showed unwonted apprehension of the difficulties of framing a “scientific tariff.”

Near Eastern clouds were somewhat dispelled by settlement of the Porte’s pecuniary claims on Austria-Hungary for the annexation of Bosnia. Sir Edward Grey at Coldstream (22 January) thought the chances of agreement among the Powers increased; discussions with France and Russia had made for confidence; knowledge that Germany and Italy were genuinely working for peace had removed friction with them; and he hoped the King’s coming visit to Berlin would promote confidence in British goodwill. At home, the Craughwell murder in Co. Galway (22 January)—a constable killed while protecting two men working for a boycotted woman—was taken as an effect of Mr. Birrell’s laxity. The Tottenham murders by two Russian anarchists after a robbery attempt were used, with even less reason, to assail administration of the Aliens Act; the Home Secretary at Leeds on 4 February successfully vindicated it, the criminals having entered as seamen, whom the Act did not touch. Unemployed marches through the West End continued; five women suffragists attempting to see Asquith at Downing Street (26 January) received the usual imprisonment on refusing to be bound over. A counter-movement produced (18 January, Caxton Hall) a Men’s League for opposing Women Suffrage, with Lord Cromer as president and Lord Welby as a treasurer.

The chief drawback to Government prospects remained uncertainty about the House of Lords, which excited Unionist gibes and advanced-Liberal resentment. The Attorney-General at South Shields (26 January) and Mr. Masterman at Yarmouth (1 February) intimated that no future Liberal Government would take office without limiting the Upper House—the former seeming to contemplate creation of new Peers, the latter some statutory limitation—while Mr. Harcourt at Rossendale (6 February) recommended Campbell-Bannerman’s 1906 plan. It was not clear when the conflict would begin, though Mr. Pease, the Liberal whip, at Plymouth on 12 February strengthened belief that it would arise on the Budget. Mr. Lloyd-George, receiving a portrait of himself by Sir Luke Fildes, R.A., at the Law Society (30 January), referred to his “bad jest” about “robbing henroosts” and emphatically disclaimed any vindictive spirit: the single purpose of a Chancellor should be the protection and confirmation of the national interests. Mr. McKenna at Fishmongers’ Hall (28 January) promised to maintain the Navy in strength.

Chapter II — The Session until Easter

In grey cold February weather the King and Queen opened Parliament on Tuesday, 16 February. A procession of wives and children of the unemployed, with minatory banners and a car representing “The Englishman’s Home,” marched from Cavendish Square to the Horticultural Hall; the Queen wore two of the larger Cullinan diamonds.

The Royal Speech opened with the cordial Berlin reception and its expected effect on Anglo-German friendship; noted Waterways and Fisheries treaties and renewed arbitration agreements with France, Italy and Spain; declared that Persia imperatively needed “representative institutions in a practical form,” while views were being exchanged with Russia; found the Near East improved; and thanked the fleet for aid after the Italian earthquake. Satisfaction was expressed at the reception of Indian administrative reforms and at the South African Federation Convention. An unusually long financial paragraph stated that, owing especially to old-age provision and the increased cost of the Navy, expenditure would be considerably increased and would leave less time for other legislation. The programme included the Irish Land Bill and Housing and Town Planning Bill postponed from 1908, a Welsh Disestablishment Bill, labour exchanges, wages boards in sweated trades, London electoral and registration reform, amendment of Old Age Pensions inequalities, prohibition of landing fish caught in prohibited areas, inebriates, milk supply, and shop hours. The Poor Law Commission’s recommendations were under consideration.

In the Lords, Lansdowne welcomed the Berlin visit and the American treaties, doubted whether representative institutions were a panacea for Eastern troubles, stressed the need to trust the men on the spot in India, and commented severely on Ireland and on the “amazing” old-age pension figures from that country. Crewe ascribed Irish disorder to the Land Act of 1903 and called the Crimes Act a rotten weapon. Londonderry on 17 February described lawlessness over twenty-two counties, boycotting, cattle-driving developing into cattle-maiming, the Craughwell murder, and shooting crimes since the Arms Act was dropped. Lord Denman replied that roughly one-third of Ireland was disturbed—Clare and East Galway worst—that agrarian outrages (343) were far below 1880–81 and 1886 levels, that 1,210 persons had been brought up for agrarian offences (1,114 ordered to find bail), and that of 680 cattle-driving cases in 1908 fewer than 100 were organised crimes. Lord Macdonnell sketched land history since 1902, deplored Wyndham’s severance from Ireland, and advised a little more concession on the 1903 bonus so that purchase might finish in ten or fifteen years. The Lord Chancellor condemned jury-packing, held that the Crimes Act was not justified, and observed that the Conservative party might yet have to grant Home Rule. The Address was agreed.

In the Commons the Sessional Order against Peers’ interference in elections was retained by 221 to 142 after Mr. MacNeill moved to drop it. Balfour congratulated the country on keeping foreign affairs out of party politics, hoped for full naval discussion, and charged Ministers with driving capital abroad by rash speech; he ridiculed the “cult of consistency” that barred the Crimes Act. Asquith described Persia as going from bad to worse—Britain would neither make loans nor support the Shah under his present policy—vindicated old-age pensions in advance of the Poor Law Report, and insisted that capital went abroad as manufactured goods and created employment at home. Finance must be the first business of the session; social reform had to be paid for; the Old Age Pension Act was only the first instalment. Mr. Henderson for Labour foreshadowed opposition to increased naval expenditure; Mr. Barnes moved an unemployment amendment blaming private landownership and “capitalism,” and prescribing land-value taxation, afforestation, railway nationalisation and shorter hours. Mr. Harold Cox ridiculed putting skilled artisans to afforestation. Mr. Burns’s reply—distinguishing skilled-trade fluctuation (meetable by insurance linked to labour exchanges) from unskilled casual labour, and reporting experience adverse to land reclamation, women’s workrooms and labour colonies—irritated Labour members; Mr. Crooks expressed bitter disappointment.

Chapter III — From Easter to Whitsuntide

The Easter recess opened with Socialist and Independent Labour congresses that underlined the rift between strict Socialism and advanced Labour. At the I.L.P. Congress Messrs. Ramsay Macdonald, Keir Hardie, Snowden and Bruce Glasier withdrew from the party Council after insufficient support in the Grayson dispute, and issued a manifesto that Socialism could advance only by association with the Labour party; Grayson and his friends regarded the trade unions as tainted with middle-class Liberalism. The Socialist Congress rejected closer union with the I.L.P.; Hyndman denounced Free Trade agitation as a fraud. At the National Union of Teachers’ congress at Southport the Bishop of Manchester reaffirmed extreme Church claims while the President advocated a secular solution. Education difficulty and Roman Catholic defection reduced the Liberal majority at East Edinburgh (16 April) to little more than one-ninth of that of 1906.

The naval controversy resumed. Churchill, in a letter to his Dundee chairman published 15 April, protested against “four cardinal errors”: measuring strength in Dreadnoughts only; assuming modern fleets could be built in secrecy; treating the two-Power standard as mere numbers; and positing profound Anglo-German antagonism. Opponents held that these “errors” were shared by his leaders. Asquith at St. Andrew’s Hall, Glasgow (17 April), defended the Estimates: two new facts—the enormous twelve-month addition to German productive capacity and the acceleration of the German programme—gave no ground for alarm but required timely preparation. In spring 1912 Britain should possess sixteen Dreadnoughts apart from contingent ships, and forty pre-Dreadnoughts with 152 twelve-inch guns, against twenty German Dreadnoughts with forty eleven-inch guns; if the contingent ships were built, Britain would have twenty Dreadnoughts in 1912 against Germany’s declared intention of only thirteen complete. The sole remaining dispute was construction of the contingent Dreadnoughts; explicit assurances had been given that they would be built if necessary. Colonial offers of naval aid were cordially noted; Labour Exchanges, reduction of boy labour, and unemployment insurance were sketched as the next stage of social reform after old-age pensions and debt reduction.

Viscount Milner at Nottingham (19 April) put national defence in the forefront of Opposition policy—not for party purposes, he said, for Tariff Reform was progressing satisfactorily—and spoke of chronic, reasonable anxiety rather than panic. If the country continued to contract tremendous obligations without knowing how to discharge them, it must come to grief; the lessons of the Boer War had mainly been neglected; naval expenditure was not enough—national defence must be organised as a whole, and he strongly urged national military service. The Dominions were ready to co-operate; true Imperial principles were local self-dependence and mutual help. A new chapter of Imperial development was intimately connected with fiscal reform: had Argentina offered to build a battleship? At Worksop next day he said the last thing Government supporters desired was an appeal on the present fiscal system; he did not believe the movement against the Lords was serious—if it were, Liberals would be bound to propose an alternative, for a single-Chamber system would mean quinquennial dictatorships. He again advocated commercial treaties with the Colonies and hoped Mr. Chamberlain would live to see the victory of the principles for which he had in a sense laid down his life.

The few days before the Budget were filled by the Indian Councils Bill. On 19 April the Commons passed it through Committee under Mr. Hobhouse (Financial Secretary), the Under-Secretary for India being ill. The Government took a middle course between Unionist and Anglo-Indian Conservatism and the concessions demanded by friends of advanced Indian reformers. An amendment by Mr. Mackarness to prevent deportation or imprisonment without trial from disqualifying for Legislative Council membership was rejected, Hobhouse explaining that sedition would be punished rigorously but might be condoned for a fresh start, disqualifications being settled by the Indian Government with the Secretary of State responsible. Dr. Rutherford’s amendment precluding official majorities was refused as handing destinies to the Anglicised section of British Indians; Mohammedans were promised fully sufficient representation. Mr. Joynson Hicks’s attempt to limit ordinary members of the Madras and Bombay Councils to three was rejected 133–30. The clause struck out by the Lords empowering the Viceroy in Council to create new Executive Councils was re-inserted in amended form (distribution of business by the Lieutenant-Governor subject to the Governor-General; membership up to four) and passed 118–22 despite Earl Percy’s opposition. Third reading passed without division on 26 April. On 4 May the Lords re-inserted Clause 3 in a modified form: Bengal would have its Executive Council at once, but for any other province the Viceroy’s proclamation must lie on the table sixty days and could be estopped by an address of either House. The Commons accepted this compromise on 19 May amid Liberal protests that it retained for the Lords the power of disturbing Indian politics. Welsh Disestablishment remained on the programme as the session moved toward the Budget that was to dominate the rest of the year.

Chapter IV — From Whitsuntide to October 8

Imperial and naval display accompanied the Finance Bill’s advance. At the Imperial Press Conference the Earl of Crewe presided and the Prime Minister stressed identity of Imperial views, especially on common defence. On Saturday, 12 June, a great naval review of the Home and Atlantic Fleets was held at Spithead for the delegates: 144 warships, including seven Dreadnoughts, eight King Edwards, and four cruiser squadrons; guests visited the Dreadnought, watched a mimic destroyer attack, landing manoeuvres, and a fight at Whale Island. Naval alarmists criticised the review. That evening Lord Morley of Blackburn, speaking at Oxford, remarked on the scanty attention given to India at the Conference and noted with satisfaction the relief of tension after the Indian Councils Bill.

Meanwhile the Finance Bill passed its second reading after four days’ debate. Its rejection was moved on Monday, 7 June, by Mr. Austen Chamberlain, who indicated that the Opposition alternative was Tariff Reform and insisted that the Government proposals were collectively confiscatory: more money would be raised than needed; the burden was unfairly distributed, especially on Scotland and Ireland; businesses and investments connected with land were disturbed; income tax and super-tax would largely be levied on sinking funds rather than income; unearned increment was really deferred interest, taxed thrice—in the ground landlord’s hands, the leaseholder’s, and on death; there was no unit of valuation; the undeveloped-land duty would hit agriculture, spoil suburban amenities, and destroy mortgage margins. Let the country judge between this policy and fiscal reform.

Mr. Ure, the Lord Advocate—whom the Opposition regarded as the chief Ministerial advocate of the land taxes—replied that the Budget taxed superfluities and persons well able to pay; in ten years gross incomes reviewed for income tax had increased 30 per cent. while national expenditure rose only 15 per cent.; land was not created by its owner, was limited and essential, owed its value to the community, and could not evade taxation. The Budget was “a great and democratic Budget,” and the rich should take their share manfully. Mr. Younger feared destruction of the Scottish valuation system and consumption of raw spirit under the liquor duties; Mr. Bottomley thought undeveloped-land and ungotten-minerals taxes unworkable and stigmatised the liquor taxes as punishment for the Licensing Bill’s rejection. Mr. Pretyman described the Budget as aiming to leave as few landowners as possible the easier to prey upon, and blamed non-contributory old-age pensions. Mr. Harold Cox, entertaining the House, noted that in eight years expenditure had risen by £31,800,000, condemned reduction of the New Sinking Fund, called the land-value taxes political warfare, held the super-tax hardly worth while and designed to gratify vulgar envy of the rich, and declared that social reform as conceived by Lloyd-George and Churchill meant subsidising someone at someone else’s expense—weakening motives for creating new wealth and risking national ruin.

Outside Parliament, protests and leagues formed for and against the Bill; the contingent Dreadnoughts were among the naval questions pressed through the summer; and the Finance Bill’s committee stage on increment duty and the land taxes occupied the Commons as the session stretched toward the autumn confrontation with the Peers.

Chapter V — The Peers and the Finance Bill

October opened with further signs of conflict between the Houses. Dr. Cooper, Liberal member for Bermondsey, died after over-exertion at the Budget debates, and the seat was to be contested. On 12 October the Lords further amended the Irish Land Bill, restricting compulsory purchase so that it might operate only when the Judicial Commissioner and two Estates Commissioners were satisfied that land could not otherwise be obtained (106–31); third reading followed without division. The House Letting and Rating (Scotland) Bill was so altered—title changed to Small Dwelling-Houses in Burghs (Scotland) Letting and Rating Bill, limited to houses of £10–£20 yearly value by burgh population, compounding extension struck out—that Government withdrawal became certain. The Development Bill took its second reading on 14 October; Opposition amendments on Report (to abolish the £500,000 Consolidated Fund annual grant, to cap England/Scotland/Ireland shares at 80/11/9 per cent., to secure Commissioner independence, to limit the Road Board to five members, to strip power to build new roads) failed, and third reading followed.

Outside, the Edinburgh Review (Old Whig and Free Trade Unionist) strongly deprecated rejection or amendment of the Finance Bill. Independent Unionist peers made the case for rejection. Lord Curzon at Leeds (15 October) attacked Lloyd-George as a demagogue, noted some 250 amendments still pending, and insisted that the Lords were neither selfish nor eager for constitutional conflict. Privilege rested on each House’s resolutions, not on statute; the Lords had only waived, never abandoned, the right to amend a Finance Bill, and had never waived the right of rejection—whether for tacking (Gladstone in 1861 had admitted possible legitimacy), as mischievous, or to refer principles to the people. The Budget’s socialistic feature, he said, was that it was intended to “side-track” Tariff Reform; the Bill was being used for purposes for which Finance Bills were not designed. Viscount Milner likewise pressed the constitutional right of rejection.

Report stage of the Finance Bill began when the Commons reassembled on 18 October (after the London Elections Bill’s third reading, 161–29). Fears as to Liberal policy were already producing conspicuous withdrawals: Sir Alfred Pease had seceded in September; Lord Joicey, long a Liberal M.P. and created peer under Campbell-Bannerman in 1906, now followed so far as financial policy was concerned. An attempt by Mr. W. Rutherford to recommit the land clauses on the ground of 139 new Government amendments was rejected 180–80. A clause drawn by the Ecclesiastical Commission’s advisers, moved by Mr. Stuart-Wortley, to let certain ecclesiastical personages borrow increment duty from Queen Anne’s Bounty was rejected 196–74. Harold Cox’s motion to omit Clause 1 establishing increment duty was rejected 207–89 after two Liberals (Bertram of Hitchin and Ridsdale of Brighton) spoke against it—Ridsdale arguing that increment duty plus estate, settlement and succession duties might reach 47 per cent. of property value. Further amendments—postponing for a year the date from which increment should be calculated, exempting land purchased for development, protecting agricultural land or its purely agricultural value, exempting recreation grounds, limiting reversion duty to leases after 30 April 1909, postponing undeveloped-land duty until general valuation, exempting market gardens, and securing garden-city promoters the whole increment (Balfour ironically holding up garden-city companies, not the dukes, as “the real bloodsuckers”)—were likewise defeated, though the Chancellor made several concessions during the two-and-a-half-day land-clause debate.

Revised Budget arithmetic was then laid before the House: a grant to local authorities of one-half the proceeds of Land Value Duties; land-valuation expenses put at £250,000 less £50,000 originally estimated; Supplementary Estimates of £467,000 less £100,000 already in the Budget, producing successive total frames around £162,590,000, £164,440,000, £162,840,000, £162,102,000, £162,969,000 and £162,469,000, closing near £162,840,000. Proposed reduction of the Fixed Debt Charge from £25,000,000 to £24,500,000 accompanied a further £500,000 draught on the Sinking Fund, making £3,500,000 in all (against Cromer’s earlier suggestion of £4,000,000). Lloyd-George reported improved death-duty yield, better Post Office and stamp receipts from Stock Exchange activity, whisky consumption down by as much as 30 per cent. in some places, and £488,000 of contingencies absorbed by supplementaries; Austen Chamberlain offered moderate criticism and the debate was adjourned.

A slight diversion followed Señor Ferrer’s execution at Barcelona on 13 October. Some 8,000 or 10,000 persons in Trafalgar Square on 17 October heard violent language from Mr. O’Grady, M.P., Mr. Grayson, M.P., and Socialist speakers, some denouncing King Edward VII and Sir Edward Grey. Grey told the Commons that Britain could not interfere in foreign internal administration where British subjects or treaty rights were not involved; at the Sheffield Cutlers’ Feast (21 October) he denied that the Foreign Secretary was a “knight-errant,” while reaffirming duty where treaty obligations existed, as in Macedonia and the Congo. Lord Charles Beresford published correspondence (25 October) complaining that officers who had supported him in the naval inquiry had been placed on half-pay despite Asquith’s promise; Asquith repeated Admiralty explanations (1 November) and condemned Beresford’s action, while Beresford alleged espionage, favouritism and intimidation ruining naval morale—combated by Admiral Sir Percy Scott on 6 November.

Five more days (25–29 October) were occupied with Report stage. On 25 October the Chancellor moved a new clause requiring landowners to furnish particulars to the Commissioners; an attempt by Mr. Bertram (L., Hitchin) was defeated 139–55. The extract closes mid-Report, with the constitutional issue framed by Curzon and Milner—whether the Peers would exercise a right of rejection they insisted they had never waived—still the dominant question of the autumn.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Only one major Scottish Act of the year banned the sale or landing in the United Kingdom of fish from specified regions, checking foreign trawlers that had been destroying Moray Firth breeding grounds. Resentment at the whisky tax was offset, in Unionist eyes, by the wrecking of the House-Letting Bill and the Temperance (Scotland) Bill and by the earlier Lords fate of Scottish Land Bills. Campbeltown distilleries’ output fell 25 per cent., with a larger proportional drop in drunkenness arrests at Glasgow and elsewhere. Glasgow unemployment was severe: 19 per cent. of trade unionists out of work when the Scottish Secretary’s vote was taken on 8 July—worse than after the 1878 bank failure; the Commons declined to disapprove the Local Government Board’s withholding of the summer unemployed grant. The Hebrides were debated in both Houses; one parish paid rates of 25s. in the pound. At Vatersay the Government purchased the island from Lady Matheson and about sixty holdings were allotted by preliminary ballot. The Congested Districts Board report (May), for the year to 31 March, recorded 666 crofters settled or enlarged and 230 holdings purchased from the Board, with considerable arrears and difficulty keeping the best breeding stock. On 20 December Sir Robert Finlay, authorised by Balfour, announced Unionist Scottish land policy: peasant ownership, land banks, afforestation, and admission of pre-1886 leaseholders who had paid their own improvements to Crofters Act benefits. The Scottish Poor Law Report (1 November) had Majority and Minority schemes, mutatis mutandis, from the same signatories as the English report: in burghs of 7,000 and over, Parish Councils were to give way to Statutory Committees of Town Councils; rural Parish Councils would remain (smaller ones combined); a County Public Assistance Authority would run institutions; and the able-bodied out-relief disqualification—already generally evaded—would be abolished.

Ireland. The Registrar-General’s figures for 1908 showed a slight rise in marriage and birth rates and a slight fall in the death rate; illegitimacy was 2.5 per cent. overall (Ulster 3.4, Connaught 0.7); Ulster had the highest death rate at 18.1 per 1,000 against an average of 17.6–17.8. Emigration of natives from Irish ports in 1908 was 23,295—the lowest on record (5.3 per 1,000)—of whom 72.4 per cent. went to the United States, 12.4 per cent. to Great Britain, and 10.8 per cent. to Canada. The Estates Commissioners’ Blue-book (Cd. 4849, September) for 1908–9 and for the period 1 November 1903 to 31 March 1909 recorded 212,314 purchase agreements on 7,963 estates, cash payments proposed of £337,758, and advances applied for of £72,008,320. Agrarian disorder, cattle-driving, and the land-purchase backlog under the 1903 Act remained the standing background to the Irish Land Bill’s progress and to the Lords’ restrictive amendments of October.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1909

By Cuthbert Maughan

Internationally the year showed depression in the first half and recovery in the second across the leading agricultural and industrial countries; tariff effects were hard to isolate from other forces. The United Kingdom was “saturated with politics.” The claim that platform talk drove capital abroad was answered by the observation that foreign investment was a sign of wealth that encouraged British exports. The Budget of 1909 and its principles were said to foster a fashion for foreign securities; exports (after imports) swelled in the autumn, and by year-end most industries were active.

Cotton was the principal exception: the year began overstocked; raw material spiked (American middling more than 3d. a pound, Egyptian more than 5¼d.); in July English spinners went on short time, joined by the Continent and the United States, after overbuilding of mills in the prior boom. Shipping showed an analogous surplus of tonnage—built in booms, cheap materials in slumps. The Stock Exchange had a better year; South African mining was bright with lower Rand costs; diamonds, Rhodesian and some West African gold, and above all rubber shares, provided speculative outlets—fine Para rubber ending the year at 7s. 7½d. a pound after a range of 5s. 0½d. to 9s. 2d. Wheat advanced early then fell; English wheat closed 1s. 1d. higher after a temporary rise of 12s. 7d. a quarter; foreign wheat only slightly higher after +8s. 6d.; London household flour finally +1s. 6d. at 30s.; sugar rose on a poor beet crop. The outlook for 1910 was “distinctly encouraging.”

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. Senate elections on 3 January 1909 returned, of 103 seats, 60 Radicals and Radical Socialists, 27 Republicans of the Left, 9 Progressists, 5 Conservatives, and a gain of 2 Independent Socialists—leaving the Senate more Radical. Expectations of a Radical programme in the Bourgeois–Clemenceau lineage were disappointed; by year-end public-service inertia and indiscipline emboldened extreme parties (Maine-et-Loire teachers’ C.G.T. affiliation; Draveil defendants released under an amnesty pretext then used to denounce the Minister). The session opened 12 January with Brisson re-elected Chamber President 316 of 370 and Dubost Senate President with 229. The session was laborious and unfruitful: Morocco debates ended in the same confidence vote as since Algeciras; income-tax debate ran to 9 March as the Finance Minister leaned toward Socialist support and cabinet disorganisation began, the majority still submissive from fear of Combes. Naval debate brought Minister of Marine Alfred Picard into collision with Delcassé as President of the Commission. Clemenceau charged Delcassé with the humiliation of Algeciras; Delcassé’s prepared attack and Clemenceau’s dwelling on Morocco humiliation led the Chamber to refuse confidence by 212 to 176. The Cabinet fell after two years and nine months—an exceptionally long tenure. Expectation that Delcassé would form the next government was not realised in the portion of the chapter preserved in the extract.

Italy and the remaining foreign chapters (Germany and Austria-Hungary; Russia, Turkey and the minor Eastern states; the lesser states of Western and Northern Europe including Spain after Ferrer’s execution; southern Asia; the Far East; Africa; America; Australasia) are listed in the Register’s plan; only the France narrative is carried in the available extract text.
