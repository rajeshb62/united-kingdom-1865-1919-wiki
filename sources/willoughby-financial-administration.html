The System of Financial Administration of Great Britain (1917)

A technical report on the living machinery of British national finance — estimates, votes, Treasury sanction, Bank of England issues, audit, and the Consolidated Fund — written from interviews and documents collected in London in July–August 1914.

Editor’s Note (Frederick A. Cleveland, January 1917)

Cleveland recounts a ten-year plan for scientific handbooks on public business. In the spring of 1913 the plan was laid before the trustees of the Rockefeller Foundation. The studies proposed first were: (1) methods of budget-making and financial control; (2) civil-service standards; (3) pensions and retirement allowances; (4) public accounting. An appropriation was placed in the hands of a special committee of Jerome D. Greene, Charles D. Norton, Charles P. Neill, William F. Willoughby, and Raymond B. Fosdick.

Three university men were sent as an unofficial commission to England, France, and Germany. They worked from a prepared outline, personal interviews reduced to writing and submitted for correction, and documents collected on the ground. The commission began in England early in July 1914, with tentative plans for August and September in Paris and Berlin and supplementary inquiries in Belgium, Holland, and Switzerland. William F. Willoughby was to give special attention to English budget materials; Westel W. Willoughby to budget and civil service in France; Lindsay to civil service in England and to budget and civil service in Germany. They interviewed as a body in London, using the American Embassy.

The outbreak of war in August interrupted the continental work. Westel W. Willoughby had made progress in Paris; William F. Willoughby and Lindsay had gone as far as Holland. The members reassembled in England, completed the British collection, and returned to America. They obtained full data on British financial administration and fragmentary material on France, Germany, Belgium, Holland, and Switzerland for later studies.

The first draft of the present report was prepared by William F. Willoughby during his absence in Peking as constitutional adviser to the Chinese Government, after a conference of the three members and agreement on presentation and conclusions. The other two members later revised and all three signed. The text was offered to the newly organized Institute for Government Research, of which William F. Willoughby had been made executive head.

Cleveland notes that since the visit an interesting volume covering some of the same ground, with slightly different conclusions, had appeared: E. Hilton Young, M.P., The System of National Finance (Smith, Elder, 1915). “The point of view and popular treatment by a member of the British Parliament only gives added value to the more technical discussion of this report.”

Foreword (Frank J. Goodnow, Chairman, Institute for Government Research, Washington, D.C., January 1917)

The Institute was incorporated 10 March 1916 and began operations 1 October 1916 at 818 Connecticut Avenue, Washington. It is “an institution of citizens for cooperating with public officials in the scientific study of business methods with a view to promoting efficiency in government and advancing the science of administration.” The Rockefeller studies described in the editor’s note fitted that program. The completed report was accepted for publication as the first volume of “Studies in Administration.”

Introduction (A. Lawrence Lowell, Cambridge, Massachusetts, 20 December 1916)

Few subjects, Lowell writes, have attracted more American attention in recent years than financial administration; few need more careful overhauling; and no foreign system is better worth study than England’s. Direct imitation of a foreign institution is “usually disappointing, and sometimes mischievous”; study for suggestion is “wholly wise.” The authors describe British financial administration “in terms so clear as to be easily understood by anyone without technical knowledge, and with a singularly well chosen proportion between the different parts of the subject.”

They follow the course of financial action from departmental estimates through consolidation for Parliament, Commons procedure, subsequent orders on the Bank of England, expenditure, and accounting. “The casual reader may be surprised at the small amount of space devoted to the discussion of the appropriations in Parliament, but this is right, because, in fact, those debates relate to the political and not the financial aspects of the matter.” Lowell applies Bagehot: “One can say of the relation of the House of Commons to the financial administration what Bagehot said about the relation of good ministers to departments, that it is not their business to manage the finances, but to see that they are properly managed.” Unity of financial management depends on lodging authority and responsibility for appropriation and expenditure in one hand — the ministry in power. Taxation is legislative; expenditure is “essentially an executive or administrative function,” to be carried on under careful legislative supervision but not under the legislature’s immediate direction. After the law-making power has determined objects of spending, the executive must have “a free hand, unfettered by directions imposed for local, personal or political motives.” The book is chiefly concerned with the machinery inside the executive — “particularly the functions of the Treasury in framing the estimates and controlling the expenditure.”

Chapter I — Analysis of the Problem of Financial Administration of a Government

The commission finds no adequate existing analysis of the problem (H. C. Adams’s Science of Finance is praised but does not meet the report’s needs). Financial administration has an essential unity: estimates, appropriation, accounting, reporting, and audit are parts of one system. The chief merit of the British system, and the chief defect of the American, is the degree of that integration.

Operations are grouped on the revenue side (legislative determination of sources and loans; executive collection; accounts; independent audit; reports), the custody side (a treasury for receipt, custody, and issue), and the expenditure side (executive estimates of outlay, income, and treasury condition; submission so that surplus or deficit is visible; legislative modification of revenue and appropriations; machinery for incurring liabilities, adjudicating claims, disbursing, and documenting; books; reports; independent audit; auditor’s report to the legislature). These form an unbroken chain from estimates through grant, spend, record, audit, and report back to the next estimate. A year must not be isolated from past experience. The document that consolidates comparative needs, prior authorizations, and resources is “The Budget” — “a definite plan or proposal for financing the business of a future period both with respect to revenues and expenditures.”

The chapter reprints the recommendations of President Taft’s Commission on Economy and Efficiency (The Need for a National Budget): a presidential budget with message, summary financial statement, object classifications, comparative estimates, proposed changes in law; a Treasury book of estimates and consolidated financial report; departmental reports of expenditures by appropriation and by class of work.

The vital principle through the chain is responsibility, accountability, and control. Waste occurs not only from fraud but because working units seek the largest share of a lump sum they assume will be spent anyway. Control must be real at each stage: heads of units, department heads, the central finance officer (ministerial or with power to revise), the legislature, and (more for municipal than national budgets) public opinion. Budget procedure is constitutionally significant because it locates executive responsibility for plans and legislative responsibility for independent review; dissolution takes both policy and party to the electorate. After grant, control over allotment and over purchases (personnel and stores) remains. American appropriations bind by minute specification; British appropriations bind only on large heads and leave wide executive discretion, checked by a Treasury that is “almost… an agent or organ of Parliament for controlling the administrative services.” The commission devoted its chief attention to this control and found it “worked out with exceptional thoroughness.”

Chapter II — Some Fundamental Features of the English System of Financial Administration

The report covers custody, voting, and disbursement, not the raising of revenue. Until 1854 England had three financial years (estimates, appropriations, accounts). By 17 & 18 Vict. c. 94 s. 2 the fiscal year became 1 April–31 March for all purposes.

Public accounts may be kept on an accrual basis (France keeps years open until all accruals are collected and paid) or a cash basis (England and the United States close the books at year-end). The English and American systems give prompt treasury figures; they give less scientific cost information.

No expenditure may be made except under parliamentary authority — annual appropriation or permanent statute. Historically Commons first controlled the total the Crown might take in taxation, not the detail of spending; discretion in expenditure was a Crown prerogative. The modern principle that Parliament may determine purposes and manner of spending is complete, but “the most characteristic feature of the appropriation system of Great Britain is the extent to which discretion is still left to the Executive.” It stops short of minute specification because it believes the Crown is better placed to decide details.

Maitland is quoted: in old times “there was no national revenue; whatever money came to the king’s hand was his.” Medieval taxes were occasional. Early appropriation attached the proceeds of a tax to a purpose (1348, 1353). After 1665 and especially after 1688, grants were appropriated to particular purposes. Lord Welby’s memorandum to the 1902 Select Committee on National Expenditure traces annual navy and army votes after the Revolution, Somers’s appropriation clauses, a single navy vote until 1798, and large “extraordinaries” voted after the fact.

Two reforms made a general appropriation system possible: the Civil List (separation of the sovereign’s personal revenues from those of the state) and the Consolidated Fund. William III’s grant with a £700,000 ceiling first put a Civil List on the statute book. George III surrendered most hereditary revenues for £800,000 a year. George IV’s list distinguished personal use (£60,000) from government support. Since William IV the sovereign receives a salary covering personal and court expenses. The 1913–14 Finance Accounts show a Civil List of £470,000 (privy purse £110,000; household salaries £125,800; household expenses £193,000; works £20,000; royal bounty £13,200; unappropriated £8,000), a permanent charge on the Consolidated Fund like debt interest and judges’ salaries. Macaulay’s remark on the “whimsical perversion” of the name “Civil List” is quoted via Higgs.

The Commissioners of Public Accounts in 1785 condemned earmarking (seventy-four charges on Customs; militia on the land tax; hereditary annuities on the Post Office) and recommended “one Fund into which shall flow every stream of the public revenue and from whence shall issue the supply for every public service.” The Consolidated Fund Act of 1787 (27 Geo. III c. 13) created that fund; Irish revenues were similarly treated in 1816 and the two funds united. Appropriations in aid later take certain receipts out of the fund — “an unfortunate departure from a sound principle.”

The Consolidated Fund stands to the credit of the Exchequer — the public treasury — at the Bank of England and the Bank of Ireland (Scotch banks receive and remit in rotation). Thomas Gibson Bowles, before the 1902 committee, derives “Exchequer” from the checkered cloth and describes the three-lock chest (Teller, Clerk of the Pells, Auditor). In 1834 revenues were ordered paid into the two banks to the Exchequer account. The banks are “mere bankers of the Government”: they receive and pay to officers charged with settling obligations; they take the place of the old Teller.

No tax or expenditure is considered unless recommended from the Crown. Standing Orders 66 and 68 (early eighteenth century) bar petitions or motions for grants unless so recommended, and bar compounding Crown debts without official certificate. Members may not move to increase the Crown’s demand. The rare exception is an Address to the Crown asking a special grant (monument, reward for service), still voted in committee. This “at one stroke” prevents American-style pork-barrel initiation by individual members.

Permanent charges are “Consolidated Fund Services”; annually voted charges are “Supply Services.” Estimates, accompanying tables, and appropriation accounts cover only Supply. The 1913–14 Finance Accounts recap Consolidated Fund issues of about £37.3 million against total Exchequer issues of £239.8 million — roughly one-sixth. Items include the fixed debt charge (interest, terminable annuities, unfunded interest, management, New Sinking Fund), Road Improvement Fund, local taxation accounts, Civil List, annuities and pensions, salaries and allowances, courts of justice, miscellaneous. Harold Cox’s “Magnificent Muddle of Department Finance” is quoted at length: public-office annuities buried under miscellaneous while similar annuities appear on Office of Works estimates; Queen’s Colleges on both Consolidated Fund and Irish education votes; Irish land purchase and agricultural grants split from Land Commission and Agriculture votes; Indian Army Pension Deficiency treated as Consolidated Fund rather than army. The commission accepts the criticism: estimates understate total cost of particular services.

Supply, when voted, is still paid from the Consolidated Fund. The rest of the book follows Supply procedure.

Chapter III — The Estimates: Preparation and Submission

Every appropriation rests on a formal estimate — both a calculation of need and a formulated demand of the Executive on Parliament. Estimates are the act of the Ministry as a whole. Except Army and Navy (presented by those departments but only after Treasury approval), they are submitted by the Treasury. In disputes the Treasury decides, subject to rare Cabinet appeal on large policy (social reform, army or navy increase). Tradition supports the Treasury.

Army and Navy procedure: the Chancellor first settles an approximate total with the Secretary of State for War and the First Lord of the Admiralty and then with the Cabinet (navy policy turning chiefly on the new-construction programme). The department then draws detailed votes, each submitted to the Treasury with explanations of increases and decreases. New charges of importance must have prior Treasury sanction; the Treasury is often represented on originating committees. Form and classification of estimates are laid down by the Treasury.

About 1 October the Treasury issues an Estimates Circular to accounting officers of the civil services (the 1912–13 circular of 2 October 1911, signed C. Hobhouse, is printed in full). Forms must be returned; past-year figures checked; estimates due by 1 December, latest 13 January; if detail will be late, an approximate total by 15 December so the Chancellor can foresee the aggregate. Building expenditure statements to 30 November; pension statements likewise. Economy is imperative; supplementary estimates are to be avoided, but nothing likely to be necessary may be omitted. Even an estimate no larger than last year’s must be justified; “the habit of regarding each year’s estimate as the starting point for the next is one against which special vigilance is needed.” Vacancies are to be noted; explanations written on prescribed forms; multi-year special services need progress reports (spend to date, progress, likely completion and cost); stamp receipts estimated; half-year spend to 30 September given; special circumstances for the Vote on Account flagged. Regulations require statute citations, exclusion of unsanctioned charges, seriatim justification of each subhead, no inference of sanction from mere printing in the estimates, Treasury letter references, transfer of prior-year figures when charges move, gross charges and gross receipts, personal remuneration under “Salaries, etc.,” and notes of extra remuneration over £25.

Departmental estimates are largely a summation of items already approved by the Treasury during the year. Comparative and explanatory data are required. Preparation inside departments is described; Treasury examination follows; then transmission to the House of Commons. Supplementary estimates remain available when necessity arises after the main book is closed.

Chapter IV — The Estimates: Character and Form

Estimates are classified into Army, Navy, Civil Services, and Revenue Departments. The Treasury is treated as a civil service, not a revenue department. Civil Services fall into seven historical classes: (1) Public Works and Buildings; (2) Salaries and Expenses of Civil Departments; (3) Law and Justice; (4) Education, Science and Art; (5) Foreign and Colonial Services; (6) Non-effective and Miscellaneous; (7) Old-age Pensions, Labour Exchanges, Insurance, etc. Revenue Departments: Customs and Excise, Inland Revenue, Post Office.

Appropriations are by “votes” — 15 Army, 15 Navy, 120 Civil, 3 Revenue, 153 in all. The chapter lists every vote: Army from Pay through War Office and three non-effective votes; Navy from Wages through Admiralty Office and non-effective pay; Civil Class I (Royal Palaces, Osborne, parks, Houses of Parliament buildings, legal, art and science, diplomatic and consular, revenue, labour exchange, public buildings, surveys, harbours, Peterhead, rates on government property, Irish works and railways, Palace of Peace at The Hague); Class II (Lords, Commons, Treasury, Home, Foreign, Colonial, Privy Council, Board of Trade and satellites, Agriculture, Charity Commission, Government Chemist, Civil Service Commission, Exchequer and Audit, Friendly Societies, Local Government Board, Lunacy, Mint, National Debt Office, Record Office, Public Works Loan Commission, Registrar-General, Stationery, Woods and Forests, Works, Secret Service, and the Scottish and Irish departmental votes); Class III (law charges, courts, land registry, public trustee, county courts, police, prisons, reformatories, asylums, and the Scottish and Irish counterparts including RIC); Class IV (Board of Education, museums and galleries, universities, Scottish and Irish education); Class V (diplomatic and consular, colonial, telegraph subsidies and Pacific Cable, Persian loan); Class VI (superannuation, miscellaneous, Irish hospitals, temporary commissions, local loan fund repayments, Ireland Development Grant, exhibitions, Civil Contingencies Fund repayment); Class VII (old-age pensions, national health insurance commissions for the four countries, labour exchanges and unemployment insurance, National Insurance Audit, tuberculosis grants, Highlands medical service, friendly societies’ deficiency, Unemployed Workmen Act). Classification is by organization unit, one appropriation per branch, United Kingdom/England distinguished from Scotland and Ireland, effective from non-effective (pensions, retired pay). Quarters, buildings, stationery, and printing are centralized in Office of Works and Stationery Office; the C&AG’s appropriation-account report shows costs borne by other services so total cost of each service can be reconstructed.

Subheads are “subheads under which votes must be accounted for to Parliament.” Vote totals (except Army and Navy) cannot be exceeded without Parliament; subhead allotments may be transferred with Treasury sanction (virement). Army and Navy may transfer even between votes. Accounts and the C&AG’s report follow subheads and flag improper virement. T. Gibson Bowles’s 1902 paper attacking army/navy vote transfers is quoted: it defeats appropriation, encourages overestimate and a “huge pooled fund,” postpones surrender of balances, and obtains only formal late-session sanction. The commission answers with Porto Rican experience of the authors (transfers create incentive to save) versus Washington practice (year-end scramble to spend). Sir Edward Hamilton’s 1902 paper is quoted: without virement, departments would pad every vote. Safeguards are Treasury authority, visible accounts, report to Commons, and Public Accounts Committee review.

Supporting details and the order of presentation, with general summaries and analyses, complete the chapter’s account of the book of estimates as it reaches the House.

Chapter V — The Estimates: Appropriations in Aid

“Appropriations in aid” assign miscellaneous “extra receipts” of a service (fees, fines, sale of property, and, in a special case, War Office recoveries from Indian, colonial, and Egyptian governments of over £2½ million) as an aid to the formal grant from the Consolidated Fund. Extra receipts so treated do not pass through Exchequer receipt and issue accounts, though they are rigidly accounted for. Estimated extra receipts are the maximum usable; excess is surrendered as miscellaneous Consolidated Fund income. Where receipts exceed needs (the Mint), only enough is appropriated in aid to cover expenditure, plus a “token” or “nominal” vote of £10 or £100 so the estimate still comes before Parliament. The great revenue departments, exceptionally, use collections to pay expenses of collection, but bookkeeping treats the money as having passed through the Exchequer.

The theory treats each service as a financial entity whose income belongs primarily to it; Parliament and the public are thought to care about net charge on the taxpayer. The present form dates from Public Accounts Committee observations of 1881 and the Public Accounts and Charges Act 1891 (54 & 55 Vict. c. 24 s. 2). Applied first to Army and Navy, then gradually to civil votes (Exchequer and Audit 1883–84, diplomatic and consular 1885–86, thirty-nine votes in 1892–93). The 1894 PAC special report and Treasury minute of 15 December 1894 defend the system: it avoids overstating revenue and expenditure (Treasury in 1881 put the inflation at more than £4 million a year), gives departments credit and incentive to realize receipts, and creates uniformity. Gross expenditure and appropriations in aid both appear in the estimates and can be criticized; token votes preserve parliamentary review.

The commission is unconvinced. Higgs’s War Office example is “extreme and very special.” Net figures hide activity. Estimates, appropriations, and treasury accounts should cover all operations; net cost to taxpayers can be shown by classification without taking receipts off the Exchequer books. Contrast is drawn with the American system of reimbursable appropriations. Despite later tacit approval by the 1902 National Expenditure and 1912 Estimates committees, the commission treats appropriations in aid as a breach of Consolidated Fund unity.

Chapter VI — The Estimates: Action upon Them in Parliament

Financial power now resides wholly in the Commons. The Lords’ claim to amend money bills ended in the crisis over the Lloyd George Budget of 1909 and the Parliament Act 1911 (1 & 2 Geo. V c. 13). A Money Bill, certified by the Speaker after consulting two members of the Chairmen’s Panel, if sent up at least a month before the end of the session and not passed unamended within a month, is presented for Royal Assent without Lords’ consent. “Money Bill” covers taxation, charges on the Consolidated Fund or money provided by Parliament, supply, appropriation, receipt, custody, issue or audit of public money, and loans — not local rates. The Speaker’s certificate is conclusive and not questionable in any court. Enacting words under the Act name the Commons alone. The Crown’s veto has not been used for two hundred years and is idle given the rule that members may not move to increase estimates.

Standing Orders on supply and ways and means (especially S.O. 14–17, 66–71) are printed. Committees of Supply and Ways and Means are appointed at the beginning of the session (committee of the whole, not a select committee). Supply is first order on Thursdays. Not more than twenty days before 5 August are allotted to annual army, navy, and civil estimates including votes on account (supplementaries, votes of credit, and war or new-service estimates extra; up to three additional days by unamendable motion). On allotted days other business is barred before eleven; at ten on the last day but one the chairman puts remaining class totals; on the last day the Speaker puts outstanding reports. Two Fridays equal one sitting. The Speaker leaves the chair without question except on first going into army, navy, or civil supply or a vote of credit. No grant except on Crown recommendation; no money proceeding except in committee of the whole; Indian charges likewise; motions for aid or charge are adjourned to a future day and referred to committee of the whole.

Authorization of expenditure (“voting of supply”) is distinguished from appropriation of funds (“ways and means”). Committee of Supply approves votes by resolution; the House agrees; Committee of Ways and Means votes the funds; only then is a bill drafted, sent to the Lords, and assented. Money is not available until the appropriation bill is signed, except votes on account (credit for use after 1 April while the session continues). Excess votes regularize overspending after the fact; votes of credit cover war or unforeseen large needs.

The House in practice adopts estimates as submitted. Supply days are the opposition’s opportunity to debate policy and administration, not to recast figures; closure at twenty days forces remaining grants. Lowell is quoted: the Government no longer cares which grants are debated, “knowing very well that all these grants must be adopted under closure when the twenty days expire,” yet the procedure gives “the fullest opportunity for criticising the administration.” What is gained is publicity and located responsibility, feasible only because the Treasury exercises day-to-day control and the C&AG and Public Accounts Committee (opposition represented) audit after the fact.

The 1902–03 Select Committee on National Expenditure, barred from criticizing policy, found Supply discussion partisan, poorly informed, and more apt to urge increase than decrease. A grand-committee proposal was rejected as a delegation the House would not accept. The committee recommended an Estimates Committee analogous to Public Accounts but looking forward. In 1912 a Select Committee on Estimates was established — scrutiny of detail without sharing ministerial responsibility for the programme of work.

Chapter VII — Disbursement of Public Funds

Legislative control requires a guardian of issues. Under the old Exchequer the Auditor held one of three keys; in 1811 Lord Grenville as Auditor refused a £500,000 issue despite five Lords of the Treasury, for want of Crown authority during George III’s incapacity; in 1832 the Auditor hesitated over the Russo-Dutch loan until the law officers advised. In 1834 Clerk of the Pells and Auditor were abolished and a Comptroller General of the Receipt and Issue of the Exchequer created. The Exchequer and Audit Departments Act 1866 consolidated issue-control and audit in one officer: Comptroller General of the Receipt and Issue of Her Majesty’s Exchequer and Auditor General of Public Accounts — “Comptroller and Auditor General.”

Funds at the Banks of England and Ireland are subject to Parliament. The Treasury must first obtain “credits” on the Exchequer. For Consolidated Fund services two Lords of the Treasury requisition the C&AG, who if satisfied grants credit by warrant. For Supply a royal order must precede the requisition, because supply is granted to the Sovereign, who is under no legal duty to use it until he signifies that wish.

Credit does not move cash. Issues go only to “principal accountants” (those who receive issues directly from the Exchequer accounts): the Comptroller of the National Debt and the chief cashiers of the two banks (debt service); the accountants-general of the revenue departments (who spend collections on the spot, later adjusted through the Exchequer); and the Paymaster-General, general paymaster for military and civil services. Principal accountants requisition the Treasury, which orders the banks to transfer from credits to their accounts. The C&AG does not intervene at this second stage; he has already granted the credit.

The Paymaster-General is banker, not accounting officer: he pays orders drawn by departmental accounting officers, satisfied as to form and supporting documents. He keeps a separate book account for each civil vote and consolidated Army and Navy accounts, but one cash balance. He can pay any in-form order so long as the general balance suffices, adjusting requisitions later. Pay offices were consolidated 1830–56 to reduce idle balances. The 1856–57 Committee on Public Monies endorsed the single-balance system. A Treasury minute of 21 February 1885 (Chancellor answering W. H. Smith) describes the Paymaster’s sources: extra receipts as appropriations in aid; Treasury Chest Fund (expenditure abroad); Civil Contingencies Fund; departmental deposit funds; and daily Consolidated Fund issues. He exhausts outside cash before drawing the Fund, so daily Exchequer-issue returns do not equal expenditure except at prescribed adjustments — monthly, fairly accurate quarterly, and as close as possible at 31 March to the C&AG’s audited expenditure nine or ten months later.

Revenue departments (except Telegraph and Packet) pay collection expenses out of revenue before paying into the Consolidated Fund, then present statements; the Treasury issues corresponding amounts from the proper votes and the departments repay them as revenue.

Departmental “accounting officers” examine and settle claims and draw on the Paymaster. They are personally liable and are not bonded. Sub-accountants receive imprests from principal accountants or public moneys through other channels.

Chapter VIII — Treasury Control over Expenditures

Expenditure of appropriations is not mandatory. Maitland: money is granted to the queen and placed at her ministers’ disposal; statute does not say “You shall spend so much”; rather, “Here is money for this purpose and for that; spend it as you please; we trust the discretion of your advisers.” No one has a trust claim against the Treasury or a secretary of state under the Appropriation Act.

The Treasury is the organ through which the Crown decides how far to use the grant. Theoretically every expenditure needs Treasury sanction. Normal continuing spend is taken as sanctioned unless the Treasury orders retrenchment; any new clerk, new work, or departure requires express sanction even if already in the printed estimate. William Blain (clerk in charge of estimates) told the 1902 committee that passing an estimate does not authorize an increased establishment; the Audit Office will not pass the spend without a separate Treasury letter. Higgs: s. 27 of the 1866 Act gives the Treasury practical control over public expenditure; proposals for increased establishments, pay, services, or works need sanction or risk disallowance; the Treasury regulates Civil Service organization by Orders in Council and circulars. Control is over incurring liabilities — programme, plant, organization, personnel, equipment — not over individual vouchers. The Treasury is “in effect a general board of administrative control.”

Parliament allots lump sums for “Salaries and Wages”; organization tables in the estimates are information only. Establishments exist by past Treasury action until the Treasury authorizes change. Flexibility plus continuous review is contrasted with American statutory itemization of posts months in advance.

Quarters, equipment, and supplies are controlled through two organs described in the next chapters: the Stationery Office and the Office of Works.

The Treasury is technically a board. The Lord High Treasurer’s office was put in commission in 1714 (First Lord and junior Lords). Real authority passed to the Chancellor of the Exchequer. Lowell is quoted: “by a strange process of evolution the powers of the Lord High Treasurer have, by law, become vested in a board; and by a still later custom they are actually wielded by quite a different officer.” The First Lord is often Prime Minister and does not manage the office; junior Lords assist the Parliamentary (Patronage) Secretary, the government whip. The Chancellor is assisted administratively by a Permanent Secretary (administrative head) and a Permanent Financial Secretary (financial control), both remaining through changes of ministry. The financial secretary’s staff groups departments into seven divisions, each under a principal clerk who lives with those departments’ papers all year (Sir Robert Chalmers’s 1912 evidence: one division Army, Navy, and Colonial Office; another legal departments and Home Office; another Irish departments). Estimate clerks support them. Departmental and interdepartmental committees of investigation are used; the Treasury is a general organ of administrative control.

Chapter IX — Office of Works and Public Buildings

“Public works proper” (roads, river and harbour works, canals) are distinguished from “works of the Government” (buildings and plants for government use). England does so little of the former that the chapter ignores it. Military and naval works stay with War Office and Admiralty construction departments; after Cabinet settlement of the programme Parliament does not relocate posts — no American pork-barrel stations.

Civil works are concentrated in the Office of Works and Public Buildings: planning and construction of civil buildings in the United Kingdom and of diplomatic and consular buildings abroad; miscellaneous ceremonial and institutional works (South Kensington, Imperial College, London University); alteration, maintenance, and repair of those buildings, royal palaces, parks, ancient monuments, Osborne, Brompton Cemetery, British Museum property; renting of private buildings; fixtures, carpets, furniture; fuel, toilet stores, brooms, towels.

No building is authorized except on the demand of the using service, presented not to Parliament but to Works, which decides whether to proceed. The request appears in Works estimates, not the user’s, after Treasury scrutiny — a single works programme. Estimates show for each work and for the programme: original total estimate; revised total; probable spend to end of current year; amount voted this year; further amount to complete; amount asked for next year — so commitments, residual, and new charges are visible. Subheads separate new works/alterations/purchases; maintenance and repairs; furniture; fuel, light, water, household articles; caretakers; rents, tithes, insurance. Votes are by class of building (diplomatic and consular, revenue buildings, etc.). Works also presents estimates for surveys, Board of Trade harbours, Peterhead, rates on government property, Irish public works and railways — capital outlay in one place.

Transfers among items (not increasing a building’s authorized total or the works vote) may be made with Treasury sanction when one job lags and another can usefully absorb money. Construction, renting, maintenance, and supply procedure, and Works’ own powers to control user departments’ demands, complete the chapter.

Chapter X — Stationery Office

His Majesty’s Stationery Office supplies stationery and printing to all departments, military and naval as well as civil, at home and abroad. It originated in Burke’s Administrative Reform Act of 1782 as patents fell in and departments ceased separate purchase. By the date of the report it is “a great purchasing, contracting, distributing, and selling Department, having a turnover of £897,670.”

Departments submit no stationery estimates. A single vote (1914–15 net £1,069,272 after £168,000 appropriations in aid; gross £1,237,272) is accounted for under sixteen subheads: salaries; horses and carts; police; incidentals; printing, paper, parchment, binding, books and maps, and miscellaneous small stores for public departments; printing etc. for the two Houses; Stationery Office publications; gazettes; parliamentary debates; customs bills of entry; free-library parliamentary papers. Supporting tables break paper into printing paper, official correspondence paper, envelopes, note paper, account-book paper, telegraph envelopes, wrapping. A special table gives actual printing and paper spend for 134 organization units over three years.

Until recently the lump under each subhead was available to all services, removing incentive to save. The Controller recommended, and the 1911 Select Committee on Publications and Debates’ Reports adopted, allotment to departments on the basis of past spend, not to be exceeded without Treasury approval. Purchase, warehousing, and issue procedure and the Office’s powers to refuse or reshape departmental demands are described on the same control principles as Works.

Chapter XI — The Audit of Public Accounts

Lord Welby, before the 1902 committee: for a hundred and sixty years Commons laboured under “the illusion that it could control expenditure by putting checks upon the issue of money from the Exchequer instead of ascertaining how the money had been spent.” Tudor–Stuart Exchequer audit “limped leisurely” — in 1782 accounts twenty and thirty years old, some back to William III, were still open; audited accounts were declared before the Treasury, not Parliament. Pitt created a Board of Audit that was prompt and effective as far as it went but still reported to the Treasury. The 1834 Comptroller of the Exchequer still watched issues (advances), not expenditure.

Sir James Graham at the Admiralty in 1831 obtained an Act requiring an annual Navy Appropriation Account of audited spend under each head — the model of later appropriation accounts — but without forcing the House to judge it. War and Ordnance followed in 1846. The Public Monies Committee of 1856–57 “knocked on the head once for all” issue-watching as sufficient control and demanded independent officers to ascertain how money had been spent; concurrent audit extended to Treasury, revenue, and civil accounts; accounts to Parliament before the close of the succeeding year; Audit Board communicating direct, not through the Treasury; head of audit not second in rank to other permanent heads. A standing order of 1861 (Gladstone) created the Committee of Public Accounts; the Exchequer and Audit Departments Act 1866 (29 & 30 Vict. c. 39) created the independent auditor.

The C&AG is an officer of Parliament. Appointed by the Crown, he holds during good behaviour, removable only on address of both Houses; his salary is a Consolidated Fund charge — the same securities as judges. Yet the Treasury appoints his clerks, and Orders in Council regulate numbers and salaries; his internal regulations need Treasury concurrence. Accounting methods — how accounts are kept and rendered — are prescribed by the Treasury.

He audits appropriation accounts, may disallow items, and is expected to comment on unwise or extravagant expenditure, not merely legality. The Standing Committee of Public Accounts (Standing Order; Bowles dates the first order 1862) examines those accounts. Originally eleven members (later fifteen), quorum five; chairman by usage from the Opposition (departed from only twice; the conclusion chapter says only 1869 since 1866). Guided by the C&AG’s reports but not confined to them; C&AG and a principal Treasury officer always attend. Functions extend to wisdom, faithfulness, and economy, to receipts as well as issues, to the form and number of estimates (changes submitted to the Committee, as in 1867 and 1881), and to the Treasury’s own compliance with law. Bowles: it “does actually on the Appropriation Accounts what the House of Commons does theoretically on the Estimates, and does it to far more purpose.” The 1911 Epitome of the Reports from the Committees of Public Accounts 1857 to 1910 and of the Treasury Minutes thereon is the working code of accounting method.

Committee recommendations have no force until the Treasury answers in a minute, accepting, modifying, or refusing. Bowles wanted power to disallow and a guaranteed House occasion for the reports; the commission doubts Parliament would breach the principle that final executive responsibility rests in the Treasury.

Chapter XII — The System of Financial Reports

Parliament receives two sets of figures: Exchequer receipts and issues, and audited receipts and expenditure.

Finance Accounts of the United Kingdom (short title “Finance Accounts”) date from 1802 — until then, Welby says, the House had no regular information of how voted money was spent. From 1802 to 1866 it learned only issues (imprests), not departmental expenditure. The report remains a compact cash statement of Exchequer income and issues and of year-end resources and liabilities (1913–14: 101 small-octavo pages), rendered with great speed (1913–14 ordered printed 25 May; the principal table as a special return 27 April). That return is the basis of the Chancellor’s budget proposals.

The 1913–14 income-and-expenditure account (Sinking Fund Act 1875 s. 4) shows tax and non-tax income totalling £198,242,897 against Consolidated Fund and Supply issues totalling £197,492,969, surplus £749,928. Part II is the Exchequer balance sheet: opening balances at the Bank of England and Bank of Ireland; debt created (Treasury bills, terminable annuities under Telegraph, Telephone Transfer, Post Office London Railway, Land Registry Acts); ways-and-means advances; Cunard, Suez drawn shares, China indemnity, East Africa loan; issues for capital, bullion, debt redemption, old sinking funds; closing balances. John Bradbury signs for the Treasury; H. J. Gibson, C&AG, certifies. Only cash-convertible assets are listed — not Crown lands, buildings, ships, or stores. Supporting tables cover contingent liabilities, guaranteed loans, unrepaid colonial advances, and public-debt operations. A separate annual National Debt return gives dead-weight debt and sinking-fund history from 1875.

Appropriation Accounts are the C&AG’s audited statements of spend against votes, by subhead, with commentary on virement and irregularity — the material of the Public Accounts Committee, available many months after the Finance Accounts.

The Estimates themselves are a third great public document of proposed spend.

The chapter’s last heading is the lack of any general report of audited receipts and expenditures that would bring the two families of figures into one comprehensive surveyed statement.

Chapter XIII — The Budget

A budget is not a separate operation but the means of surveying both sides of the account together so that deficit or surplus is visible. Britain is “noted for its budget system,” yet the book reaches it last. Since the Revolution an annual statement “partaking of the nature of a budget” has been made; the phrase that the Chancellor “opens the budget” was in force soon after 1750. (A note derives “budget” from French bougette, a leather bag of papers.)

For 1914–15 three documents do the work: (1) Financial Statement 1914–1915: Statement of Revenues and Expenditures as Laid before the House by the Chancellor of the Exchequer when opening the Budget, May 4, 1914; (2) the Budget Speech of that day; (3) Financial Statement 1914–1915: Statement Explaining the Proposals Made by the Chancellor… — six pages of rate changes without argument, which the authors think might have been folded into the first paper. The first statement, seven pages, is “in effect the real budget.”

Tables reproduced include: 1913–14 estimated versus issued expenditure (Consolidated Fund £37.3 million issued; Supply £160.2 million; grand total issues £197.5 million; plus £4.4 million chargeable to capital — telegraphs, telephone transfer including National Telephone purchase, Post Office London Railway, Land Registry — total State responsibility £201.9 million); 1913–14 revenue versus budget and versus 1912–13 (taxes £163.0 million against £160.0 million estimated; non-tax £35.2 million; total £198.2 million); Exchequer balance sheet 1913–14; 1914–15 estimated expenditure versus the previous year’s estimate (Supply up £9.9 million, of which Navy +£5.2 million, Civil +£2.1 million, Post Office +£1.8 million; total £206.0 million plus £5.3 million capital); and the “final balance sheet” 1914–15 opening with Customs, Excise, estate duties (including proposed revision), stamps, land tax, and the rest of the tax and non-tax programme.

The chapter’s last heading is again the lack of any single general, comprehensive budgetary statement of the kind Chapter I and the Taft commission specified.

Chapter XIV — Conclusion

The inquiry was undertaken so that a system “generally held to be one of the most efficient that has been devised by any nation” might inform American national, state, and local reform. The chapter does not urge a particular American statute; it recites British features and their contrast with American practice, pointing to Taft-commission reports, Bureau of Municipal Research Municipal Research numbers, and Cleveland’s papers.

Ten principles are enumerated: (1) a budget considering all needs at once with balanced comparative statements; (2) executive preparation and submission of the plan; (3) parliamentary and especially opposition opportunity to examine and force answers in committee of the whole; (4) residual responsibility for the proposal and every material change remaining with the executive — Commons rules forbid motions to increase, and convention restrains material decrease; (5) no spend until Parliament approves, then rigid accounting; (6) parliamentary audit by a C&AG with judicial tenure and Consolidated Fund salary; (7) a Public Accounts Committee chaired from the opposition; (8) a Treasury not coordinate with spending departments but superior, acting as Parliament’s agent; (9) distinction of votes, subheads, and supporting details, with virement on subheads (and on army/navy votes) under Treasury and audit safeguards; (10) appropriations not mandatory — spend only on Treasury authorization.

Almost none of these, the authors say, obtain in the United States: no true budget; Congress as programme-framer using estimates as data; audit by an officer of the spending branch; moribund expenditure committees; morally obligatory appropriations spent to the last dollar; no vote/subhead flexibility; no day-to-day central controller. British executive dominance of the programme is tolerable only because Treasury, C&AG, and Public Accounts Committee stand above the spending departments. Reformers should distinguish formulation of a budget, action upon it, and organs of control — and press the first and third before asking legislatures to surrender determining voice over figures.
