The Annual Register 1907

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Education Echoes, Party Divisions, and the Irish Outlook

The acute political activity of the autumn session of 1906 was followed by a marked lull, broken at first only by the failing echoes of the controversy on the defunct Education Bill. The Primate, in a New Year’s letter to the clergy and laity of his diocese, pleaded for consideration of the subject without narrowness of mind, and reasserted the demand of Church people for elementary Christian teaching in all schools, coupled with recognition of the toil and expenditure undertaken to ensure an education “on the lines of corporate Christian life.” The Bishop of London formulated the aim of Churchmen as religious teaching everywhere, denominational schools where special circumstances justified them, and the safeguarding of parents’ rights. Both bishops stressed the financial burden of the prolonged controversy; the Bishops of London and Southwark issued a joint appeal for 50,000l. to repair and extend Church schools, while the Roman Catholic Archbishop of Westminster appealed (13 January) for 100,000l. for the schools of his diocese. The Bishop of Hereford regretted the Bill’s failure and outlined in The Times of 24 January a scheme of compromise providing undenominational schools within the reach of all children. The Archbishop of Canterbury, addressing the Canterbury Diocesan Education Society on 21 January, admitted that Voluntary Schools could not go on exactly as they were and that changes must accept popular control and freedom of teachers from denominational tests; Lord Crewe replied next day that it was useless to reopen communications except on the lines already discussed before the House of Lords.

Though Lord Hugh Cecil and other High Churchmen claimed the destruction of the Education Bill as a Church victory, many Unionists shared the Primate’s desire for a settlement on political grounds. Mr Leverton Harris declared that the Conservative party had “made a profound tactical blunder”; Mr Austen Chamberlain’s New Year’s Message to the Outlook urged a constructive Unionist policy of imperial union and social reform, with tariff reform as the means of providing funds “without robbery or jobbery.” Mr Joseph Chamberlain, through the Montreal Star (10 January), told the people of Canada that of all bonds uniting nations the bond of commerce was the strongest, and predicted the ultimate success of mutual preference. Tariff Reformers pressed to put preference first on the Unionist programme; the Daily Graphic suggested a fiscal truce and found little encouragement.

The Liberals were not allowed to forget their own divisions. Impressive Rectorial Addresses by Mr Haldane at Edinburgh (10 January) and Mr Asquith next day at Glasgow drew from Mr Keir Hardie, speaking at Darvel on 12 January, a remarkable attack: both Ministers belonged to the Liberal Imperialist section which had nearly wrecked Liberalism; of the three prominent Liberal Imperialists in Scotland, two were of the Birmingham type and had supported the war, while Lord Rosebery, “the best man of the three,” was the scapegoat. Haldane’s national ideals, Hardie said, were drawn from Japan and Germany; Britain was following the pathway that led Rome to ruin.

Meanwhile the plan for reorganisation of the Field Army, foreshadowed by Mr Haldane in July 1906, was announced at Glasgow on 11 January and embodied in a special Army Order published on 14 January. Its leading feature was abandonment of the continental model of Army Corps and substitution of the Japanese and Indian model of large divisions, each containing three infantry brigades with divisional cavalry and artillery. Organisation for war would be one cavalry division of four brigades, six divisions, army troops, and line-of-communication troops; peace organisation would be four cavalry brigades, six divisions, and army troops, with headquarters at Aldershot, Tidworth, London, Dublin, Edinburgh, and York. The Order was not to come into effect for some considerable time and attracted only slight attention.

It became increasingly evident that education and the tariff question would be much less prominent than Ireland and the modification of the Lords’ veto. Speaking at Newcastle on 15 January, Mr Bryce sketched and defended the Ministry’s Irish administration and argued that nothing but good could come from a large measure of local self-government enabling an Irish authority to deal with education and spend a certain sum in its own way. Those in Ireland who desired separation, he said, were an insignificant minority. Two days later, bidding farewell to his constituents, he referred to reform of the House of Lords, the plural vote, and a large extension of Irish self-government, hoping Nationalist leaders would accept something less than Home Rule. A new League was formed to defend the Union.

The final report of the Royal Commission on Trinity College, Dublin, issued 21 January, multiplied difficulties rather than clearing the way. The Commissioners agreed that Trinity was satisfactory for Protestant Episcopalians but not for Roman Catholics and could not be so altered as to satisfy the Catholic bishops; four of nine recommended admission of additional colleges to the University of Dublin, one approved in principle but desired postponement, and four disapproved. On 25 January Mr Bryce, on the point of exchanging the Chief Secretaryship for the ambassadorship at Washington, received Roman Catholic and Presbyterian deputations in Dublin and announced the Government’s own solution: a new college in the University of Dublin free from theological tests, abolition of the Royal University of Ireland, and a reconstituted University of Dublin comprising Trinity, the new college, and the Queen’s Colleges of Belfast and Cork. The scheme was regarded by Trinity men as subordinating their body to a new organisation unable to dissociate learning from politics or controversial theology; meetings of protest were held in Dublin, Oxford, Cambridge, and London; the Roman Catholic hierarchy showed little favour; and the scheme was practically stifled at birth.

Mr Birrell’s appointment as Chief Secretary was officially announced on 24 January; Mr McKenna succeeded him at the Board of Education, Mr Walter Runciman became Financial Secretary to the Treasury, Mr Charles Hobhouse Under-Secretary for India, and Dr T. J. Macnamara Secretary of the Local Government Board. Debate on the House of Lords, the referendum, and Ministerial attacks on the Upper House filled the remaining weeks before the session. Mr Winston Churchill spoke on the Colonial Conference; Mr Lloyd-George at Belfast; and a women’s suffrage demonstration in London advertised the growing agitation as the outlook for the session took shape.

Chapter II — Opening of the Session and the Attack on the Lords

Parliament was opened by the King in person, accompanied by the Queen, on Tuesday, 12 February. The Speech from the Throne recorded continued friendliness with foreign Powers; satisfaction that the Jamaica earthquake had been met by courage and self-control, with grateful recognition of American naval aid; the Amir’s visit to India; and, in India, a steadfast effort to widen the base of peace, order, and good government while guarding the strength and unity of executive power. The Estimates had been framed with economy consistent with efficient maintenance of the public service. On the constitutional difficulty the Speech was deliberately obscure: “Serious questions affecting the working of our Parliamentary system have arisen from unfortunate differences between the two Houses. My Ministers have this important subject under consideration with a view to a solution of the difficulty.” Prominence was given to licensing reform, clearer definition of the functions of the regular and auxiliary military forces, land valuation in Scotland, measures further associating the people of Ireland with the management of their domestic affairs, and reform of University education in Ireland. Bills were announced for a Court of Criminal Appeal, regulation of hours of labour in mines, amendment of the patent laws, valuation of property in England and Wales, enabling women to serve on local bodies, small holdings in England and Wales, and better housing of the people.

In the Lords the Address was moved by the Earl of Castletown and seconded by the Earl of Chichester. The Marquess of Lansdowne regretted the absence of reference to the Colonial Conference, defended the Lords’ action on the Plural Voting and Education Bills, and urged that the country apprehended far less danger from an unreformed House of Lords than from an uncontrolled House of Commons. The Marquess of Ripon replied that the Lords differed from the Commons only under a Liberal Government, and that the Government were considering how differences between the two Houses were to be solved.

In the Commons Mr Balfour doubted whether the promised legislation would all be carried through, ridiculed Ministerial talk of crushing the Lords, and declared that the House could not find something which was both Home Rule and not Home Rule. On Free Trade he professed himself unable to make out the Prime Minister’s real views. He charged the Government with want of tact and judgment over the New Hebrides Convention and with inconsistency on indentured labour. Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman answered point by point. The House of Lords, he said, had been called a watchdog, but this watchdog was sometimes somnolent and sometimes awoke to ferocious activity: when the Government was of a certain complexion the Lords abdicated their powers as a revising body; when the other side came into office they became aggressive. The difficulty “must be settled.” As for Irish legislation, he asked whether the House could really deny the Irish people the right to manage their own domestic affairs, provided they did not interfere with British affairs or impair the supremacy of Parliament. The Irish people should have what every self-governing Colony had—that was his “larger policy.” Mr John Redmond declared that his party regarded the Government as pledged to deal with the Irish problem on lines leading to complete self-government. Mr Keir Hardie complained that the Speech contained no reference to State insurance, old-age pensions, or the unemployed, and claimed that the aged and deserving poor had the first claim on a surplus understood to be between four and five millions. Mr Winston Churchill sketched the history of the New Hebrides Convention and defended the labour regulations as superior to the Chinese labour ordinance in the Transvaal; Mr Lyttelton and Sir Gilbert Parker replied that the Government had blundered into the Convention and given the Colonies the maximum of offence with the minimum of information.

The session rapidly filled with the Army Estimates and Mr Haldane’s explanation of his policy; the Deceased Wife’s Sister Bill (second reading in the Commons); South African controversies; bye-elections at Aberdeen and Brigg; Progressive defeat at the London County Council elections; introduction of the Territorial and Reserve Forces Bill; the Hague Conference and the Prime Minister’s scheme for “arrest of armaments”; Navy Estimates debates; a Women’s Franchise Bill; Welsh Disestablishment; the Education (Scotland) Bill; reform of parliamentary procedure and enlargement of the work of standing Committees; financing of social reforms; and the Canadian Pacific Mail Contract. The Unemployed Act, the Hexham election, and Lord Rosebery at the Liberal League carried the House to the Easter adjournment.

Chapter III — Territorial Army, Budget, and the Colonial Conference

The brief Easter recess diverted public attention to preparations for the Hague Conference and the Colonial Conference. The Independent Labour Party congress at Derby and the Social Democratic Federation congress at Carlisle condemned the Territorial and Reserve Forces Bill; the ILP, divided on women’s suffrage, compromised on universal adult suffrage with immediate extension to women on the same conditions as men. Mr Birrell, addressing the National Union of Teachers in Dublin on 4 April, made no reference to University education, whence it was inferred that no Bill would be introduced in 1907.

The Commons reassembled on 8 April. Next day the Territorial and Reserve Forces Bill came on for second reading. Earl Roberts, speaking at Birmingham on 4 April, had commended it as the greatest official step toward a National Army, but inadequate without compulsory training at least of the Swiss type. An elucidatory War Office memorandum of 8 April stated that the Government would adhere to the Cardwell lines of 1872, territorialising Army units and dividing service between Colours and Reserve. The chief lesson of the South African War, as summarised by the Elgin Commission, was that no military system would be satisfactory which did not contain “powers of expansion outside the limits of the regular forces of the Crown.” A second-line force must be formed on a non-professional basis for home defence; the Militia should be incorporated in it; a new Special Contingent under Part III of the Bill would replace the extinct Militia Reserve. Total estimated average normal cost of personnel and horses under the new scheme was put at 22,470,000l., against 22,200,000l. for Regular and Auxiliary Forces in the Estimates of 1907–8. Mr Wyndham moved an amendment regretting that the proposals, while destroying the Militia, discouraging the Yeomanry, and imposing new liabilities on the Volunteers, would not provide an adequate force for home defence or prompt support for the Regular Army. Sir Edward Grey defended the two-line organisation against the Opposition three-line plan; Mr Arnold-Forster declared that all Mr Haldane had done hitherto was to destroy, and that the expeditionary force of 160,000 men was a “spectral force.”

On 11 April Sir Edward Grey read a letter from Lord Cromer, British Resident in Egypt, tendering his resignation under medical advice after forty-nine years of public service. Grey, speaking with deep feeling, expressed the Government’s profound regret and announced that the appointment of Sir Eldon Gorst as successor had been approved by the King. Mr Balfour cordially endorsed the tribute. Procedure debates then established the scheme of Grand Committees; motions to reserve large categories of Bills for Committee of the whole House were defeated by large majorities.

Mr Asquith’s Budget statement and the Budget resolutions occupied the House amid parliamentary and press comment; later in the session the Finance Bill returned the House to the tea duty and the death duties as instruments of revenue for the social programme Ministers hoped to finance, including the old-age pensions increasingly demanded by Labour and the Trade Unions. The King’s visits to the Kings of Spain and Italy were widely interpreted as diplomatic demonstrations of the Entente system. The Colonial Conference of 1907 became the imperial centrepiece of the year. Premiers were welcomed; Sir Frederick Pollock’s Committee and resolutions from Australia and New Zealand prepared the ground; the Conference opened with welcomes from the King and the Prime Minister. Procedure, future meetings and a permanent secretariate, imperial defence (with addresses by Mr Haldane and Lord Tweedmouth), emigration, and an Imperial Court of Appeal were discussed. Entertainments to the Premiers and a great Tariff Reform demonstration at the Albert Hall accompanied the official sittings. Colonial Preference was firmly rejected by the Home Government; imperial coastwise trade, naval defence, and penny postage were also debated. Mr Balfour spoke at the Primrose League; the Prime Minister at Manchester. When the Conference closed, most Colonial Ministers left England between 18 and 21 May, disappointed at the rejection of Preference but holding that the Conference would promote the ultimate success of their aims and that its indirect results were of the highest value. Mr Winston Churchill at Edinburgh on 18 May declared that the Government had “banged, bolted, and barred the door against Imperial taxation of food.” Prince Fushimi’s visit and debates on the Congo and secondary education filled the Whitsuntide adjournment.

The Irish Councils Bill was introduced and received a mixed welcome that was soon to turn to rejection. Social reform schemes—eight hours for miners, temperance, shop hours, old-age pensions—advanced alongside the Territorial Bill, which was to be guillotined, and Lord Newton’s Bill for reform of the Lords. The Probation of Offenders Bill and further Colonial Conference debates on colonial defence completed the pre-Whitsun programme.

Chapter IV — Irish Councils Rejection, Land Bills, and the Close of the Session

Echoes of the Conference continued through the Whitsun recess. Among indirect results was the promise obtained by General Botha of an Imperial guarantee for a Transvaal loan of 5,000,000l., which excited Opposition apprehension of a “bargain” for Chinese repatriation—an assertion promptly contradicted in the House. Viscount Milner, speaking at York on 31 May, ascribed to the Government’s South African policy the “absolutely unrelieved gloom” of the Transvaal financial situation and strongly advocated Preference as a step to Imperial consolidation. Empire Day (24 May) was celebrated with increased energy, especially in the schools; a Federal Conference on Education met at Caxton Hall under the Earl of Crewe.

Political interest was speedily diverted nearer home. It became apparent that the Nationalist rank and file would have nothing to do with the Irish Councils Bill or with mere “instalments of Home Rule.” The Bill was violently denounced by the Dublin Corporation, the Sinn Fein party, Nationalist dissentients such as Mr Healy, and members of the Roman Catholic Episcopate. The Irish National Convention—the largest ever held in Ireland, numbering 3,000 delegates—met at the Mansion House, Dublin, on 21 May. Mr John Redmond moved a resolution declaring that nothing could satisfy Irish national aspirations but complete control of domestic affairs; that the Bill was utterly inadequate in its scope, unsatisfactory in its details, and deserving of rejection by the Irish nation; and that the Irish party should press for a native Parliament with a responsible executive over all purely Irish affairs. He advised the Convention to reject the Bill; the resolution was carried unanimously and endorsed next day by the United Irish League of Great Britain. The Bill was generally believed to be dead.

The Commons reassembled on 23 May. The Vaccination Bill, substituting a statutory declaration for the magistrate’s certificate of conscientious objection, was read a second time by 122 to 14. The Small Holdings and Allotments Bill for England, introduced on 27 May by Mr L. V. Harcourt, now seemed likely to succeed the moribund Irish Councils Bill as the chief measure of the session. The Government proposed to set up a class not of small owners but of occupying, cultivating tenants under public authority, with security of tenure but no dual ownership and no saleable tenant-right. Small holdings were defined as exceeding five but not exceeding fifty acres and limited to 50l. in annual value; County and Borough Councils would administer the Bill, or, in their default, Commissioners under the Board of Agriculture.

The Territorial and Reserve Forces Bill passed through Committee, Report, and Third Reading in the Commons and went to the Lords, where Earl Roberts again pressed the case for national service. The Finance Bill occupied the House on the tea duty and death duties. Irish University education and Irish land purchase were debated; the Jarrow election returned a Labour member; the Evicted Tenants Bill received its second reading and passed the Commons. The Land Values (Scotland) Bill advanced. Sir Edward Grey spoke on Macedonia. A Unionist vote of censure on the Government for rejecting Colonial Preference was debated; the Edalji case figured on the Home Office Vote; the Colne Valley election returned Mr Victor Grayson as an independent Socialist. The Qualification of Women Bill advanced; a pension to Earl Cromer was debated; the King inspected the Home Fleet and visited the German and Austrian Emperors. Foreign affairs, the Congo, and national finances occupied both Houses. The Belfast riots—the gravest in that city since 1886—grew out of waterside labour disputes and police disaffection; troops were brought from Dublin; the strike was settled on 15 August with the aid of Sir Antony Macdonnell and the Board of Trade. The Land Values (Scotland) Bill was rejected in the Lords; compromises were reached on the other land Bills; the Deceased Wife’s Sister Bill and the Transvaal Loan completed the legislative harvest. The session closed with the King’s Speech and a summary of a year dominated by Army reform, Irish disappointment, land legislation, and the imperial conference.

Chapter V — Anglo-Russian Agreement, Territorial Associations, and the Autumn Campaign

Throughout September, except in Ireland, there was a truce in political warfare. The Anglo-Russian Agreement, or set of Agreements, had been signed on 31 August; its full terms were not published until 24 September. It was received with disfavour mainly by advanced Liberals, who deplored the danger to the nascent political life of Persia and the prospect that Russian autocracy might be strengthened and emboldened to place a loan in London. The Agreement might provide a counterpoise to Germany by restoring the weight of Russia in the councils of Europe, but even that was held to be doubtful.

The Trade Union Congress at Bath (2–7 October), the most largely attended on record, demanded a miners’ legal eight hours’ day, reduction of hours in all trades, further legislation for the unemployed, compulsory State insurance, poor-law amendment, restriction of systematic overtime, and a non-contributory, non-discriminating old-age pension scheme. Resolutions advocated secular education, demanded abolition of the House of Lords, old-age pensions of 5s. per week for all persons over sixty, public works for the unemployed, and housing legislation, and condemned compulsory military training. The Northumberland Miners’ Union decided to join the Miners’ Federation, removing the chief opposition to the eight hours’ movement. A contributory old-age pension scheme put forward on 3 September by Lord Avebury and other authorities found little favour with the advocates of the reform. Mr Haldane, speaking at Ballomil, Abernethy, on 7 September, specified the aged poor as among the questions with which the Government must deal, though only by instalments.

At Blair Atholl Mr Haldane summarised the main features of his Army scheme: for Scotland a Lowland and a Highland division, the latter with three mountain batteries; transition on 31 March 1908; about 3,000,000l. to spend on the Territorial Army. Volunteers would engage for four years but might resign on short notice; they would be asked to train for fifteen days each year, with an eight-day camp for those who could not. The War Office published a model scheme for county associations, composed of a president (the Lord-Lieutenant), chairman, vice-chairman, secretary, and members appointed by the Army Council—at least half officers of the territorial forces, the rest on the recommendation of County and Borough Councils and any University. The association would raise and maintain the force, provide, clothe, and equip the men, but leave training to the general commanding, receiving a yearly grant from Army funds. By the end of October more than a dozen county associations were in process of formation. On 26 October the King entertained the Lords-Lieutenant at Buckingham Palace and addressed them on their duties in connection with the new force: the Act revived much of the importance formerly belonging to their office; ultimate success must depend on the goodwill and public spirit of the nation.

Unionist speeches of the autumn denounced the Government’s laxity in repressing cattle-driving in Ireland and predicted that Socialism would swallow up Liberalism. Many Unionists, especially thorough-going followers of Mr Chamberlain and (in a less degree) Lord Milner, advocated old-age pensions and other measures hitherto regarded as socialistic, proposing to get the funds from “the foreigner” through Tariff Reform rather than from the rich through heavier direct taxation. The fiscal controversy tore the party; the Tariff Reformers expressed profound dissatisfaction with Mr Balfour and with tolerance extended to Free Trade Unionists. Mr Jesse Collings at Birmingham described the party as going through the wilderness without a Moses. The new Australian tariff was bitterly resented in the manufacturing centres. The Liverpool (Kirkdale) election (27 September) returned a Unionist, Mr McArthur, over a Labour candidate who received Liberal and Irish support; the victory was ascribed largely to the ultra-Protestant character of Liverpool Unionism.

The Deceased Wife’s Sister Act continued to agitate the clergy. The Bishop of London said no clergyman should perform such a marriage or lend his church for one; Lord Halifax collected 8,700 clerical signatures assuring the Primate of support in vindicating the Church’s marriage law; the Primate condemned the Act but recommended advice rather than absolute prohibition. The Church Congress at Yarmouth (1–4 October) heard the Bishop of Norwich treat Disestablishment as possibly a gain to the Church though a loss to the State. Lord Rosebery at Glasgow intimated that the Select Committee on the House of Lords, of which he was chairman, would shortly report, and strongly supported the continuance of a Second Chamber.

The railway servants’ agitation threatened a general strike; Mr Lloyd-George, President of the Board of Trade, intervened with skill and courage, and conciliation boards were set up by all the railway companies and their servants, averting the strike. Women’s suffrage agitation disturbed Liberal meetings; Mr Lloyd-George commented on the suffragists’ policy. At the Guildhall banquet Sir John Fisher spoke on the Navy; the Prime Minister dealt with foreign affairs; Congo agitation continued; the Colonial Office was reorganised. The German Emperor paid a visit. The Unionist Conference at Birmingham carried a resolution on Tariff Reform; Mr Balfour’s speech was closely scrutinised by the Press; Mr Asquith commented; Lord Milner linked Tariff Reform with social reform; Earl Cromer defended Free Trade; the Duke of Devonshire and Lord George Hamilton wrote in the Free Trade interest; Mr Bonar Law spoke at the Hotel Cecil. In Ireland an outrage on Mr Blake, abortive trials for cattle-driving, and Mr Birrell’s dealings with the Irish Landowners’ Convention marked the autumn; cattle-driving abated toward the close of the year. Small holdings administration, medical inspection of children, licensing reform, the final decision on naval works at Rosyth, and Mr Lloyd-George’s intervention in the cotton trade dispute filled the remaining weeks. Mr Haldane reported progress on the Army scheme; Lord Curzon was chosen as an Irish representative peer; and the outlook for 1908 pointed to old-age pensions, education, licensing, and the unresolved conflict with the Lords.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Though Scottish issues formed so large a part of Parliamentary politics during 1907, the trend of popular feeling was still obscure at its close. Considerable opposition was manifested from the first by landlords and large farmers to the Small Landholders (Scotland) Bill, and fear that the Land Valuation Bill might lead to taxation of feu duties alarmed large classes of investors until the Prime Minister’s reply to a deputation on 28 June set the fear at rest. The autumn campaign against the Lords was opened by the Prime Minister at Edinburgh on 5 October and endorsed, according to the Scottish Liberal Association, by some seven hundred Liberal meetings. The Land Bill was strongly favoured in the Highlands; the attitude of Lowland large farmers was indicated by Lord Carrington’s refusal to attend the conference of the Scottish Chamber of Agriculture. Fear of Socialism found expression at the Glasgow municipal elections in November in a severe Socialist defeat. The prospect of Irish devolution occasioned the formation of an association with similar aims for Scotland; such hopes were partly satisfied by the institution of a Scottish Grand Committee. Among Unionists the fiscal schism, especially in Glasgow, was even more acute than in England.

The division of property between the Free Churches by Lord Elgin’s Commission was considerably advanced by a Memorandum of 26 July. Of the Aged and Infirm Ministers’ Fund and the Retired Professors’ Fund the Committee allotted 35,000l. to the Free Church; for support of ministers, itinerant preachers, and general administration the Free Church was to receive 250,000l.; from foreign and colonial mission funds 25,000l. Total funds were stated by the Scottish Secretary on 8 August as 1,618,000l. The “Wee Frees” received some 310,000l. besides costs of the great lawsuit of 1904 and other sums, so that their total receipts might amount to about 500,000l.; of churches, the Free Church received about 120, the United Free Church about 900, and 38 were allotted jointly. The Established Church contemplated modification of the terms of subscription to the Westminster Confession. A Scottish Education Bill, crowded out in 1906 and 1907, was promised for 1908. An excellent start was made in the organisation of the Territorial Army, to which was committed the chief share in the defence of the new naval base at Rosyth. The Prince and Princess of Wales were at Edinburgh on 10–11 July laying the foundation-stone of the new School of Art; the Premiers of Australia, Cape Colony, the Transvaal, and New Zealand received the freedom of Edinburgh on 10 May; the freedom of four towns was conferred on the Prime Minister. The year was the bicentenary of the Union, though there was no formal celebration.

Bad weather caused heavy losses—depreciation of crops during harvest alone estimated at 2,000,000l.—partly compensated by rent abatements. Industrially the year was one of great prosperity: coal excellent; steel and iron thriving for the first nine months until the American crisis gave a serious check; textile trades thriving. Output of tonnage on the Clyde was 619,919 tons against 598,841 tons—the highest figure previously attained—in 1906, including three large Allan liners, turbine steamers, cruisers, and a new turbine yacht for the King. The market was now overstocked, and the outlook for 1908 was relatively dark.

Ireland. Irish affairs again furnished British parties with abundant material for controversy. The abortive University project, the unfortunate Irish Councils Bill, the Evicted Tenants Act, the disorders, and the Belfast riots have been dealt with in the parliamentary chapters. Mr Birrell’s appointment was welcomed by the Nationalists; the sudden suspension of cattle-driving at the year’s close showed that a trying term of office had not yet exhausted his popularity.

Cattle-driving, begun in October 1906 and encouraged by about a dozen members of Parliament, was essentially a device to compel tenants of grazing land on the eleven months’ system to refrain from renewing tenancies, in the hope that owners might then sell under the Land Act for peasant proprietors. Economically it was disastrous: the grazing industry was prosperous; large tracts were specially suited for it; and the small holders themselves looked to the graziers to purchase their young stock. Along with it went boycotting. Though denounced by Mr Justice Ross from the bench on 17 May as “an audacious and widespread conspiracy amounting to highway robbery,” local magistrates dismissed cases, and juries usually refused to convict. Unionist demands for the Crimes Act of 1887 were unsuccessful. On 27 August six counties—Clare, Galway, Leitrim, Longford, King’s County, and Roscommon—were proclaimed as requiring additional police. Graver outrages occasionally recurred: cattle maiming near Athenry; moonlighter attacks; a bomb explosion at Lord Ashtown’s shooting lodge at Glenaheiry on 18 August. The Belfast riots, set up by labour disputes and police disaffection, required troops from Dublin; settlement was reached on 15 August. Within the Nationalist party breaches were almost healed by the end of the year: terms of union published on 24 December bound Nationalist members, pending Home Rule, to support every measure for Irish amelioration offered by any English party, and to support majority decisions under the party pledge. Early in the year 22,000l. had been collected in Australasia for the cause; in the summer three members seceded to Sinn Fein.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1907

By F. Harcourt Kitchin

The notable trade expansion which began toward the end of 1904 continued to gather force during 1905 and 1906 and did not reach its maximum till about the middle of 1907. Then a rather pronounced decline set in, and the year closed with signs of reaction very evident. The boom of 1905–6 had been much assisted by bountiful harvests nearly all over the world; the contraction of 1907 was due in the first instance to prospects of short harvests and the rapid advance in the price of wheat. As industrial activity fell off, America, the land of feverish expansion and speculation, got into trouble. A series of financial panics culminated in October, when the whole fabric of credit in the United States was shaken to its foundations. The storm burst on 22 October with a run on the Knickerbocker Trust; within four days several banks and trust companies had failed, Stock Exchanges had shut, and private firms had collapsed. On 26 October the New York Clearing-House Committee decided to issue clearing-house certificates. Gold was demanded in London; the Bank of England raised its rate to 5 per cent. on 31 October, to 6 per cent. on 4 November, and to the remarkable figure of 7 per cent. on 7 November—an advance of 3 per cent. in eight days. The Bank of France raised its rate to 4 per cent.; Belgium to 6 per cent.; the German Reichsbank had been at 7 per cent. since 29 October. The American drain of about 20,000,000l. caused the Bank of England’s reserve to drop to 17,694,000l. on 6 November, after which it recovered steadily. Cautious restriction of credit in the United Kingdom, Germany, and Canada checked the boom but saved those countries from the devastating collapse which was the fate of the United States.

A second adverse influence was the dearness of money; a third was the advance in the cost of coal. Grain and coal were almost the only commodities considerably higher at the end of December 1907 than a year before. Yet the decline in industrial activity had little effect on British foreign trade until quite the close of the year. Taken as a whole, the country’s export and import trade constituted a new record: the volume of trade, which had first reached 1,000,000,000l. sterling in 1906, increased in 1907 to 1,164,080,913l., an increase of 95,514,595l. or 8.9 per cent. Imports rose from 607,888,500l. to 645,904,176l. (6.3 per cent.); exports of United Kingdom produce and manufactures from 375,575,338l. to 426,204,596l. (13.5 per cent.); re-exports from 85,102,480l. to 91,972,141l. (8.1 per cent.). Exports of articles wholly or mainly manufactured advanced by 36,645,000l., or 12 per cent.; the greatest proportionate advance in exports (27 per cent.) was in raw materials, nearly the whole increase due to coal and coke.

The year was inevitably bad for Stock Exchange business. London dealt with one of the biggest liquidations of securities known in modern times and came through without a single failure of first-class importance, but depreciation was in many cases terrible. Home Railway stocks dropped heavily, partly owing to the labour difficulties averted by Mr Lloyd-George. Commodity prices moved almost continuously upwards for the first five months, reaching in May the highest average for twenty-five years; thereafter, except in foodstuffs, the course was steadily downwards. Copper ranged between 110l. 12s. and 55l. 10s. a ton; Straits tin from 200l. to 119l.; Cleveland pig iron from 62s. 6d. to 48s. 9d. In the iron and steel trades the year was one of disappointed hopes ending in depression; shipbuilding looked as if a very slack period was before it. The year marked the top of the trade cycle: a period of violent fluctuations in money, securities, and staple commodities, of exceptional interest to the observer but of great difficulty to most traders.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. At first sight the political situation in France seemed little altered in personnel or issues; yet there were interesting changes in party tactics, and events in the South and unrest in the ports reawakened, even among advanced Radical groups, the instincts of authority and national self-preservation. The law of 2 January regulating public worship was met by Catholic refusal to make the preliminary declaration of public meeting; proceedings were begun against clergy who said Mass without declaration; at Arras the cathedral was the scene of riot and bloodshed. The Budget was voted on 30 January. M. Caillaux announced that he had solved the problem of the income tax. In a Chamber debate on 30 January M. Clemenceau declared that the way the law of 1905 had been passed had “placed them in the midst of incoherence,” parodying MacMahon—“J’y suis, j’y reste”—whereupon M. Briand left the Ministerial bench, was brought back, and constrained the Ministry and Chamber toward diplomacy and conciliation rather than force in Church-State questions. A circular of 3 February dealt with conditions for letting rectory houses to parish priests. Papers seized at the residence of the Papal Nuncio, Mgr Montagnini, were partly retained for a Parliamentary Committee.

On 8 March the electric workers in Paris struck suddenly at 8 P.M.—an experimental mobilisation of revolutionary forces by the General Confederation of Labour. The Government threatened to place military engineers at the companies’ disposal; M. Clemenceau, answering M. Jaurès, declared that society had the right to live, and was endorsed by 378 to 68. On 12 March the explosion on the battleship Jéna in dock at Toulon brought the public face to face with the problem of order and discipline in the Navy; Committees of Inquiry were appointed in both Chambers. Dockers’ strikes at Nantes, disturbances encouraged by Confederation emissaries, and discontent with the weekly day of rest law filled the spring. M. Clemenceau refused teachers the right of combination under the law of 1884; a strike of provision trades and café waiters proved a fiasco; Confederation speakers were arrested at Paris and Nantes.

From mid-May to early June the South of France was convulsed by the revolt of the wine growers—an economic crisis caused by glut after three successive abundant vintages, aggravated by watering and sugaring of wines. Noisy multitudes traversed vast distances; a strike against taxes was decreed; at Narbonne troops used their weapons; at Agde and Béziers there were outbreaks among troops recruited from the distressed districts; at Perpignan the prefecture was burnt. The Chamber debated a Bill to check adulteration; mayors resigned; strong measures became unavoidable. M. Marcellin Albert, “the King of the Beggars,” visited M. Clemenceau on 23 June while a warrant was out for his arrest and was directed to go home and surrender. On 28–29 June the Chamber by 323 to 233 affirmed confidence that the Government would secure respect for law and restore order; the Senate voted the law on watering and sugaring of wines. General Hagron, Vice-President of the Higher Army Council and Commander-in-Chief designate, asked to be relieved of his command, citing disorganisation under the two years’ service law; General Metzinger followed; General de Lacroix was appointed to the prospective chief command. Little by little the mayors returned to their posts; departmental elections in July–August were a victory for Radicals and Radical Socialists over the Right and the Socialists.

Italy. Italian affairs in 1907 continued under the Giolittian system of broad parliamentary management, with labour questions and the maintenance of the Triple Alliance alongside cordial relations with France remaining the principal themes of the Register’s survey.

Chapter II — Germany and Austria-Hungary

Germany figured in the English narrative chiefly through naval and commercial rivalry, the German Emperor’s visit to the United Kingdom later in the year, and the King’s visit to the German and Austrian Emperors after inspecting the Home Fleet. The Anglo-Russian Agreement was discussed at home partly as a possible counterpoise to German weight in European councils. Austria-Hungary continued to share with Russia the management of Balkan reform questions under the Concert.

Chapter III — Russia, Turkey, and the Minor States of Eastern Europe

The outstanding diplomatic event of the year affecting Russia was the Anglo-Russian Agreement, or set of Agreements, signed on 31 August and published in full on 24 September. It regulated the two Powers’ relations in Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet. In Britain it was received with disfavour mainly by advanced Liberals, who feared for the nascent political life of Persia and foresaw that Russian autocracy might be strengthened and emboldened to place a loan in London; even the hope that it might restore Russian weight as a counterpoise to Germany was held to be doubtful. Turkish and Balkan questions, including Macedonia, continued to occupy Sir Edward Grey and the European Concert; a Foreign Affairs debate in the Commons and continuing Congo agitation kept extra-European human-rights questions before the House.

Chapter IV — Asia: China, Japan, India

In India the King’s Speech looked forward to a steadfast effort to widen the base of peace, order, and good government while carefully guarding the strength and unity of executive power; the Amir of Afghanistan’s visit to India was noted with satisfaction. Mr John Morley’s autumn speeches dealt with Indian administration. Japan maintained her position as Britain’s ally; Prince Fushimi’s visit to Britain was among the ceremonial events of the year. China continued the slow process of post-Boxer reconstruction and reform as treated in the Register’s Asiatic chapters.

Chapter V — The United States and Latin America

The United States experienced the severe financial panic of October 1907. The storm burst on 22 October with a run on the Knickerbocker Trust; within four days banks and trust companies failed, Stock Exchanges shut, and private firms collapsed. On 26 October the New York Clearing-House Committee issued clearing-house certificates. The collapse of credit, more severe in America than elsewhere, suspended the banking system for some months and transmitted a gold drain of about 20,000,000l. to London and the Continent, forcing the Bank of England rate up to 7 per cent. by 7 November, as detailed in the Finance chapter. American naval aid after the Jamaica earthquake was gratefully recognised in the King’s Speech at the opening of the session.

Chapter VI — British Colonies and Dependencies

The Colonial Conference of 1907 was the central imperial event of the year. Preference was rejected by the Liberal Government—Mr Churchill’s phrase that the door had been “banged, bolted, and barred against Imperial taxation of food” became the classic summary of Free Trade firmness. Defence, a permanent secretariate, emigration, judicial appeal, and postal communications (including penny postage) were discussed; Colonial Premiers generally valued the Conference’s indirect results despite disappointment on tariffs. The Transvaal, under General Botha, obtained an Imperial guarantee for a loan of 5,000,000l.; Viscount Milner and Unionist critics continued to assail Liberal South African policy and the grant of responsible government, ascribing to it the “absolutely unrelieved gloom” of Transvaal finance. Empire Day (24 May) was celebrated with increased energy, especially in the schools; a Federal Conference on Education met at Caxton Hall under the Earl of Crewe. The New Hebrides Convention with France excited Australian and New Zealand resentment over indentured (Kanaka) labour clauses, which Opposition speakers compared to the Chinese labour controversy of 1906. Newfoundland, South African land settlement, and Nigerian railway development figured in parliamentary colonial debates. The Colonial Office was reorganised later in the year.

Chapter VIII — Obituary of the Year

Among names prominent in the English narrative were Viscount Goschen, commemorated at the opening of the session, and Lord Cromer, who resigned the British Residency in Egypt under medical advice after forty-nine years of public service and was succeeded by Sir Eldon Gorst; a pension to Earl Cromer was debated later in the session. Lord Curzon of Kedleston was chosen as an Irish representative peer.

Chapter IX — Chronicle of Events

The year was dominated at home by Mr Haldane’s Territorial and Reserve Forces Act and the creation of county associations under the Lords-Lieutenant; by the Colonial Conference and the rejection of imperial preference; by the Irish Councils Bill and its rejection at the Dublin National Convention of 3,000 delegates; by the Small Holdings and Allotments Act for England, the Scottish land bills (Land Values rejected by the Lords), and the Evicted Tenants Act; by the Deceased Wife’s Sister Act and clerical resistance led by the Primate and Lord Halifax; by women’s suffrage agitation and the railway conciliation settlement achieved by Mr Lloyd-George; by Unionist fiscal strife at Birmingham and Free Trade defence from Lord Cromer and the Duke of Devonshire; and by the Anglo-Russian Agreement of 31 August. Abroad the French wine-growers’ revolt and the Jéna disaster, and the American financial panic of October, were the outstanding events recorded in the extracts. The trade cycle reached its peak at mid-year: British overseas trade set a new record of over 1,164,000,000l. even as reaction and American crisis closed the year in difficulty.
