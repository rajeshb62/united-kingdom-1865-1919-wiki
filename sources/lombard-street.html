Lombard Street: A Description of the Money Market (1873)

Walter Bagehot’s masterwork on the theory and practice of the English money market, the constitution of the Bank of England, the role of the banking reserve, and the principles of crisis management that became the basis of modern central banking.

Source

Bagehot, Walter. Lombard Street: A Description of the Money Market. London: Henry S. King & Co., 1873 (10th edition, Project Gutenberg etext).

Bagehot’s classic analysis of the English banking and credit system, written with the explicit aim of describing the concrete realities of the Money Market rather than abstract theory. The book is the foundation of the doctrine of the lender of last resort and remains one of the most influential works on banking ever written. Bagehot, the editor of The Economist, drew on his extensive observation of financial crises — particularly the panics of 1857 and 1866 — and on his intimate knowledge of the Bank of England’s operations and the London discount market.

Chapter I — Introductory

Bagehot announces his intention to describe the money market in concrete, plain language. “I venture to call this Essay ‘Lombard Street,’ and not the ‘Money Market,’ or any such phrase,” he writes, “because I wish to deal, and to show that I mean to deal, with concrete realities.” A notion prevails that the Money Market is “something so impalpable that it can only be spoken of in very abstract words,” but Bagehot insists it is “as concrete and real as anything else; that it can be described in as plain words; that it is the writer’s fault if what he says is not clear.”

He deliberately avoids the bitter partisan controversies over Peel’s Act of 1844. Half the supposed difficulty of the subject, he argues, arises from these abstract debates — he means to speak “as little as is fairly possible” of the Act, because “if you say anything about the Act of 1844, it is little matter what else you say, for few will attend to it. Most critics will seize on the passage as to the Act, either to attack it or defend it, as if it were the main point.” For Bagehot, the Act is “only a subordinate matter in the Money Market”; the quarter-century since 1844 has transformed Lombard Street into “a most vigorous adult world which then was small and weak,” and the Post-Peel agencies are the phenomena that need describing.

He introduces Lombard Street as “by far the greatest combination of economical power and economical delicacy that the world has ever seen.” The scale of London’s floating loan-fund is demonstrated through comparative deposit figures:

London’s unknown deposits — those in banks not publishing accounts — are proportionately even larger. Bagehot argues that concentration of money in banks, rather than its sheer quantity, is the decisive cause of England’s financial supremacy. “A million in the hands of a single banker is a great power; he can at once lend it where he will, and borrowers can come to him, because they know or believe that he has it. But the same sum scattered in tens and fifties through a whole nation is no power at all: no one knows where to find it or whom to ask for it.”

This concentration produces a constant stream of lending. “If any nation wants even to make a railway — especially at all a poor nation — it is sure to come to this country — to the country of banks — for the money.” English bankers advance on foreign stocks with “a margin” — finding eighty per cent of the money while the nominal lender finds the rest. In domestic enterprises, “we have entirely lost the idea that any undertaking likely to pay, and seen to be likely, can perish for want of money.”

Bagehot then analyses the democratic structure of English commerce made possible by cheap borrowed capital. A trader with £10,000 of his own can borrow £40,000 through discounts and compete with an established merchant prince trading on £50,000 of his own capital, underselling him because he needs only to pay interest (perhaps 5 per cent) on the borrowed portion. “If a merchant have 50,000 pounds all his own, to gain 10 per cent on it he must make 5,000 pounds a year, and must charge for his goods accordingly; but if another has only 10,000 pounds, and borrows 40,000 pounds by discounts (no extreme instance in our modern trade), he has the same capital of 50,000 pounds to use, and can sell much cheaper.”

This constant levelling promotes commercial energy and rapid adaptation. The new man “has his way to make in the world” and knows that changes of circumstance are his opportunities: “he is always on the look-out for them, and always heeds them when he finds them.” Bagehot deploys Darwinian language: “The rough and vulgar structure of English commerce is the secret of its life, for it contains ‘the propensity to variation,’ which, in the social as in the animal kingdom, is the principle of progress.”

But the democratic structure also undermines commercial morality. Great firms “with a reputation which they have received from the past, and which they wish to transmit to the future, cannot be guilty of small frauds. They live by a continuity of trade, which detected fraud would spoil. When we scrutinise the reason of the impaired reputation of English goods, we find it is the fault of new men with little money of their own, created by bank ‘discounts.’ These men want business at once, and they produce an inferior article to get it.”

Lombard Street is described as “a sort of standing broker between quiet saving districts of the country and the active employing districts.” Agricultural counties such as Somersetshire and Hampshire deposit surplus savings in London banks, where they are employed to discount the bills of industrial Yorkshire and Lancashire. “Lombard Street is thus a perpetual agent between the two great divisions of England, between the rapidly-growing districts, where almost any amount of money can be well and easily employed, and the stationary and the declining districts, where there is more money than can be used.”

This organisation gives England an enormous competitive advantage: capital flows to the most profitable trades “as surely and instantly where it is most wanted, and where there is most to be made of it, as water runs to find its level.” When the Suez Canal opened, continental nations predicted that Mediterranean ports would capture Eastern trade and “The Greeks, the Styrians, the Italians, the Dalmatians, and the Sicilians” would use the Canal. But “none of the nations named by Tocqueville had the capital, or a tithe of it, ready to build the large screw steamers which alone can use the Canal profitably.” Only England had the floating capital ready. The same principle of borrowed capital enables new traders to undersell established rich men “who depends on his own capital only,” as “a new man, with a small capital of his own and a large borrowed capital, can undersell a rich man who depends on his own capital only.”

Chapter II — A General View of Lombard Street

A structural survey of the money market: the Bank of England, the joint-stock banks, the private banks, and the bill brokers. The Bank of England holds the ultimate banking reserve of the country — the only large, concentrated stock of cash that can be drawn upon in a crisis. Bagehot explains the distinction between the Issue Department (which issues notes against gold and securities under the statutory limits of Peel’s Act) and the Banking Department (which conducts ordinary banking business with the note and coin reserve). The Banking Department’s reserve is the sum available to meet depositors’ demands, but it is also the only reserve on which the entire banking system relies.

This single-reserve system is inherently vulnerable: because all London banks keep their reserves with the Bank of England, a drain on the Banking Department’s reserve threatens the whole credit structure. The Bank of England is thus “not only a bank of issue, not only a bank of deposit, but the keeper of the ultimate cash reserve of the whole country.” Bagehot traces through the institutional anatomy: the great joint-stock banks, with their enormous branch networks and deposit bases; the older private banks of the City and West End, with their aristocratic clientele; the bill brokers, who act as intermediaries between the banks and the trading community, borrowing at call and re-lending on discount; and at the centre the Bank of England, whose reserve is the ultimate pivot of the entire market.

He examines why London developed this particular structure rather than the multiple-reserve systems of other countries. In Scotland, for instance, each bank kept its own reserve. In the United States, national banks were legally required to hold specified proportions of cash. But in England, the peculiar history of the Bank of England — its status as a government-chartered corporation, its gradual assumption of the central reserve — created a unitary system where “the ultimate cash reserve of the whole country rests with the Banking Department of the Bank of England.”

Chapter III — How Lombard Street Came to Exist, and Why It Assumed Its Present Form

A historical account of the evolution of English banking from its origins to the 1870s. The Bank of England was founded in 1694 as a corporation to lend money to the government, receiving a Royal Charter and certain privileges in return. Its original charter conferred privileges that gradually gave it a dominant position: it was the only joint-stock bank permitted in England until 1826, and within the London radius until 1833. This legal monopoly shaped the entire development of English banking.

Country banks developed piecemeal during the eighteenth century, often springing up in response to local trading needs — a factor, a brewer, a local merchant would open a banking business on the side. “At first,” Bagehot notes, “the business of banking was hardly a distinct trade. The goldsmiths were the first bankers; they combined the business of a banker with that of a goldsmith.” Even after the South Sea crisis and the gradual emergence of a more professional banking class, most county bankers were “tradesmen in other lines — drapers, brewers, or the like — who took deposits, issued notes, and discounted bills as an adjunct to their ordinary business.”

London private banks — firms such as Child’s, Hoare’s, Drummond’s, Coutts — evolved from the goldsmiths of the seventeenth century. They conducted a more sophisticated business, dealing with the aristocracy, holding government deposits, and acting as correspondents for the country banks. The bill brokers arose as intermediaries, originally as mere agents who brought borrowers and lenders together for a commission. Their evolution was crucial: the brokers gradually ceased to be simple agents and began to deal as principals — borrowing from the banks at call (repayable on demand or at very short notice) and re-lending the money by discounting commercial bills. This transformation concentrated the lending power of the whole country in Lombard Street.

The Bank of England’s position as keeper of the reserve was the result of historical accident as much as deliberate design. The joint-stock banks and private banks found it convenient to keep their balances with the Bank of England, and this practice ossified into a system. Once established, the system could not be undone: “the habitual relation of the Bank of England to the other banks” became “not so much a matter of law or of deliberate arrangement as the result of a growth which no one planned and no one could now reverse.”

Chapter IV — The Position of the Chancellor of the Exchequer in the Money Market

The government’s influence on the money market through the management of public deposits. The Exchequer accounts — the balances held by the government at the Bank of England — fluctuate enormously with the collection and disbursement of revenue. When taxes come in, large sums are withdrawn from circulation and deposited at the Bank, reducing the banking reserve; when the government pays out money (for dividends on the National Debt, salaries, or public works), these sums return to the money market and increase the reserve.

Bagehot describes the mechanics of these flows in detail. The collection of the income tax, the customs and excise duties, and the land tax all produce seasonal movements in the Exchequer balances. The quarterly payment of dividends on the National Debt — some £26 million per year — sweeps an enormous sum from the Exchequer into the hands of fundholders, who deposit it with banks, which re-lend it. The management of the National Debt — conversion operations, the issue of Exchequer bills, the timing of dividend payments — gives the Chancellor of the Exchequer “a great and growing influence” over the money market, though he exercises it “involuntarily” more often than by design.

Bagehot argues for greater publicity and predictability in these government operations so that the market can adjust. He notes that the Bank of England could mitigate the disturbance of government transactions by smoothing the ebb and flow of deposits, but that “the Bank does not attempt to estimate the probable duration of these fluctuations,” and the result is “a certain amount of avoidable disturbance.” He calls for the Chancellor to recognise that government finance and the money market are inseparable: “a Chancellor of the Exchequer who does not understand the Money Market can never thoroughly understand his own office.”

Chapter V — The Mode in Which the Value of Money Is Settled in Lombard Street

The determination of the rate of interest and discount. The open market rate — the rate at which bill brokers and other lenders discount commercial bills — fluctuates with the supply of loanable funds and the demand for credit. The Bank Rate (the minimum rate at which the Bank of England discounts bills) stands above the market rate in ordinary times, but the Bank’s rate becomes decisive when money is scarce. “In all common years,” Bagehot observes, “the Bank of England has little or no influence on the rate of interest. It simply follows the market. Its rate rises after the market rises and falls after it falls.”

But in times of stringency the Bank’s rate becomes dominant precisely because the Bank controls the ultimate reserve. When the open market rate rises above the Bank Rate, merchants and bill brokers can profit by discounting at the Bank and re-lending in the market, forcing the Bank to raise its own rate. The mechanism by which the Bank of England’s rate influences the whole money market has two stages: when the Bank raises its rate, it attracts gold from abroad (because higher interest rates draw foreign capital), which increases the reserve and checks the outflow of bullion; when it lowers the rate, the reverse happens and gold tends to leave. The Bank Rate thus operates as a controlling instrument over the foreign exchanges and the internal credit cycle.

Bagehot describes the process as a self-correcting one: “The rise in the rate of discount operates upon the trade of the country exactly as a rise in the price of any other commodity. It lessens the demand.” Higher rates discourage speculative importation, reduce the volume of bills drawn, and encourage export of goods to settle foreign balances — all of which correct the exchange rate and stop the drain of gold.

He also examines the concept of “the value of money” itself, distinguishing between the purchasing power of money in the commodity markets and the rate at which money can be borrowed in Lombard Street. The two are connected but distinct: a fall in the Bank Rate does not necessarily mean a fall in general prices, nor does a rise necessarily mean inflation. The rate on Lombard Street is “the price of loanable capital,” not the price of goods.

Chapter VI — Why Lombard Street Is Often Very Dull, and Sometimes Extremely Excited

The alternation of confidence and panic in the money market. In ordinary times the market functions quietly — bills are discounted, loans are made, credit circulates freely. “Lombard Street is usually as quiet as a plain English market town,” Bagehot remarks, “though in times of pressure it is like a city besieged.” But credit is inherently psychological: “A state of panic … is a sudden and unreasonable want of confidence.”

Periods of commercial speculation generate excessive optimism, which leads to over-trading, the acceptance of bad security, and the extension of credit beyond prudent limits. “The trade of a country,” Bagehot writes, “is not a fixed amount; it varies with the spirit of the times — with the hope of gain and the courage of speculators.” When some signal event — a prominent failure, a political shock, or a drain on the Bank’s reserve — punctures confidence, the market swings violently from excess of trust to excess of fear. Borrowers who were eagerly accommodated become pariahs; sound securities that were taken without question become unsaleable; the machinery of credit, which operates silently and efficiently in ordinary times, suddenly seizes up.

He describes the psychology of panic in vivid terms: “At the beginning of a panic we all believe that we can get £5,000,000 of notes when we need them, and at the end we are all afraid that we cannot get £500,000.” The tendency of borrowers and lenders to alternate between these extremes is “the governing condition of the money market,” and panics, though terrifying, are “as old and as periodic as the history of English commerce.”

Bagehot draws on the experience of the panics of 1825, 1837, 1847, 1857, and 1866. Each followed the same pattern: a period of speculation (in foreign loans, railway shares, or some new industry), a shock that broke confidence, a sudden contraction of credit, and finally, intervention by the Bank of England or the government to stem the panic. The consistency of the pattern is what makes a science of crisis management possible.

Chapter VII — More About the Mode in Which the Value of Money Is Settled in Lombard Street

A deeper analysis of the interaction between the Bank of England’s reserve, the rate of discount, and the foreign exchanges. Bagehot explains the mechanism of gold movements in detail: when England imports more than she exports, the balance must be settled in bullion, draining the Bank’s reserve. The Bank must then raise its rate to attract gold back from abroad — for “gold flows to the market where interest is highest.”

He distinguishes between two kinds of drain on the reserve: an internal drain (caused by a loss of confidence at home, when the public convert deposits into notes or specie) and an external drain (caused by an adverse balance of payments). “A panic is a period in which the two often happen together — in which a foreign drain of gold and a domestic distrust of credit combine to produce a common catastrophe.” The Bank’s rate must be raised high enough to attract foreign gold, but the real difficulty is that an internal drain may continue even after the exchange has turned favourable, because the public’s demand for cash is driven by fear rather than by economic calculation.

The India Council’s sales of bills in London are identified as a further factor affecting the exchanges and the reserve. The Government of India had to remit large sums to London to meet expenses in England — interest on the Indian debt, payments for stores, furlough allowances — and did so by drawing bills on India and selling them in London. These “India Council bills” were a large and fluctuating element in the foreign exchange market, and their sale or withholding could materially affect the supply of loanable funds in Lombard Street.

Bagehot emphasises that the Bank’s reserve must be adequate not only to meet domestic demands but also to withstand external drains. He argues that the Bank should maintain a reserve of at least £12 million to £14 million as a minimum — but that even this may be insufficient in a severe crisis. The Bank Rate is “the proper instrument for correcting both kinds of drain,” but it must be used promptly and decisively, not hesitatingly.

Chapter VIII — The Government of the Bank of England

A searching critique of the Bank’s constitution and management. The Court of Directors is composed largely of London merchants and bankers — “eminent in their own trades” but possessing no special training in central banking. The Governor and Deputy-Governor serve for two-year terms and typically ascend through a prescribed rotation — first Deputy-Governor, then Governor — ensuring that “no single individual acquires commanding experience or authority.” A man typically serves one term as Governor and then returns to private business; by the time he has learned the complexities of central banking, he relinquishes office.

Bagehot is scathing: “The Bank is managed on the principle of having no principles.” The Direction is amateur in character, slow to adapt to changing circumstances, and prone to vacillate between inaction and belated action. “It appears to me that the constitution of the Bank of England is radically bad,” he declares. “The real reason why the Bank of England has been so long managed as it has is that it has never been considered as a national bank — as a bank on which the credit of the country depends.”

The secrecy of the Bank’s proceedings — its refusal to publish detailed accounts, its habit of announcing decisions without explanation — contributes to the instability of the market. “If it were known that the Bank of England would act in the way that I have described, there would be a manifest diminution in the severity of panics. The knowledge that the Bank of England would lend freely in a panic would of itself inspire confidence. But as long as the market is left to guess at the Bank’s policy, panics will be more severe than they otherwise would be.”

The management of the reserve is a direct consequence of this defective constitution: the Bank lends timidly in a crisis (because the directors are afraid of the responsibility) and freely when no crisis exists (because they want to earn dividends). The two-year term of the Governor means that “the management of the Bank is in the hands of men who are always learning and never learned.”

Chapter IX — The Joint Stock Banks

The rise of the London joint-stock banks after the Bank Charter Act of 1833, which permitted joint-stock banking within the London radius. The Act renewed the Bank of England’s charter but removed the prohibition on other joint-stock banks operating within sixty-five miles of London. This opened the door to a new kind of banking institution.

Banks such as the London and Westminster Bank (founded 1834), the London Joint Stock Bank (1836), and the Union Bank of London (1839) grew rapidly. They attracted deposits by offering interest on current accounts — a practice the private banks had generally disdained — and by conducting aggressive lending operations. Their business model was simple: pay interest to attract deposits, lend the money at a higher rate, and keep only a small reserve. Bagehot notes that the London and Westminster Bank had deposits of £2.5 million in 1836, rising to over £14 million by 1857.

“The joint-stock banks early adopted the practice of keeping only a small reserve of notes and coin in their own tills,” Bagehot observes, and relied instead on two forms of liquid assets: money at call with the bill brokers, and deposits at the Bank of England. This made them dependent on the central reserve without bearing proportional responsibility for maintaining it. They competed fiercely with both the private banks and the Bank of England, undercutting rates and offering services that the older banks considered undignified.

Bagehot analyses the dangers of the deposit system in the joint-stock banks: the ease with which deposits can be withdrawn in a panic, the temptation to lend long and borrow short, and the moral hazard of relying on the Bank of England to provide liquidity in a crisis. “A joint-stock bank … may pay interest on deposits for years, and attract an immense mass of deposits, but the moment a panic begins, these deposits may vanish, and the bank may be ruined.” He includes detailed balance-sheet statistics from the London and Westminster Bank covering 1845–1857, showing the growth of deposits, the proportion of cash in hand, and the changing composition of assets.

He also discusses the competition for deposits that drove up interest rates and drove down credit standards. “The payment of interest on deposits naturally leads to a competition among banks to attract them, and this competition tends to lower the quality of the securities on which money is lent, because each bank is anxious to employ its deposits profitably.” The danger was that the system encouraged “overtrading” — lending more and more to less and less creditworthy borrowers — while the banks themselves held insufficient reserves against the withdrawal of deposits.

Chapter X — The Private Banks

The surviving private banks in Lombard Street and the West End: firms such as the Rothschilds, Barings, Glyn Mills, Coutts, Drummonds, Child’s, Hoare’s, and others. These were the historic names of English banking, most of them partnerships in which the partners were personally liable — unlimited liability — for all debts. Their history stretched back to the goldsmiths of the seventeenth century.

Unlike the joint-stock banks, the private banks tended to hold their own reserves more conservatively. “They keep a larger proportion of their deposits in actual cash than the joint-stock banks, because they have not the same power of attracting deposits by offering interest, and because their reputation depends on their known solidity.” They conducted business with a select clientele of wealthy individuals, aristocratic families, and the gentry. Their lending was more personal and more cautious.

Their relationship with the Bank of England was close but informal. The private banks had long maintained deposit accounts with the Bank of England, and many of them kept their entire cash reserve there. “They have no agreement with the Bank of England; they keep their money with it simply because it is convenient, and because it has always been the custom.”

Bagehot observes the gradual decline of private banking as the joint-stock banks absorbed an increasing share of deposits. “The private banks are gradually losing their business. The deposits which they used to receive from the country bankers are now largely taken by the joint-stock banks, and the wealthy people who used to keep their accounts with the private banks are beginning to find the joint-stock banks more convenient.” The concentration of banking into fewer, larger institutions was an unmistakable trend.

He also examines the special position of the Rothschilds, who as both bullion brokers and bankers occupied a unique place in the system. “The house of Rothschild is perhaps the greatest name in Lombard Street, but it is more a foreign than a domestic power; its main business lies in the great loans and the bullion trade of the world.” Barings, by contrast, was more purely a merchant bank, acting as accepting house for international trade and as financial agent for foreign governments.

Chapter XI — The Bill Brokers

The functions of the bill brokers as the intermediaries of the London money market. Firms such as Overend, Gurney & Co. (which failed spectacularly in 1866, an event that hangs over the entire book) acted as the great clearing-house of credit: they borrowed money at call from the banks, often at low rates of interest, and employed it to discount commercial bills at a higher rate, profiting from the spread.

The bill brokers thus linked the surplus cash of the banking system with the demand for credit from the trading community. “The bill-brokers stand between the bankers and the merchants; they borrow from the one and lend to the other.” The banks found it convenient to place surplus cash with the brokers at call, earning some interest on money that would otherwise lie idle; the merchants found the brokers more accessible and more flexible than the Bank of England.

Bagehot analyses the fragility of this system carefully. The brokers’ liabilities are payable on demand (or at very short notice — “at call” in the market’s phrase), but their assets — the discounted bills — are repayable only at maturity, which might be three months or more. In normal times this mismatch is manageable because the inflow of maturing bills roughly matches the outflow of call loans. But in a panic the banks call in their loans simultaneously, the brokers cannot realise their bills except at ruinous loss (by re-discounting at “panic prices”), and the whole discount market seizes up. At that point, only the Bank of England can intervene as the lender of last resort.

The evolution of the discount market from a mere agency business to one in which brokers dealt as principals is a central theme. “Originally the bill-broker was a mere agent; he brought the man who wanted to lend money and the man who wanted to borrow together, and charged a commission for his services. But gradually he became a principal; he borrowed from the lender on his own account, and lent to the borrower also on his own account.” This transformation concentrated risk in the brokers themselves, and the failure of a great broker like Overend, Gurney & Co. could bring down the whole market.

Bagehot provides extensive analysis of the Overend failure. The firm, once the largest discount house in the world, had expanded beyond its traditional business into speculative ventures — shipping, ironworks, and other industrial investments — and was brought down when these ventures failed. Its collapse on 10 May 1866 precipitated the most severe panic since 1825. The Bank of England was forced to lend enormous sums to keep the market afloat, while the government suspended the Bank Charter Act to permit additional note issues.

Chapter XII — The Principles Which Should Regulate the Amount of the Banking Reserve to Be Kept by the Bank of England

The core of Bagehot’s policy recommendations and the classic formulation of the lender-of-last-resort doctrine. This is the most famous chapter in the book, the one that established Bagehot’s name in the history of central banking. He sets out three principles:

1. Hold a much larger reserve than current practice. Bagehot argues that the existing standard — one-third of banking liabilities, as suggested by Governor Weguelin and Mr. Hankey — is inadequate. “No one would now ever be content if the banking reserve were near to one-fourth of its liabilities.” He analyses the composition of the Bank’s liabilities, distinguishing between those that are likely to be withdrawn in a panic and those that are stable. The reserve must be sufficient to meet the worst-case scenario: a simultaneous internal and external drain. He argues that the Bank should aim for a reserve “considerably larger than one-third” — perhaps as much as one-half, depending on circumstances — and that it should be built up by retaining profits and by refusing to force dividends.

2. Lend freely in a panic, but at a high rate of interest, on good security. This is the central principle. In a crisis, the Bank should advance money “without stint” to all solvent borrowers who offer acceptable collateral — but at a penal rate (Bagehot suggests Bank Rate plus a premium, perhaps one to three per cent above the pre-panic rate). The high rate deters speculative borrowing (ensuring that only those who genuinely need money will come to the Bank) and attracts gold from abroad (because foreign investors will send gold to earn the high interest). The free lending calms panic by assuring the market that “anyone who has good security can get the use of money.” Bagehot’s formulation is emphatic: “The only safe plan is to lend money freely, largely, and in the end successfully. A panic is a species of neuralgia in the money market; it must be treated by relieving the pressure, not by increasing it.”

3. Make its policy known in advance. The Bank should declare publicly that it will follow these principles, so that the market knows what to expect. “The knowledge that the Bank of England would lend freely in a panic would of itself inspire confidence.” Uncertainty about the Bank’s intentions compounds panic — borrowers do not know whether help will be available, and this uncertainty itself produces hoarding and contraction. A clear, publicly known policy would reduce both the frequency and the severity of panics.

Bagehot’s crucial distinction is between lending freely to the market at large and bailing out individual mismanaged institutions. “Every great panic brings some destruction,” he writes; it is not the Bank’s duty to save unsound firms. “It is the duty of the Bank of England to prevent the destruction of the whole credit system, not to rescue every individual firm that has been imprudent.” The lender of last resort is there to preserve the system, not to protect individual speculators from the consequences of their own folly.

He also addresses the objection that lending freely might lead to an over-issue of notes and a depreciation of the currency. The high rate of interest charged by the Bank is the safeguard: it ensures that the extra notes go only to those who genuinely need them and that they are withdrawn as soon as the panic subsides. The Bank’s lending should be short-term — discounts of bills with a few weeks to run, or advances against government securities — so that the money returns automatically when the bills mature.

Chapter XIII — Conclusion

A summing-up of the argument and a survey of possible reforms. Bagehot acknowledges the difficulty of reforming so deeply entrenched a system, but insists that palliatives are necessary because the system itself — the single-reserve system under which the entire banking structure rests on the Bank of England — cannot be fundamentally altered. “We cannot do otherwise,” he concludes. “The one-reserve system is fixed upon us. The only practical imitation of the American system would be to enact that the Banking Department of the Bank of England should always keep a fixed proportion — say one-third of its liabilities — in reserve.”

He rejects the French plan of government appointment of the Bank’s directors, which would politicise the institution. “The direct appointment of the Governor and Deputy-Governor of the Bank of England by the executive Government would not lessen our evils or help our difficulties. I fear it would make both worse.” And he rejects the American system of fixed reserve ratios for national banks as too rigid and likely to provoke panic: “if one-third were fixed by law, the moment the banks were close to one-third, alarm would begin, and would run like magic.”

The best available remedies are: the maintenance of adequate reserves (which must be higher than the one-third standard then in use), the timely publication of the Bank’s accounts (so the market can judge the state of the reserve for itself and is not left to guess at it), a clear and publicly stated lending policy (the lender-of-last-resort doctrine announced in advance), and the gradual improvement of the Bank’s internal governance (longer terms for the Governor, more professional management, and a recognition by the Court of Directors that the Bank is not an ordinary joint-stock bank but a national institution). “The Bank must learn to be a national bank, and to act as such; it must learn that it is not only a corporation that ought to provide profits for its proprietors, but a national institution that ought to keep the national credit.”

The book ends with the measured hope that “education and discussion will in the end so far improve the constitution of the Bank of England, that it will act, and will be seen to act, on a true and perfect system.” Bagehot does not expect sudden or radical change — the Bank’s “defects are too old, too inveterate, too much ingrained into the character of the institution” for that — but he believes that the power of reasoned argument, applied persistently, can bring gradual improvement.

The English proportion of 11.2 per cent — calculated by including only the Bank of England’s reserve against the combined deposits of the Bank and the London joint-stock banks — was the lowest of all four systems, though Bagehot notes that the English figure understates the true position by omitting the reserves held by provincial banks and the private banks. The comparison demonstrates the “overwhelming predominance” of English banking resources in absolute terms, but also the thinness of the English reserve relative to liabilities.

Note B — Extracts from evidence given by Alderman Salomons, chairman of the London and Westminster Bank, before the House of Commons Select Committee in 1858. This is the longest appendix note and contains detailed testimony on:

  • The lending practices of joint-stock banks during the panic of 1857. Salomons explains that the London and Westminster Bank did not withdraw accommodation from its own customers during the crisis; on the contrary, “our advances to our customers on the 31st of December were nearly 500,000 pounds higher than they were on the 1st of October.” The contraction was achieved by allowing bills discounted for brokers and strangers to mature without replacement.
  • The distinction between discounting bills and making advances on bills deposited as security. Salomons explains that when the bank lends money on bills as security, it is not a re-discount and carries no further liability; when it discounts a bill, the bank’s liability continues until maturity. This distinction was important because the joint-stock banks’ practice of lending on deposited bills rather than discounting them allowed the banks to control their exposure more precisely.
  • The banks’ confidence that the Government would always intervene. Salomons states bluntly: “If the Bank of England had stopped payment, all the machinery of Government would have stopped with it, and we never could have believed that so formidable a calamity would have arisen if the Government could have prevented it.” He and his fellow bankers “never for a moment” contemplated that the Bank of England might fail, and expected that the Government would issue a letter suspending the Bank Act — which it did.
  • The competition among banks for deposits and the understanding reached after 1857 to moderate the payment of interest on deposits. Salomons confirms that “all the banks have come to an understanding that it is not desirable, either for their proprietors or for the public, to follow closely at all times the alterations of the Bank.”
  • A statistical table showing the deposits, cash in hand, money at call, and government securities of the London and Westminster Bank at half-yearly intervals from 1845 to 1857. The table shows the bank’s deposits growing from £2.5 million in 1845 to over £14 million by 1857, while cash in hand as a proportion of deposits fluctuated but generally declined.

Note C — Statement of circulation and deposits of the Bank of Dundee at ten-year intervals from 1764 to 1864. This is a remarkable long-run series showing the growth of deposits in a provincial Scottish bank from nothing (the Bank of Dundee had no deposits at all in 1764) to nearly £685,000 by 1864. The note illustrates the long-term growth of banking intermediation in Scotland, where banking had developed earlier and more thoroughly than in most of England. Bagehot uses it to demonstrate that deposits are not a fixed quantity but can be built up over time as the public becomes accustomed to using banks.

Note D — The full proceedings of the half-yearly meeting of the proprietors of the Bank of England held on 13 September 1866, reprinted from The Economist. Governor Launcelot Holland’s address describes the Bank’s actions during the panic of 1866, triggered by the failure of Overend, Gurney & Co. on 10 May. The Governor reported that the Bank had made “extraordinary advances” totalling some £45,000,000 in the three months from May to July, including £20,000,000 in the first fortnight of the panic. The meeting records the debate among proprietors over:

  • The suspension of the Bank Act of 1844, which temporarily permitted the Bank to issue notes beyond the statutory limit
  • The Bank’s decision to pay interest on deposits to attract funds during the crisis
  • The propriety of the Bank’s lending policy and the conduct of the directors
  • The governance of the Bank and the question of whether the proprietors should have more control over the direction

Governor Holland defended the Bank’s actions vigorously, arguing that it had done “all that was possible” to meet the crisis and that the suspension of the Bank Act had been “absolutely necessary.” The debate among the proprietors — some of whom criticised the Bank for having lent too freely, others for not having lent freely enough — gives a vivid contemporary picture of the controversies that Bagehot’s book sought to resolve.

Also included is a summary of the financial results for the half-year, showing the Bank’s profits, the dividend declared (which, despite the extraordinary advances, was maintained), and the state of the reserve at various dates during the crisis.
