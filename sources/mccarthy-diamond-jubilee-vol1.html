Contents

Preface — The author states that these two concluding volumes (the Sixth and Seventh) cover the period from the Diamond Jubilee (1897) to the Accession of Edward VII. They aim not merely to record events and dates but to survey life and social progress across the whole span of Victoria’s reign, with retrospect chapters on politics, literature, art, science, and social change.

Chapter I — Colonial Federation — South African Troubles

The Diamond Jubilee of 1897 gives occasion for a review of colonial relations. The Colonial Premiers, Colonial troops, and representatives from Asian and African dependencies figured prominently in the Jubilee procession. McCarthy traces the development of Canadian federation as a model, then turns to the South Africa Committee investigation into the Jameson Raid. The telegrams between Rhodes, Jameson, and Chamberlain’s Colonial Office are examined — including the testimony of Flora Shaw (colonial correspondent of the Times) and the famous “sealed instructions” telegram. The chapter also covers the parliamentary formal censure of Rhodes and the debate on colonial federation schemes.

Chapter II — Greece, But Living Greece Once More

The Cretan revolt against Ottoman rule and the intervention of the European Powers. McCarthy recounts the story of the Greek national movement for the rescue of Crete, the landing of Greek troops, and the outbreak of the Greco-Turkish War of 1897. The European concert (England, France, Russia, Italy, Austria, Germany) attempted to maintain order but was divided in its sympathies. The chapter covers the Greek defeat, the armistice, the Treaty of Constantinople, and the establishment of an autonomous Cretan state under Prince George of Greece.

Chapter III — Employer and Workman

The condition of the working classes in late-Victorian Britain. McCarthy surveys the continuous efforts toward improvement during the later years of the reign: factory legislation, the employer’s liability question, workmen’s compensation, the eight-hours movement, the efforts of individual philanthropists, trade-union activity, and the growing recognition of labour’s claims in Parliament. The chapter discusses the conciliation movement in industrial disputes and the emergence of a more organised labour representation in politics.

Chapter IV — The Death of Gladstone

Ascension Day, 19 May 1898 — the death of William Ewart Gladstone at Hawarden. McCarthy describes the scenes at Hawarden, the Queen’s message, the lying-in-state in Westminster Hall, and the funeral in Westminster Abbey. A full biographical retrospect of Gladstone’s career is woven into the narrative: his early Toryism, his conversion to Liberalism, his great administrations, the Home Rule schism, and his final retirement. The chapter dwells on his character — his devotion to his wife, his religious intensity, his physical vigour even in old age, his love of books and Homeric scholarship, and his moral authority over the nation.

Chapter V — “Another Daybreak in the East”

Eastern affairs in the aftermath of the Greco-Turkish War. The title is taken from Shakespeare. McCarthy examines the condition of the Ottoman Empire, the Armenian question, the continuing unrest in Crete and Macedonia, the ambitions of the European Powers in the Levant, and the complications arising from the decline of Turkish authority in Europe. The chapter also touches on the relations between the Powers and the Sultan, and the growing influence of Germany in the Near East.

Chapter VI — The Far East

China and the Far Eastern question. The Dowager-Empress Tzu-hsi and her system of personal rule. McCarthy discusses the scramble for Chinese concessions — the Russian lease of Port Arthur, the German seizure of Kiaochow, the British lease of Weihaiwei, and the Japanese emergence as a Pacific power. The chapter covers the “Hundred Days of Reform” under the Kuang-hsü Emperor, the conservative reaction led by the Dowager-Empress, the Boxer movement in its early stages, and the growing tension between the European powers and China.

Chapter VII — “How We Have Performed Our Roman Rites”

The Ritualist controversy in the Church of England. McCarthy examines the conflict between the High Church (Ritualist) party and the Low Church and Evangelical parties over liturgical practices — the use of incense, vestments, altar lights, processions, and the eastward position. The chapter covers episcopal charges, the Public Worship Regulation Act, the prosecutions of Ritualist clergy, the imprisonment of some clergymen for contempt of court, and the broader question of religious liberty within the established church.

Chapter VIII — “Here’s a Woman Would Speak”

The Women’s Rights movement in the closing years of Victoria’s reign. McCarthy surveys the agitation for the enlargement of women’s sphere: the struggle for admission to the universities and the medical profession, the Married Women’s Property Acts, the movement for women’s suffrage, the work of women in local government and the poor law boards, and the changing social position of women. The chapter also covers the Queen’s own views on women’s questions, and the emergence of the “New Woman” in literature and society.

Chapter IX — The Old World and the New

The Spanish-American War of 1898. McCarthy describes the outbreak of the conflict between Spain and the United States, beginning with the Cuban insurrection and the destruction of the USS Maine in Havana harbour. The chapter covers the American declaration of war, Dewey’s victory at Manila Bay, the land campaign in Cuba including the charge of the Rough Riders at San Juan Hill, the destruction of Cervera’s fleet off Santiago, and the Spanish surrender. The Treaty of Paris — the cession of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States, and Cuban independence — is discussed. McCarthy reflects on the emergence of the United States as a world power and the implications for European diplomacy.

Chapter X — Some Deaths in 1897

Obituaries of notable figures who died in the year of the Diamond Jubilee. McCarthy devotes particular attention to Sir John Everett Millais (the painter), the Duke of Devonshire (the seventh Duke, formerly the Marquess of Hartington), Professor J. R. Seeley (author of The Expansion of England), and other figures from politics, literature, and the arts. The chapter is both a record of individual lives and a survey of the generation that was passing away.

Chapter XI — The Death-Roll of 1898

The death of Prince Bismarck receives first place. McCarthy describes the profound impression made on the English people by the passing of the Iron Chancellor, and provides a biographical sketch of his career from the unification of Germany to his dismissal by William II and his retirement at Friedrichsruh. Other notable deaths recorded include Sir William Harcourt (the statesman, though his death actually occurred later), Professor Huxley, Lord Acton, and leading figures in literature and science. McCarthy uses the chapter to reflect on the changing of the European political guard.

Chapter XII — Peace Claims Her Victories

The Hague Peace Conference of 1898–1899. McCarthy recounts Tsar Nicholas II’s Rescript of August 1898 inviting the Powers to a conference on disarmament and international arbitration. The chapter covers the opening of the conference at The Hague in May 1899, the debates on arms limitation (which achieved little), the establishment of the Permanent Court of Arbitration, the conventions on the laws of war (the Hague Conventions), and the declaration against the use of asphyxiating gases and expanding bullets. McCarthy is cautiously hopeful about the conference’s significance despite its limited practical results.

Chapter XIII — The Session of 1899

The Parliamentary session of 1899. McCarthy surveys the political landscape: the Liberal party under new leadership following the retirement of Sir William Harcourt, the growing divisions within Liberalism over imperialism and the South African question, the Conservative-Unionist Government under Lord Salisbury, and the key legislative measures of the session. The chapter also covers the budget, the naval estimates, Irish land bills, and the early parliamentary skirmishes over the Transvaal that foreshadowed the coming war.
