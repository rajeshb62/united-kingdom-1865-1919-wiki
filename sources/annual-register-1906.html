The Annual Register 1906

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The General Election

The new year found the country immersed in preparations for a general election which the Annual Register judged the most exciting and startling since 1880, if not since 1832. The positions of the two great parties had been completely reversed. The late Ministers were arraigned on charges of reckless extravagance, misjudgment of South African affairs, the introduction into the Transvaal of “Chinese slavery,” the endowment of denominational education, and the propounding of crude and self-contradictory fiscal reforms.

The Liberals, formerly the party of criticism and obstruction, now posed as reformers and claimed to be the real Free Traders and apostles of economic progress.

Among the issues before the electors, the fiscal question was probably the most important, especially in rural constituencies. Next came Chinese labour; then the modification of the Education Act of 1902. Home Rule, though somewhat unexpectedly revived by Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, was held by the Duke of Devonshire and others to be an improbable subject for early legislation by the new Government.

The Free Food Unionists occupied a peculiarly delicate position. The Duke of Devonshire, in a letter of 29 December 1905 to the honorary secretary of the Unionist Free Trade Club, declared that the proposals of the Tariff Reform League were “indistinguishable from pure Protection,” that a Unionist majority would almost certainly mean the adoption of that programme, and that Free Trade Unionists should therefore support Free Trade candidates irrespective of party. Some Free Fooders followed this counsel; others clung to party loyalty while repudiating Chamberlainite Protection.

Mr. Chamberlain’s election address attacked the new Ministry as essentially a Home Rule and Little Englander Government which must exist, if at all, by Irish votes, and insisted that the Colonies were awaiting a preferential response from the mother country. Mr. Balfour, in his address to the electors of Manchester, denounced the Liberal programme as surrounded with obscurity and warned that Home Rule, Disestablishment, and the destruction of voluntary schools had lost none of their ancient charm for Radical lawmakers.

Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman’s address contained no specific programme and no mention of Home Rule. He attacked the late Government’s wars and adventures abroad, its reckless finance, and its confused Army reorganisation, and declared that Protection was immoral, oppressive, and corrupting.

Sectional bodies crowded the field with manifestoes. The Cobden Club, the United Irish League, the Social Democratic Federation, and the Free Church Council all issued appeals; the last-named sent ministers about the country by motor car to deliver Liberal speeches, while special prayers were offered in the churches.

Full-page newspaper advertisements defended Chinese labour; cartoons, especially those against Chinese labour, aroused lively indignation among Unionists. At Derby on 3 January Mr. Chamberlain declared that under Free Trade there were a million able-bodied male paupers in the United Kingdom—a statement which Liberals and Free Trade economists immediately and successfully challenged as a misuse of the poor-law figures.

At the Albert Hall on 5 January the Prime Minister, while affirming that the Liberals remained a Home Rule party, announced that they would not introduce a Home Rule Bill in the coming Parliament but would proceed step by step toward the larger settlement.

A Blue-book on Chinese labour, issued 21 December 1905, became a principal campaign document on the Liberal side. Polling began on 12 January. The chapter records a Unionist rout of extraordinary completeness as the returns came in from London, the industrial North, Scotland, and Wales.

The residual strength of Tariff Reform within the diminished Opposition was carefully analysed; Wales returned no Unionists; Scotland became predominantly Liberal; and the prediction that the Government would be dependent on Nationalist votes was falsified by the scale of the Liberal and Labour majority over all other groups combined.

Chapter II — The New Parliament and Early Session

The new Parliament assembled on 13 February. Mr. J. W. Lowther was re-elected Speaker on the motion of Sir Edward Grey, seconded by Mr. Keir Hardie, who thus marked the arrival of Labour as a distinct parliamentary force rather than a mere wing of Liberalism. The King’s Speech promised legislation on Trade Disputes, Workmen’s Compensation, Merchant Shipping, Education, and the reform of Irish government; careful consideration of the social and economic conditions of the rural districts of Great Britain; and a Colonial conference on the question of preference.

The Address debate ranged widely over Chinese labour, the fiscal question, Army organisation, India, Education, women’s suffrage, the Macdonnell controversy, and Home Rule.

Chinese labour was the first great test of the new majority. Lord Elgin’s telegram of 20 January had directed that no further Chinese labourers should be imported into the Transvaal pending the establishment of representative government there. The Prime Minister on 22 February defended this course, and the subsequent debate of 26 February produced a Ministerial majority of 416 to 91.

On fiscal policy Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman reaffirmed Free Trade as the settled doctrine of the Government; Mr. Chamberlain continued to press imperial preference as the true imperial bond; Mr. Asquith, as Chancellor of the Exchequer, warned that a 2s. corn duty would mean a rise of about 2½d. on the four-pound loaf and brushed aside the subtler forms of retaliation as unworkable.

Mr. Haldane, at the War Office, sketched a policy of two-year service with the colours and of carrying out the recommendations of the Esher Committee for the reorganisation of the military departments. Mr. Morley, as Indian Secretary, dealt with the partition of Bengal and with the difficult question of military administration under the new arrangements that had followed the Curzon-Kitchener dispute.

Mr. Birrell, President of the Board of Education, outlined an Education Bill that would place all schools maintained from the rates under the local education authorities, impose the Cowper-Temple restriction on religious teaching in provided schools, and grant “facilities” for denominational instruction under strict conditions—including a four-fifths parental majority where special facilities were claimed for a particular denomination.

Women’s suffrage made its first dramatic appearance in the new House when Mr. Keir Hardie moved an amendment to the Address in favour of the enfranchisement of women; it was rejected by 114 votes, only 46 members voting for it. In Ireland the Crimes Act of 1887 was suspended in the few districts where it still operated; Sir Antony Macdonnell was continued as permanent Under-Secretary for Ireland; and the Government’s Irish policy was described as Home Rule by instalments rather than by a single large measure of the Gladstonian type.

The Navy Estimates stood at 31,869,500l., a reduction of about 1,500,000l. upon the previous year, while the two-Power standard was reaffirmed as the measure of national security. The Army Estimates of 29,796,000l. showed a reduction of nearly 3,000,000l. Mr. Redmond and the Nationalists pressed for an Irish Council and for the speedy restoration of the evicted tenants as the session settled into its legislative work.

Chapter III — The Education Bill and the Budget

The Education Bill became the central measure of the session and the principal battleground between Nonconformists, Anglicans, Roman Catholics, and Jews, as well as between the two Houses of Parliament. Passive resisters continued their campaign against the rates levied under the Act of 1902 even as the new Bill sought to recast the whole settlement of elementary education.

Clause by clause the four-fifths rule, the powers of the local education authorities, Cowper-Temple teaching in provided schools, and the special facilities for denominational instruction in schools transferred to the public authority were fought over in the country and in the House. Anglican bishops and Catholic prelates organised resistance; Free Church councils rallied Nonconformist opinion behind Mr. Birrell; and the Jewish community sought safeguards for its own schools.

Women’s suffrage agitation accompanied the parliamentary struggle outside the chamber. Disturbances at the House of Commons on 25 April and further scenes on 19 May, when the Prime Minister and Mr. Asquith were interrupted by women demanding the vote, advertised the determination of the militant section of the movement and foreshadowed more serious collisions later in the year.

Mr. Asquith introduced the Budget on 30 April. He announced a realised surplus of nearly 3,500,000l.; reduced the tea duty from 6d. to 5d. the pound; repealed the coal export duty which had been a standing grievance of the export trade; left the income tax at 1s. in the pound but introduced a notable relief for earned incomes as distinct from income from property; and raised the fixed debt charge (the new sinking fund) from 9,500,000l. to 13,500,000l., thereby accelerating the reduction of the National Debt swollen by the South African War.

The Labourers (Ireland) Bill and other social measures advanced alongside the Education struggle. The chapter closes with the Education Bill still in the thick of debate, its friends already warning that the Lords would mutilate it beyond recognition and its enemies organising resistance in the constituencies, in Convocation, and among the managers of voluntary schools.

Chapter IV — Committee Struggles, Plural Voting, and the Transvaal Constitution

Through the summer the Education Bill occupied the committee stage day after day. Clauses 1, 4, and 7—the local-authority clause, the facilities clause, and the four-fifths clause—were remodelled under Mr. Birrell’s management. Denominationalists complained that the concessions were illusory and that the Bill destroyed the religious character of the voluntary schools; Nonconformists complained that too much had been given away to the Church and to Rome. Amendments from every quarter thickened the Bill without reconciling its opponents.

On 25 June Mr. Lewis Harcourt introduced the Plural Voting Bill, designed to abolish the plural franchise at parliamentary elections so that no elector should vote in more than one constituency. It obtained a second reading on 2 July by 323 to 96, the Opposition denouncing it as a partisan attack upon property and upon the university seats.

Workmen’s Compensation legislation also advanced as part of the Ministry’s labour programme, extending the range of industries and the classes of workmen covered by the principle of compensation for accidents.

The most important imperial announcement of the year came on 31 July, when Mr. Winston Churchill in the Commons and the Earl of Elgin in the Lords explained the main provisions of the new Transvaal Constitution. The Lyttelton Constitution, devised by the late Government, was declared “stillborn” and unworkable.

Responsible government was to be granted at once; Boer and Briton alike would receive the full privileges and rights of British citizenship; the legislature would be a single chamber of sixty-nine members elected by adult male suffrage, with a nominated Legislative Council subject to a property qualification of 25,000l. capital or 250l. a year; and no new licences for Chinese labourers would be issued after 30 November. The Opposition denounced the measure as a surrender to the recently defeated Boers, who, they warned, would use the vote as a substitute for arms and would dominate the new Colony.

The Prime Minister, rising near the close of the debate, declared that he had never heard a more unworthy speech—his words drowned by Opposition protests—and the resolution approving the Constitution was carried by 316 to 83.

In the Lords the Plural Voting Bill received a second reading on 3 August by 105 to 38, only to be rejected later on principle when the real issue was joined. The Education Bill itself was read a second time in the Upper House on 1–2 August without a division, the peers reserving their strength for the committee stage after the recess. The session then adjourned for the summer, leaving the great constructive Bills incomplete and the constitutional tension between a Liberal Commons and a Unionist Lords already clearly visible.

Chapter V — Recess, Dreadnought, Autumn Session, and the Lords

Bank Holiday speeches and the East Denbighshire bye-election kept party feeling alive after the adjournment. Mr. Churchill predicted a satisfactory Licensing Bill for 1907; Earl Carrington, at an agricultural show at Broughton Castle near Banbury, commended the Land Tenure Bill as a measure of justice to the farming class.

Viscount Milner, replying to an address of appreciation of his services in South Africa—presented privately at his own desire and signed by more than 370,000 adult males, with separate addresses from Natal (some 3,000 signatures) and from Cape Colony (20,000)—warned that the danger in South Africa was disunion and at home the growth of a spirit of anti-nationalism. The East Denbighshire poll on 14 August showed an increased Liberal majority; a Labour candidate announced by Mr. Keir Hardie did not appear.

On 8 August the Court of Appeal, in The King, on the prosecution of the Board of Education, v. The County Council of the West Riding of Yorkshire, decided that under the Act of 1902 a local authority might deduct from teachers’ salaries the proportion estimated to be due for denominational teaching.

The judgment seemed both to defeat the intentions of Parliament in 1902 and to destroy the passive resisters’ grievance. Some magistrates continued to issue distress warrants; others adjourned summonses sine die. The Government took the case to the House of Lords, which reversed the judgment on 14 December.

The Report of the War Stores Commission, appointed in 1905 after Sir William Butler’s committee, was issued as a Blue Book on 10 August. It found a deplorable lack of cohesion and intercommunication between the departments at the War Office; inexcusable carelessness and extraordinary ineptitude in the business of the Director of Supplies in South Africa; and a loss of public money estimated at between 750,000l. and 1,250,000l.

Higher officers were for the most part charged with stupidity and lack of business capacity rather than corruption, though petty corruption among non-commissioned officers was noted. Disciplinary action followed on 16 October: twelve officers were dealt with by methods varying from censure to removal from the army, and nine officers and non-commissioned officers were discharged for misconduct. Mr. Haldane announced that the nucleus of an administrative staff would be trained at the London School of Economics.

Against this administrative blot the construction of H.M.S. Dreadnought stood as a national achievement of the first order. Her keel plate had been laid on 2 October 1905; she was launched in the presence of the King on 10 February 1906; and on 4 August the King and the Prince of Wales inspected her.

She was designed to carry ten 12-inch guns so mounted as to facilitate all-round fire, twenty-seven 12-pounder quick-firers for defence against torpedo craft, and five submerged torpedo tubes; her main armour belt reached eleven inches; her coal capacity would enable her to steam 5,800 sea miles at moderate speed or 3,500 at 18 knots, extendable by oil fuel; her speed was to be 21 knots. She was the first large warship fitted with turbine engines, and the admiral and all officers were berthed forward of the engines instead of astern.

She left Portsmouth for steam trials on 1 October 1906, precisely 364 days after the first keel plate, and both steam and gun trials were in every way satisfactory—proof, the Register observed, that Great Britain could keep well ahead of other nations.

Army reorganisation advanced by the Army Order published 13 September constituting, for the first time, a General Staff for the British Army in accordance with the recommendation of the Esher Committee. It was to be composed of (a) the general staff at headquarters—fifty-seven officers, including three major-generals—and (b) the general staff in commands and districts—114 officers, mostly of the rank of colonel or below.

Members would as a rule have passed through the Staff College and have had eight years’ service; appointments would be for four years. Mr. Haldane insisted that such a staff was necessary not only in war but to make the Army progressive in peace, and he hoped it would become a real bond of union with the forces of the self-governing Colonies.

The Spectator, at the instance of its conductors, embodied an experimental company of working-class lads of average age nineteen for six months’ thorough training; the results were held to show that a good infantry soldier could be made within that time. Considerable sections of the Liberal party, however, remained bitterly hostile to all popularisation of military training, and especially to drill and rifle practice in schools.

Friction between Liberalism and Labour appeared in the Cockermouth contest, in South Wales attempts to coerce non-unionists into the Miners’ Federation, in Clyde boilermakers’ strikes, and in a resolution of the Scottish Liberal Association at Peebles on 5 October, carried by 55 to 34, urging Liberal opposition to all candidates who would not dissociate themselves from Socialism. Unionists, for their part, revived suspicions that their leaders had somewhat relaxed their Unionism in connection with Sir Antony Macdonnell’s appointment and the devolution schemes of 1904–5.

When the autumn session opened, “strangers” in the House, the Plural Voting Bill, the Evicted Tenants question, and above all the Education Bill in the Lords dominated business. Municipal elections, speeches by Mr. Balfour, Mr. Asquith, and Mr. Austen Chamberlain, the Trade Disputes Bill, the Land Tenure Bill, the Town Tenants (Ireland) Bill—with a proposal to expropriate Lord Clanricarde—the Merchant Shipping Bill, land settlement and Chinese vice in the South African compounds, deputations on old-age pensions and the Congo Free State, and naval affairs filled the calendar.

The Lords reconstituted the Education Bill almost beyond recognition on report; rival deputations thronged Westminster; the Plural Voting Bill, having passed the Commons, was rejected by the Lords; the Trade Disputes Bill and the Town Tenants and Land Tenure Bills ran their course under pressure of time; a Licensing Act Amendment Bill, a Workmen’s Compensation Bill, and an Education (Provision of Meals) Bill advanced; and the traditional “Massacre of the Innocents” cleared the Order Paper of measures that could not be saved.

Efforts at compromise on the Education Bill between the two Houses failed. The constitution of the Orange River Colony was announced on lines akin to those of the Transvaal; the writ for Worcester was suspended; a Wireless Telegraphy Convention was debated; other legislation was hurried through; and the King’s Speech closed the session.

The political outlook was one of open conflict between a massive Liberal majority in the Commons and a Unionist majority in the Lords, with the mutilated Education Bill as the symbol of the struggle and the party machines already preparing for the next phase of the constitutional quarrel.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Apart from its share in the politics of the United Kingdom, Scotland had a quiet year marked by great commercial and industrial prosperity. At the general election only Stirling Burghs escaped a contest—and that through the sudden illness of the Unionist candidate—and of the seventy-two members returned only twelve were Unionists: two of them for University seats, four owing their return to Liberal-Labour rivalry, and two Free Traders.

Scottish opinion thus showed itself decidedly adverse to fiscal reform. Eleven Labour candidatures against Liberals helped to exacerbate the autumn dispute between the two progressive forces. The Scottish Home Rule Association took occasion to urge the need of devolution for Scotland as well as Ireland.

Of legislation specially affecting Scotland, the Fatal Accidents and Sudden Deaths Inquiry Bill and the Statute Law Revision (Scotland) Bill were useful measures of reform; the National Galleries (Scotland) Bill substituted for the old Board of Manufactures a new board of seven trustees nominated by the Secretary for Scotland. A Royal Commission on the expediency of instituting Registration of Title in Scotland was announced on 1 May.

Slight additional encouragement was given to the teaching of Gaelic in primary schools, in order to provide a supply of teachers for some 25,000 Gaelic-speaking children—a measure which provoked some criticism as a waste of time and a possible fosterer of separatism, but which commanded a strong preponderance of Scottish opinion. Acute indignation was aroused in November by the proposal to remove the Scots Greys from Edinburgh and Glasgow and so to deprive Scotland of a cavalry regiment altogether.

Mr. Haldane explained on 21 November that Piershill Barracks had become insanitary for cavalry, that the cost of new barracks would be very heavy, and that the removal might not be permanent; but Lord Rosebery, at a protest meeting in Edinburgh on 3 November, scoffed at the arguments from sanitation and economy and declared that if such treatment were continued the Scottish nation would be compelled to examine more closely the benefits it received from the Treaty of Union.

In ecclesiastical matters the Free Churches Commission proceeded with much inevitable delay in apportioning the property of the former Free Church between the United Free Church and the “Wee Frees.” A report of 19 October stated that about a thousand congregations had then had their cases decided.

The General Assemblies of the three Churches opened in Edinburgh on 22 May, Lord Colebrooke being Lord High Commissioner for the Church of Scotland. The fourth centenary of the University of Aberdeen was celebrated on 25–27 September with great éclat; the King and Queen visited the city on the last day to open the new buildings of Marischal College—laboratories, lecture rooms, the Mitchell Tower and Hall—erected by public subscription at a cost of upwards of 200,000l.; some 120 honorary degrees were conferred upon delegates from universities as far afield as Japan and Ecuador.

In commerce and industry the year was one of great prosperity. The shipbuilding output of the Clyde reached the highest figure on record, the highest estimate being 601,658 tons for the year, with about 500,000 tons remaining for 1907; engineering output measured by indicated horse-power (608,620 i.h.p.) was also the largest recorded.

Among vessels launched were the Cunarder Lusitania; H.M.S. Agamemnon, the most formidable warship ever built on the Clyde; the Canadian Pacific liner Empress of Ireland; and the Pacific Steam Navigation liner Oriana. Of 374 vessels launched, 151 went to foreign and 30 to colonial owners.

A seven weeks’ strike of the “black squad”—boilermakers, riveters, and other ironworkers—in October and November threw nearly 17,000 men out of work before the threat of a general lock-out terminated the strike on 21 November. The jute trade was prosperous; mineral oil benefited from interference with the Caucasian industry by the Russian revolutionists; aluminium, motor-car manufacture, and electrical engineering gave favourable reports; the herring season on the north-east coast was excellent; and the average price obtained for 1,951 shorthorn cattle slightly exceeded 64l. a head—an increase of 250 per cent. on that of about twenty years previously.

Ireland. Politically the year was one of comparative quiet, though Irish affairs still exercised an appreciable influence on English politics. At the general election more than three-fourths of the Irish seats were uncontested, and the balance of parties was hardly changed. The Nationalists lost South Dublin to the Unionists and won West Belfast from them; Liberals were returned in two Ulster constituencies; and of the eighty-two Nationalist members four, including Mr. W. O’Brien and Mr. Timothy Healy, stood independent of the regular Irish Parliamentary party. The Nationalist leaders, while declaring they would be satisfied with nothing less than full Home Rule, avowed their intention of giving the Ministry time to mature its plans for Irish legislation.

The Earl of Aberdeen made his State entry as Viceroy into Dublin on 3 February. Three days later the operation of the Crimes Act, 1887, was suspended in the few districts in which it was still in force; on 13 February new regulations were issued for the inspection of estates under the Land Purchase Act; and on 6 March Mr. Long’s instructions to the Land Commissioners were cancelled and they were directed to frame their own rules. Delay in the restoration of the evicted tenants to their holdings was bitterly complained of by the Nationalists both in Parliament and in Ireland. The Sale of Intoxicants Bill provided for the Sunday closing of licensed premises in Dublin, Belfast, Cork, Limerick, and Waterford—hitherto exempted—and for universal closing at 9 P.M. on Saturdays; it divided the Nationalist party but eventually became law.

The Labourers (Ireland) Bill and the Town Tenants Bill became items in the conflict between the two Houses; the latter was partly occasioned by the eviction at Loughrea of one of the Marquess of Clanricarde’s tenants. Mr. Bryce on 28 May sketched a scheme for encouraging the teaching of Irish in primary schools.

Royal Commissions on Irish Railways, on the working of the Congested Districts Board, on Canals and Waterways, and on Trinity College, Dublin, were appointed and took evidence during the year.

The Irish Land Purchase Acts continued to work satisfactorily. An issue of 7,000,000l. guaranteed land stock at 89 was made in June and was over-subscribed to about nine times the nominal value of the loan. Some agrarian disturbances and outrages were reported from the south and west, partly in connection with attacks on large grass farms and partly due to attempts to force landlords to sell under the Acts.

A few regrettable exhibitions of disloyalty were reported, notably the action of Alderman Kelly of Dublin in cutting down the Union Jack from the steamer conveying the Corporation down the Liffey at the opening of the new drainage works on 24 September; the Corporation declined to take notice of his act. The Gaelic League continued its efforts for the encouragement of the Irish language and the revival of Irish customs.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1906

F. Harcourt Kitchin recorded that 1906 was one of the best general trade years the world had known. The industrial expansion which began toward the end of 1904 had been assisted by bountiful harvests almost everywhere, and the United Kingdom, the Continent of Europe, and North and South America had shared in a series of trade records—the Americas even more than Europe.

The only grey spots were Russia, still troubled politically but showing an extraordinary power of recuperation; South Africa, suffering from the effects of the late war and a surfeit of party politics; and China, where unsold stocks at Hong-Kong and Shanghai had seriously accumulated. Japan, on the other hand, was picking up after the war drain and threatened to become a more serious competitor in Far Eastern markets than ever before.

Reports from India, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand were most cheerful—almost too much so, since there were doubts whether the boom in Canadian land and railways could last.

Money in the technical sense understood in the Money Markets was scarce nearly all through the year. The autumn saw the Bank of England rate of discount go up to 6 per cent. and stay there for some months; at no time was the market rate of discount in London below 3 per cent. The demand was due principally to the requirements of industry and railway development, but partly also to the waste of wealth by earthquake and fire.

The great disaster at San Francisco in April caused destruction of property exceeding 50,000,000l. in value; with a similar catastrophe at Valparaiso, though on a smaller scale, it threw a heavy strain on the world’s supplies of available capital. British insurance offices were badly hit at San Francisco and ultimately paid about ten and a half millions sterling, but as some little set-off the disaster caused such a demand for cement, steel, and other building materials as to benefit, directly and indirectly, many British industries.

As the prices of high-class securities are much influenced by the current value of money, the year was not a good one for large holders of good securities. Consols fell as much as 3 points to 86; Great Western Railway Ordinary declined by 12 to 133½; London and North Western Ordinary by 8 to 153; Metropolitan Railway Ordinary had a ghastly fall of 27 to 60.

On the other hand, Canadian, Mexican, and many South American securities showed great strength on account of the rapid development proceeding in those countries. The only loan operation worthy of permanent record was the issue of Russian Five per Cents. Of a total of 90,000,000l., the 13,000,000l. offered in London were readily taken up on terms giving subscribers nearly 5 per cent.; the new scrip shortly afterwards, in consequence of internal disturbances in Russia, fell as low as 12 discount, but recovered steadily later when it was seen that the long-prophesied revolution had failed to come off.

There had never been a year during which a greater amount of over-sea trade was carried on. The aggregate imports and exports of the United Kingdom for the first time exceeded 1,000,000,000l. While imports increased by nearly 43,000,000l., or 7.6 per cent., to 608,000,000l., the exports of British and Irish produce increased by no less than 45,800,000l., or 13.9 per cent., to 375,600,000l.

Re-exports of colonial and foreign produce were also 7,300,000l. (9.5 per cent.) higher, at 85,000,000l. The greater part of the increase in imports consisted of raw materials, and the greater part of the increase in exports of manufactured goods, so that the foreign trade of the year was in an unusually healthy state.

One of the most notable features was the advance in metals: raw copper and tin by about 30 per cent., lead by 25 per cent., antimony by nearly 100 per cent.—advances almost wholly due to industrial demand, which revived the Cornish and Devon mining industries. Over 2,000,000 tons of new shipping were put into the water from the shipbuilding yards of the United Kingdom, a record figure; yet the expansion—due principally to excited orders placed soon after the close of the Russo-Japanese War—added to the glut of British shipping seeking employment, and shipping generally had a hard and rather unprofitable time.

Lancashire cotton equalled the prosperous figures of the previous year; some of the heavy engineering trades, as well as motors and cycles, had a wonderful year; the electrical industry was badly hurt by high commodity prices and foreign competition. Broadly speaking, American and German absorption in their own internal demands had for the time almost suspended “dumping,” leaving British manufacturers more of the home market than for years past—an observation Kitchin offered impartially as between the advantages of open ports and of an exclusive home market.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. Republican France faced, after the passing of the law separating Church and State, the problem which Catherine de Médicis had put to Henry III after the murder of Guise: the thing was well cut, but could it be sewn up? The question affected the elections to the Chambers, the succession to the Presidency, and the completion of the process of separation. The Senate elections of 7 January, concerning Series B and vacant seats, returned 103 senators.

For the first time Socialists succeeded in electing two members—M. Flaissières, ex-Mayor of Marseilles, and M. Delhon, a vinegrower of the Hérault—and for the first time the balance of Right and Left was not appreciably modified, an indication that equilibrium had been reached and that France had accepted separation.

On 17 January the National Assembly met at Versailles to elect a successor to M. Loubet. The Radical and Radical-Socialist groups, and even the Socialists, supported a moderate Republican senator, M. Fallières; the Right supported a Radical deputy, M. Doumer. M. Fallières was elected at the first ballot by 449 votes to 371.

He could not enter on his duties till 18 February, and political life resumed its normal course amid the approach of the electoral campaign—until an unexpected explosion of religious passions broke the monotony. A clause in the Separation Law provided that in every church an inventory should be taken of artistic treasures and church furniture.

Catholics offered forcible resistance, beginning on 31 January in Paris and spreading within days to La Vendée, Brittany, the mountains of Auvergne, and Flanders. Parish clergy protested; the laity prevented entrance to the churches; in the Grande Chartreuse peasants rolled down masses of rock upon the soldiers; in the Loire, La Vendée, and the Loire-Inférieure there was bloodshed.

On the very day of M. Loubet’s retirement (18 February) the Press published an Encyclical embodying an energetic papal protest against the severance of the Concordat. The Vatican designated occupants for vacant French bishoprics without State control, and left French Catholics uncertain as to the lawfulness of forming Public Worship Associations to take over church property.

The Gallican spirit had so completely died out that not a single bishop took the initiative in compliance with the law. On 7 March, after a disturbance at Boescheppe in the Nord in which a demonstrator was killed, a vote of confidence proposed by M. Péret and accepted by the Government was rejected by 267 to 234 through a coalition of Socialists, Radical Socialists, and the Right.

M. Rouvier fell at a moment when the Algeciras Conference was at its most difficult stage, the Budget was still under discussion, and public peace was disturbed both by the inventories and by strikes.

An explosion of fire-damp in the mines of Courrières, near Lens, on 10 March resulted in the loss of more than 1,100 lives. The survivors charged the company with having neglected safety works through avarice; a strike was proclaimed and rapidly spread through the district; anarchist elements were attracted from Paris and from abroad.

M. Sarrien was charged to form a Ministry and by 13 March had succeeded, though by a childish concession to superstition the decrees were post-dated 14 March. M. Sarrien became Prime Minister and Minister of Justice; M. Clemenceau took the Interior—his first office, though long known for skill in overthrowing Ministries; M. Aristide Briand, a Socialist who had once advocated the general strike, took Public Instruction with Public Worship; M. Léon Bourgeois took the Foreign Office; M. Raymond Poincaré took Finance.

The Ministry applied the Cartesian method of dividing difficulties: M. Bourgeois got France through the critical stage of the Morocco question; M. Clemenceau suspended the inventories wherever there was organised resistance, remarking with some humour that the pleasure of counting the candlesticks in a church was not worth the sacrifice of a single human life; M. Poincaré limited his ambition to getting the Budget passed in the form left by his predecessor.

In a single month (14 March–14 April) the Finance Bill passed both Houses, and the Chamber broke up on 14 April for the elections.

The seventh Legislature under the Constitution of 1875 ended amid domestic confusion unparalleled since 1871—miners’ strikes in the Nord and Pas-de-Calais, building strikes at Clermont-Ferrand, general strikes at Lorient and Toulon, and in Paris compositors, excavators, Metropolitan railway men, and even postmen on strike. Threats of a general strike and an assault on capitalism for 1 May produced an unprecedented panic; troops guarded workshops, bakeries, and printing establishments; searches were made at the houses of revolutionists and Monarchists alike.

Amid these troubles the electoral campaign went on with remarkable discipline. Of 591 seats, 433 were decided at the first ballot on 6 May; only 169 were carried by the Opposition. When the new Chamber met on 1 June it consisted of about 175 Reactionaries, Nationalists, and Conservative Republicans or Progressists; 340 Republicans of the Left, Radicals, and Radical Socialists; and 75 Socialists either “independent” or “unified.”

For the first time the majority on the Left was large enough to be independent of the Socialist alliance. France was as resolutely hostile to priestly rule as in 1875 and 1877, and in the main accepted the separation of Church and State—so manifestly that the General Assembly of French Bishops, under Cardinal Lecot, favoured by a large majority the formation of Public Worship Associations and petitioned the Pope for the necessary leave.

M. Henri Brisson was elected President of the Chamber. After a debate of unprecedented range in which M. Clemenceau mercilessly ridiculed M. Jaurès’s Collectivist Utopias, a vote of confidence was passed on 21 June by 400 to 88. On 12 July, after a third trial as calm and dignified as its predecessors had been disturbed, the Full Court of Cassation quashed the verdict of the Rennes court-martial of 1899 against Captain Dreyfus and did not remit the case for trial before another tribunal.

Next day the Government introduced a Bill restoring Dreyfus to his rank with seniority for the whole time of unjust deprivation; Lieutenant-Colonel Picquart was at once promoted brigadier-general and a few months later general of division. Thus ended the Affair which had stirred France so profoundly.

A law establishing a weekly day of rest was forced through; M. Poincaré’s Budget exceeded four milliards of francs (revenue 4,010,922,935 francs); and the Council of State laboured at the rules for apportioning the property of suppressed ecclesiastical establishments and for applying the weekly rest—seen already to bristle with difficulties.
