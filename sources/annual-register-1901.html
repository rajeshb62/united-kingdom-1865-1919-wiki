The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1901

The Annual Register 1901

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The Queen’s Death and the Accession

Queen Victoria died at Osborne on 22 January 1901, aged 81, surrounded by her family including the German Emperor. Her 64-year reign — the longest in British history — ended with the throne passing to her son Edward VII. Parliament met on 24 January, with both Houses passing addresses of condolence. The Queen’s funeral on 2 February was a day of profound national mourning; the German Emperor remained in England for the ceremonies. The King was proclaimed across the Empire and met with an enthusiastic reception as he began his reign.

Chapter II — The New Reign

Edward VII opened his first Parliament in March. The South African War dominated debates, with controversy over guerrilla resistance and concentration camps. The Education (Cockerton) difficulty arose from a legal judgment limiting school board expenditure, precipitating a crisis in elementary education. London County Council elections returned a Progressive majority. Army estimates rose sharply under St John Brodrick’s reorganisation scheme, proposing three army corps for home defence and a fourth for colonial service. Navy estimates increased by £2,585,000. The Cockerton judgment was confirmed on appeal, leaving the Education Department to seek legislative remedy.

Chapter III — The Budget and Education

A Queen Victoria Memorial scheme was launched with national subscriptions. The Budget raised income tax to 1s 2d, increased the death duties, imposed a duty on coal exports, and maintained the sugar duty. The Irish university education question was referred to a Royal Commission. The Government introduced an Education Bill to address the Cockerton judgment and reorganise elementary education. The civil list for the new reign was settled at £470,000 annually. Army reorganisation plans were debated vigorously, with Liberal critics attacking the cost and structure.

Chapter IV — The Autumn Session

Lord Milner returned from South Africa and was elevated to the peerage. Bye-elections showed government majorities reduced. Old-age pensions were debated inconclusively. The Education Bill occupied weeks of parliamentary time, facing opposition from Nonconformists over the religious question and from those defending school boards. War Office reorganisation continued, with a new Army Council established. The concentration camps in South Africa became a major political controversy after Emily Hobhouse’s report. Liberal divisions deepened between Imperialists and pro-Boer factions. The Royal Titles Bill added ‘King of the British Dominions beyond the Seas’ to the royal style. Lord Rosebery and Mr Chamberlain made significant speeches at Blenheim and elsewhere.

Chapter V — The Close of the Year

The Duke and Duchess of Cornwall visited South Africa. International Congresses met at Glasgow. The Taff Vale judgment established that trade unions could be sued in their registered name for damages arising from strikes — a landmark in labour law. President McKinley was assassinated (September 14) at the Pan-American Exhibition in Buffalo; Vice-President Theodore Roosevelt succeeded him. Sir Redvers Buller was dismissed from his command amid controversy. Mr Chamberlain’s Edinburgh speech, urging imperial preference and a tariff for the Empire, caused political excitement; the German press reacted indignantly to his characterisation of German policy. The Hay-Pauncefote Treaty gave the United States exclusive rights to construct an Isthmian Canal. The Duke of Cornwall was created Prince of Wales. Lord Rosebery’s Chesterfield speech called for a reorganisation of the Liberal Party around progressive principles.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. The year opened with deep mourning across Scotland for Queen Victoria. The General Assembly of the Church of Scotland faced debates on theological questions. Mr Andrew Carnegie’s magnificent gift of £2,000,000 to the Scottish universities was the most notable event of the year in Scottish education; the fund was to be administered by a trust with the aim of improving higher education and encouraging research across Scotland’s four ancient universities. Scottish agriculture had a mixed year, with reasonable harvests but continued rural depopulation. The fishing industry along the east coast showed signs of revival after several poor seasons. Highland land questions remained unsettled, though crofter agitation was less pronounced than in previous years.

Ireland. Economically, Ireland showed marked progress in 1901. The co-operative dairy industry expanded to 200 central dairies by year’s end, up from 150 in 1899. Exports of Irish butter and eggs to Great Britain reached nearly £5 million for the first eleven months — an increase of almost £2 million over 1900. Bank deposits passed £40 million, with the Bank of Ireland declaring a 12 per cent dividend. Flax cultivation expanded encouragingly from 47,451 to 55,471 acres, and the Department of Agriculture under Mr Horace Plunkett actively promoted its revival. Belfast shipbuilding enjoyed its best year on record: Harland & Wolff launched 92,316 tons — including the Celtic (nearly 21,000 tons, the largest ship ever built) — and total Belfast output exceeded 145,000 tons, 26,000 above the previous record. Londonderry began shipbuilding in 1901. The linen industry, however, suffered depression with low prices, short-time working, and several Belfast firm failures, though signs of recovery appeared toward year’s end.

Politically and socially, conditions were less favourable. The United Irish League extended its influence across the south and west, and while overt agrarian crime was rare, loyalists complained of systematic intimidation. The Chief Secretary, Mr Wyndham, downplayed the League’s significance in December, calling it a political machine of negligible strength — a view derided by Unionists and Nationalists alike. Prosecutions in the latter half of December saw several MPs convicted of unlawful assembly: Mr Conor O’Kelly (North Mayo) was sentenced to two months’ imprisonment; Mr Hayden (South Roscommon) to twenty-one days; Mr John O’Donnell (South Mayo) and Mr Tully (South Leitrim) to two months and one month respectively. Mr William O’Brien, the League’s founder travelling in Australia, telegraphed congratulations. Mr John Redmond, returning from fundraising in the United States, welcomed the Government’s resort to ‘coercion’ as the ‘salt’ needed for Ireland’s political health. The Government hoped the mild sentences under the Crimes Act would check intimidation without full coercive measures.

The Russell agitation for compulsory sale of all Irish land to occupying tenants continued in Ulster, though making little apparent headway. The Roman Catholic university question was examined by a Royal Commission covering all university education in Ireland (except Trinity College), with suggestions of changes to the University of Dublin’s constitution. Protestant feeling in the North remained strongly opposed to any separate Roman Catholic university arrangements.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade

By F. Harcourt Kitchin

Financial affairs in 1901 were heavily influenced by the South African War, whose suspension of Rand gold production reduced the world’s gold supply. Interest rates were high across Europe and security prices depressed, with the conspicuous exception of American railroad shares, which boomed on improved earnings and flourishing American trade. The year benefited investors with cash but harmed institutions forced to write down assets.

The British Government borrowed heavily for war expenditure. In February, £11 million in Exchequer Bonds were allotted at an average of 97. In April, £60 million in Consols were created at 94 (producing £56.7 million), with half offered to the public and the rest placed privately with Rothschilds, J. S. Morgan & Co., and the Bank of England. Consols fell to 91 in July — their lowest since Goschen’s conversion — before recovering to 94 by year-end. India’s attempt to raise £3 million failed when its issue coincided with a collapse in American railroad markets. Colonial loans, by contrast, were notably successful: Canada, Natal, New Zealand, and most Australian states complied with the Colonial Stock Act and appeared as 3% borrowers at 91–94.

British railways had another poor year; only the London electric ‘tubes’ — especially the Central London — succeeded, drawing so much traffic from the Metropolitan and District that those companies resolved to electrify. The London and Globe Finance Corporation collapse at the end of 1900 continued to reverberate through 1901; its managing director, Mr Whitaker Wright, had conducted highly speculative mining market operations with no effective oversight, and the failure was a severe blow to chairman Lord Dufferin. The South African mines resumed small-scale crushing in May; December’s gold output reached 52,897 ounces, and a quarter of all stamps were expected to work by February 1902.

Foreign trade held up well: total imports reached £523,075,000 (a decrease of only 0.15% from the record year of 1900) and exports £291,192,000 (a 3.6% decrease). The decline was substantially attributable to a 6.7% average fall in commodity prices. The great discrepancy between import and export values was attributed to interest on British overseas capital and shipping earnings. American trade expansion was the year’s most notable feature. The ‘Billion Dollar Trust’ — the United States Steel Corporation — was formed in February under J. Pierpont Morgan, capitalised at over $1 billion, grouping Carnegie Steel, Federal Steel, American Steel and Wire, National Tube, and others. Mr Morgan also acquired a controlling interest in the Leyland shipping line. The American Tobacco Company purchased Ogden’s to enter the British market, prompting thirteen British firms to form the Imperial Tobacco Company (£15 million capital). The Register judged that American competition, while alarming, would ultimately stimulate British manufacturers.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. The year’s dominant event was the passage of the Associations Bill, which required religious congregations to seek state authorisation to exist. Premier Waldeck-Rousseau guided the measure through Parliament despite fierce opposition; it passed the Chamber in January, the Senate after amendments, and received final approval on 29 June. Domestic unrest included a protracted 128-day miners’ strike at Montceau-les-Mines, where rival ‘Red’ and ‘Yellow’ unions emerged, and a dockworkers’ strike at Marseilles — both suppressed by government intervention. Financially, France faced a deficit after abolishing municipal duties on hygienic drinks; the 1901 budget totalled 3.55 billion francs, with military, naval, and colonial expenses the largest items. A progressive inheritance tax reaching 25% on large direct-line estates was introduced. In Algeria, the village of Margueritte was attacked by natives, prompting Governor-General Jonnart’s resignation. The census recorded 38,961,945 inhabitants — a meagre increase largely attributed to immigration. The year’s highlight was Tsar Nicholas II’s September visit, who reviewed four army corps near Rheims and was entertained at Compiègne; the exclusion of Paris from his itinerary drew controversy. France pressured Morocco by naval demonstration to settle frontier disputes, and in November occupied the Greek island of Mytilene to compel the Ottoman Porte to satisfy outstanding claims. The year ended with a heavily oversubscribed 265-million-franc China indemnity loan.

Italy. The Saracco ministry fell on 7 February (388 to 102) over the Genoa dockworkers’ strike and was replaced on 14 February by a broad coalition under the veteran Giuseppe Zanardelli, with Giovanni Giolitti at the Interior. The government faced ongoing labour unrest but inherited a budget surplus of 50 million lire for 1900–1901 — Italy’s most prosperous fiscal position in Europe. King Victor Emmanuel III won popularity by declining a public subscription gift, and the birth of Princess Yolanda-Margherita on 1 June was widely celebrated. A Franco-Italian entente was reached on Tripolitania, and the two countries cooperated on the Nice–Coni railway via the Col de Tende. Italy adhered to the Triple Alliance while maintaining cordial relations with France. Domestic reforms included abolition of the flour duty, salt tax reductions, and suppression of municipal food tolls. The government dealt firmly with the Camorra in Naples, where municipal elections in November routed the Camorrists. Giolitti created thirty new senators in December to break the Upper Chamber’s hostility. The year closed with Italian public finances regarded as the soundest in Europe.

Chapter II — Germany and Austria-Hungary

Germany. The year was dominated by the Canal Bill and a new tariff. Chancellor von Bülow’s attempt to pass the Elbe–Rhine Canal Bill through the Prussian Parliament failed in May after months of agrarian obstruction. The defeat triggered the resignations of Finance Minister von Miquel, Agriculture Minister von Hammerstein, and Commerce Minister Brefeld. The government then introduced a new tariff code dramatically raising minimum duties on rye, wheat, barley, oats, meat, and cattle, alongside increases on industrial goods; it provoked opposition from free-traders, Socialists, chambers of commerce, and foreign powers (Russia, Austria-Hungary, and Italy threatened retaliation), and was referred to committee at year’s end. The Reichstag passed a revised copyright law, increased veterans’ pensions, and extended most-favoured-nation treatment to Britain until 1903. The Empress Frederick died in August. A severe industrial crisis followed the collapse of the Leipzig Bank and Saxony Credit Society. The brutal flogging of Polish children at Wreschen for refusing to learn the catechism in German — part of an intensified Prussian Germanisation campaign — sparked outrage across Polish communities in Germany, Austria, and Russia. The Emperor gave provocative speeches (threatening bayonets against revolutionaries, declaring Germany’s ‘place in the sun’), faced an assassination attempt in Bremen, and clashed with Berlin’s Socialist-Radical municipal council. The Socialist Congress at Lübeck condemned Bernstein’s reformist views by 166 to 71. The census recorded 56.3 million inhabitants.

Austria-Hungary. The nationalities conflict between Germans and Czechs was temporarily suspended to pass major infrastructure bills — railway expansion and a Moldau–Elbe–Danube canal system — thanks to Prime Minister Körber’s skilful management. The general election returned more Pan-German extremists (21 seats) and Social Democrats, while Clericals lost ground. The Reichsrath opened with scandalous demonstrations: Pan-Germans and Social Democrats cheered the Boers and shouted ‘Down with England’ during tributes to Queen Victoria. The Emperor’s throne speech appealed for national reconciliation. The language feud reignited in the autumn session, forcing Körber to warn of constitutional suspension. In Hungary, an Incompatibility Bill targeting parliamentarians with government contracts was passed, and the general election strengthened the Independence Party while defeating the veteran Liberal Koloman Tisza. In foreign affairs, Goluchowski reaffirmed the Triple Alliance and determination to maintain the Balkan status quo against Macedonian agitation. Relations with Russia cooled over Pan-Slavist propaganda. The Italian seizure of the Austrian-protected College of San Girolamo in Rome was resolved diplomatically. Despite internal strains, Austria-Hungary maintained cordial relations with Britain.

Chapter III — Russia, Turkey and the Minor States of Eastern Europe

Chapter IV — Lesser States of Western and Northern Europe

Belgium, Luxembourg, Switzerland, Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, Norway

Belgium. Army reorganisation dominated politics: King Leopold II and progressives favoured abolishing the monetary substitution system (allowing the wealthy to buy out of service), but the ruling Catholic party resisted. A government commission recommended abolition, increasing the annual contingent from 13,000 to 18,000, and reducing service length. The government’s counter-proposal — retaining substitution, cutting infantry service from 28 to 20 months, and not increasing the contingent — narrowly passed first reading, pleasing no one. Belgium deferred annexation of the Congo Free State, renewing its loan arrangement for another decade. A major anti-gambling law was passed, suppressing all public gambling without exceptions for Ostend and Spa. The birth of Prince Leopold secured the male line of the dynasty. Luxembourg extended the franchise to all taxpayers of 10 francs, increased communal subsidies, and was disappointed when the heir’s wife bore another daughter, leaving the Nassau male line still without an heir. Switzerland saw a demonstration in Geneva against the extradition of anarchist Jaffei that led to the destruction of the Russian consulate’s escutcheon, followed by apologies and expulsions; the suppression of ‘Young Turk’ publications critical of the Sultan provoked debate over the right of asylum; the Socialist movement grew markedly as the Grütli labour association voted to merge with existing socialist organisations; rising foreign residency (one in eight residents) prompted talk of mandatory naturalisation; the state’s systematic purchase of railways continued, though the Chambers refused a 20 million franc artillery upgrade.

Netherlands. The Liberal Ministry was defeated in June after a split over electoral reform and replaced by a coalition Cabinet under Dr Kuyper (Anti-Revolutionary). Before falling, the Liberals passed an army reorganisation increasing the annual contingent from 11,000 to 17,000 and establishing a Landwehr reserve. The new government’s throne speech foregrounded moral and social issues: anti-gambling, anti-drink, food adulteration laws, and compulsory insurance. Queen Wilhelmina’s marriage to Duke Henry of Mecklenburg-Schwerin in February was met with wide public enthusiasm; the Atchin conflict continued. Spain endured extreme political and religious turbulence. The ‘Electra’ affair — a play coinciding with a court case over a daughter pressured into a convent — ignited anti-clerical riots. The Conservative government fell; Sagasta formed a Liberal Ministry in March, which moved to regulate unauthorised religious orders and enforce taxation on them, sparking violent clashes at Easter processions, May Day riots in Barcelona and Valencia, and repeated declarations of a state of siege. The June elections gave the government a solid majority, but the autumn session was consumed by disputes over the budget, Catalanist demands for autonomy, and a decree requiring customs duties in gold. Portugal struggled with religious conflict: riots in Oporto and Lisbon, the closure of illegally opened chapels, expulsion of foreign monks and nuns, and a papal letter bypassing the Foreign Minister inflamed tensions. The Cortes was dissolved in June; October elections strengthened the War Minister but failed to restore business confidence; financial difficulties persisted.

Denmark achieved a landmark political change: the Conservative government that had held power for decades was overthrown after the April general election returned only 8 government supporters versus 77 Left Reform, 15 Moderate Left, and 14 Social Democrats. In July, King Christian IX accepted a pure Left Reform Ministry under Professor Deuntzer, hailed with a huge national fête in Copenhagen. The new government passed a 30-million-krone state loan, a field artillery vote, and set to work on taxation reform, legal administration reform (with juries), and church governance changes. Sweden passed its long-awaited Army Reform Bill after intense debate, establishing 240 days of training for infantry and 365 for cavalry and field artillery, with 20 öre per day pay. The bill passed both Chambers after compromise; King Oscar II noted the reform was not a complete solution. Other measures included a National Bank Reform, a Government Accident Insurance Bill, and anti-usury legislation. A constitutional episode saw the Naval Minister censured and forced to resign over the unjust punishment of Rear-Admiral Högg. Norway had a quieter year; the Radical Steen government struggled to advance its programme. The most notable legislation was the extension of universal municipal suffrage for men and a qualified municipal franchise for women. New defensive measures (frontier fortifications on the Glommen line and a naval grant) were passed despite the King’s objections about procedure. Financial strains were evident — national expenditure had doubled in fifteen years — and a new 35-million-krone state loan was approved. The consular question with Sweden moved toward a joint committee, though Norway pressed for a fully separate consular service.

Chapter VI — Asia (the Far East)

China, Japan, Korea, Hong Kong, Wei-Hai-Wei, Kiaochow, Tongking, Siam By Byron Brenan

China spent 1901 implementing the Boxer peace protocol, formally concluded on 7 September. The indemnity was fixed at 450,000,000 taels (about £65,000,000) at 4% interest, repayable over 39 years, secured on Maritime Customs, Native Customs at treaty ports, and the Salt Gabelle. Punishment of Boxer leaders was uneven: Princes Tuan and Lan were banished; six officials condemned to death (two executed in Peking); Russia’s minister refused to support further provincial punishments, raising suspicions of a separate Russo-Chinese bargain over Manchuria. Prince Chun travelled to Berlin to apologise for the murder of Baron von Ketteler. The Tsung-li-yamen was abolished and replaced by the new Wai-wupu (Board of Foreign Affairs). The Court lingered at Si-an Fu until October, returning slowly to Peking. Li Hung Chang died on 7 November at age 78, succeeded as Viceroy of Chihli by Yuan Shih-kai, who was emerging as a key figure. Russia separately tried to force a Manchurian treaty granting it de facto control over the region; China, encouraged by Britain, Japan, and others, refused to sign, but Russian troops remained in occupation. A British commercial mission under Sir James Mackay was dispatched to negotiate treaty revisions. Famine in Shensi killed an estimated 2,500,000; Yangtze floods rendered 10,000,000 homeless.

Japan navigated a year of domestic and diplomatic tension. The Ito Cabinet resigned in June over financial and underlying political questions, replaced by Viscount Katsura’s stopgap government. Japan took a strong stand against Russia’s Manchurian manoeuvre, declaring that Manchuria fell within the Anglo-German Agreement to which Japan was a party, and helped dissuade China from signing the Russian treaty. A dispute with foreign residents over land tenure (the ‘perpetual lease’ versus ‘superficies’ question) was resolved by a special Diet act, though a parallel dispute over house taxes within settlements remained unsettled. The Finance Ministry reported a surplus of ¥47,000,000 in ordinary revenue, to be applied alongside Chinese indemnity payments to debt reduction and railway expansion. Railway mileage reached 3,864 (government plus private). An iron foundry opened at Wakamatsu aimed at reducing import dependence. Marquess Ito embarked on a world tour via America, Russia, Germany, and England. Japan’s exclusion under Australia’s language-test immigration bill caused diplomatic friction.

Korea faced anti-foreign fears early in the year, averted by foreign warnings. Intrigues to oust British customs commissioner J. McLeavy Brown were checked by British, Japanese, and American intervention. A French loan syndicate collapsed under foreign opposition. A revolt on Quelpart Island cost hundreds of Catholic converts their lives before French warships restored order. Both Russia and Japan obtained land concessions at Masanpo. A Japanese-Korean company received a rail concession from Seoul to Fusan. Hong Kong separated the offices of Colonial Secretary and Registrar General; plague recurred, prompting a colonial sanitation commission. Wei-hai-wei passed from War Office to Colonial Office control, receiving a commissioner and a High Court. Kiaochow (German) opened its railway to Kaomi and extended it into Shantung. Tongking (French) formed a company to build a railway from Laokai to Yunnan city. Siam enjoyed internal prosperity under King Chulalongkorn — flourishing finances, no public debt, declining crime — but faced French pressure to cede further territory before France would evacuate Chentabun. Siam’s first railway (Bangkok to Korat) opened after eight years, costing double the original estimate. German shipping overtook British tonnage for the first time. A copyright law, new land registration, irrigation improvements, and growing caravan trade with Yunnan marked the year’s progress.

Chapter VII — Africa (with Malta)

South Africa, Egypt and the Soudan, East Africa and Uganda, West and North Africa, Malta By H. Whates

South Africa. The Boer War degenerated into a bitter guerrilla conflict after hopes of a swift peace were dashed. Lord Milner’s February despatch acknowledged retrogression; Boer commandos under De Wet, Botha, and Delarey raided Cape Colony and the annexed territories. Parliamentary institutions remained in abeyance under martial law; the Cape Parliament was repeatedly prorogued. Peace feelers — including Mrs Botha’s mission to Kruger in Europe and the Merriman-Sauer mission to London — proved futile. Lord Kitchener’s August proclamation threatening banishment and confiscation for leaders who did not surrender by 15 September failed to break resistance. The blockhouse system was expanded; mobile columns conducted sweeping drives, but despite heavy losses some 9,000 Boers remained in the field at year’s end. Major actions included Vlakfontein, Moedwill (where Colonel Kekewich was wounded), Fort Itala (where a nineteen-hour assault was repulsed), and the death of Colonel Benson at Brakenlaagte. The refugee camps for Boer women and children suffered terrible mortality — over 1,500 child deaths in August alone — leading to a Ladies’ Committee under Mrs Fawcett and the transfer of camp control from military to civil authorities, with Lord Milner instructed to spare no expense on improvements. Johannesburg began receiving refugees weekly; mining was expected to resume substantially by February 1902; Rhodesian gold output nearly doubled to 170,000 oz.

Egypt and the Soudan. Lord Cromer’s administration continued its steady advance with a budget surplus of E£64,000 and an aggregate surplus of nearly E£10 million since 1887, achieved despite heavy tax reductions and capital expenditure on public works. The Assouan and Assiout dams neared completion; irrigation loans to fellaheen relieved peasant usury. In the Soudan, recovery was slow and depopulated; revenue of E£557,179 for 1900 required an Egyptian contribution of E£417,179. The Khedive visited Khartoum for the first time. Sir William Garstin’s major report on Nile sources recommended damming Lake Tsana in Abyssinia; Lord Cromer cautioned that the immediate priority was a railway from Khartoum to the Red Sea. Anglo-Egyptian forces occupied points in the Bahr-el-Ghazal, sparking French and Belgian protests.

East and West Africa. The Mombassa–Lake Victoria railway reached the lake by year-end, total cost estimated at £5,206,000. Uganda enjoyed unbroken peace. Sir H. H. Johnston’s report identified rubber, coal, iron, and limestone as promising resources. In Somaliland, a joint Anglo-Abyssinian expedition against the Mullah Muhammad Abdullah broke up his forces, though he escaped; the Jubaland expedition failed to capture the murderers of Sub-Commissioner Jenner. In West Africa, the Gambia’s Fodi Kabbah was killed in joint operations; the Gold Coast recovered from the Ashanti war; in Nigeria, expeditions overthrew the Emirs of Kontagaro and Bida, a British resident was established at Yola, and the Aro expedition against Ju-ju centres was underway. French activity was extensive across the Upper Niger and toward Lake Chad. Morocco saw cautious reform under Sultan Mulai Abdul Aziz; France consolidated occupation of the Tuat oasis but faced Tuareg attacks.

Malta. The agitation for self-government, tied to the Italian-language question, came to a head. Elected members refused to vote educational supplies to protest regulations giving parents free choice between English and Italian. Mr Chamberlain’s July despatch firmly rejected the language claim and announced an Order in Council to raise £38,000 annually for public works. A mass meeting of 12,000–15,000 in Valletta on 11 August protested with disorder; two days later corrosive fluid was thrown over the statue of Queen Victoria. By year’s end the agitation was described as ‘smouldering ineffectually’.

Chapter VIII — America

United States and Dependencies (A. Maurice Low), Canada (George R. Parkin), Newfoundland, Mexico and Central America, the Guianas and the West Indies, South America, Pan-American Congress (H. Whates)

United States. President McKinley, inaugurated for his second term on 4 March, was shot by Leon Czolgosz, an American anarchist of Polish extraction, on 6 September at the Pan-American Exhibition in Buffalo; he died on 14 September. Vice-President Theodore Roosevelt took the oath of office, pledging to continue McKinley’s policies and retaining the existing Cabinet. Czolgosz was tried, convicted, and executed by electricity on 29 October. Roosevelt’s first message to Congress called for stronger anti-anarchy legislation, addressed the trust question, affirmed the Monroe Doctrine as cardinal to American foreign policy, and urged naval expansion. The Hay-Pauncefote Treaty, signed in November and ratified in December, superseded the Clayton-Bulwer Treaty and gave the United States exclusive jurisdiction to construct an Isthmian Canal. The Supreme Court ruled that Porto Rico and the Philippines were ‘territory appurtenant’ to the United States, not integral parts of the Union, giving Congress full legislative authority. Civil government was established in the Philippines under Governor William H. Taft; free trade was declared with Porto Rico. Seth Low’s Fusionist ticket defeated Tammany Hall in New York City. The Cuban Constitutional Convention elected Thomas Estrada Palma as president, paving the way for the withdrawal of U.S. forces.

Canada. The second contingent returned from South Africa to acclaim. Queen Victoria’s death prompted profound mourning. Parliament, sitting with a large Liberal majority under new Conservative leader R. L. Borden, produced the largest surplus in Dominion history (8.05million).Aggregateforeigntradereached381.5 million. The decennial census recorded 5,369,666 inhabitants. An abundant western harvest yielded nearly 60 million bushels of wheat. Major capital was applied to large-scale iron production at Sydney (Cape Breton), Collingwood, and Sault Ste. Marie. The Duke and Duchess of Cornwall and York made an extensive Royal tour across the country. A third contingent of 900 men was dispatched to South Africa. Canada warmly welcomed Guglielmo Marconi for transatlantic wireless experiments after cable companies blocked him in Newfoundland. Newfoundland had a quieter year; the cod and seal fisheries produced average results; iron ore output from Bell Island increased substantially. Trade reached record levels — exports of 8.63millionandimportsof7.50 million — yielding the largest surplus in the colony’s history (258,603).Thecensusrecorded220,249inhabitants.TheReidNewfoundlandCompanywasincorporatedwith25 million capital, taking over railway operation and related franchises.

Mexico and Central America. Mexico under President Porfirio Diaz enjoyed peace and advancing prosperity despite silver depreciation; American capital developed mineral wealth; British trade held only 16% of a total trade of £29.98 million (United States held 66%). In Central America, President Zelaya was re-elected in Nicaragua; President Estrada Cabrera maintained order in Guatemala. Revolutionary disturbances in Colombia led the United States to land forces on the Isthmus of Panama to keep transit open. West Indies. The year was uneventful; sugar planters struggled. British Guiana pursued gold and diamond extraction; Jamaica developed a fruit trade with England; Trinidad was prosperous from cocoa; Barbados relied on the North American sugar market; the Leeward Islands continued in depression. The projected sale of the Danish Islands to the United States was not completed.

South America. Venezuela under President Castro fostered confusion with Colombia; German grievances over debt led to German warships at La Guayra, with the United States indicating it would not oppose force for debt collection short of territorial acquisition. Argentina suffered a poor year — British ports closed to Argentine cattle over foot-and-mouth disease — with near-war with Chile over the Andean boundary averted by a Christmas Day protocol agreeing to arbitration by Great Britain. Chile mourned the death of President Errazuriz, electing General German Riesco; gold currency conversion was postponed. Brazil under President Campos Santos maintained peace and resumed specie payments on foreign debt; budget surpluses were recorded. Uruguay faced trade depression and political discontent. The Pan-American Congress convened in Mexico City on 22 October addressed compulsory arbitration, trade relations, and communications. After contentious debate, delegates affirmed adherence to the Hague Convention. The Congress was deemed of academic rather than practical effect.

Chapter IX — Australasia

Australian Commonwealth, New Zealand, Polynesia

The Australian Commonwealth was inaugurated on 1 January 1901, uniting the six colonies as states under a federal union. Edmund Barton became the first Prime Minister, leading a Cabinet of seven — five of whom had been colonial premiers. The Duke of Cornwall and York opened the first Federal Parliament in May. The dominant issues were the new federal tariff (a compromise between Free Trade and Protectionist states), the Immigration Restriction Bill enforcing a ‘White Australia’ policy through a language test excluding Asian and Pacific Islander labour, and a Defence Bill establishing a near-compulsory military system. The Commonwealth took administrative control of British New Guinea and accepted the transfer of the Northern Territory from South Australia. Federal machinery operated more smoothly than expected, though the Register notes disappointment that the new Parliament’s tone and legislative quality did not rise markedly above the old colonial assemblies; nascent secessionist sentiment emerged in Queensland.

New Zealand remained outside the Australian Confederation after a commission reported unanimously against union. Prime Minister Seddon’s government dispatched eight contingents to South Africa. The Duke of York’s visit in June drew enormous crowds, including a Maori war-dance at Rotorua. The Arbitration Act was amended to address defects; a Referendum Bill was rejected by the upper house; penny postage was adopted, straining relations with Australia; the government advanced state ownership with a State-owned coal mine. In Polynesia, friction with France in the New Hebrides escalated over alleged French moves to abolish the Dual Protectorate, prompting the Commonwealth to protest to the Colonial Office. Several violent incidents involving white settlers and missionaries were reported in the New Britain Archipelago and New Guinea.

Chronicle of Events in 1901

The year was dominated by the death of Queen Victoria on 22 January at Osborne and the accession of Edward VII. Her funeral on 2 February was a day of profound mourning across the Empire; the German Emperor remained in England for the ceremonies. The new King opened his first Parliament in March; the Duke and Duchess of Cornwall embarked on a triumphant world tour of the Empire, visiting Australia (where the first Federal Parliament opened), Canada, New Zealand, Mauritius, South Africa, and Newfoundland.

The South African War continued with Lord Kitchener’s September proclamation of banishment for Boer leaders who did not surrender. The Discovery sailed for the Antarctic in August. The incident at Tweefontein on Christmas Day saw a Boer surprise attack that inflicted heavy British losses. President McKinley was assassinated on 6 September; Vice-President Theodore Roosevelt succeeded him. The China peace protocol was signed on 7 September. The Russian-French alliance was cemented by the Tsar’s visit to France. Marconi received the first transatlantic wireless signals from Cornwall to Newfoundland in December. The U.S. Steel Corporation was formed. The Taff Vale judgment made trade unions suable in their registered names. Lord Rosebery’s Chesterfield speech called for Liberal Party reorganisation. The America Cup was retained by the Columbia against Sir Thomas Lipton’s Shamrock II.

Retrospect of Literature

By Lionel Robinson

The year produced few works of exceptional note in history, philosophy, or poetry, though fiction continued to dominate. George Meredith’s poetry volume A Reading of Life and Thomas Hardy’s Poems of the Past and Present were the year’s most important contributions to verse. In biography, Professor Sloane’s Life of Napoleon Bonaparte (four volumes), Barry O’Brien’s Life of Lord Russell of Killowen, and Lady Ilchester’s Life and Letters of Lady Sarah Lennox were notable. Andrew Lang’s The Mystery of Mary Stuart reopened the case of the Scottish queen. The completion of the Dictionary of National Biography supplementary volumes (ed. Stephen and Lee) and Rowland Prothero’s edition of Byron’s Letters and Journals were landmark publications. The war in South Africa produced numerous works, including E. T. Cook’s Rights and Wrongs of the Transvaal War. Geography and travel were enriched by Martin Conway’s Bolivian Andes, Borchgrevinck’s First on the Antarctic Continent, and Captain Deasy’s Tibetan explorations. Herbert Giles’ History of Chinese Literature was the first such work in any language. Bernard Shaw’s Three Plays for Puritans and Pinero’s Iris represented important contributions to dramatic literature.

Science

By J. Reginald Ashworth

In astronomy, a brilliant new star (Nova Persei) was discovered in February by Dr Anderson; a total solar eclipse on 18 May was successfully observed from Mauritius and the Malay Archipelago. Professor Langley continued his bolometric studies of the solar infra-red spectrum; the question of an ultra-Neptunian planet gained support from comet perturbations. The National Physical Laboratory was established at Bushy House. Lebedew experimentally confirmed Maxwell’s theory of light pressure. Wireless telegraphy advanced dramatically — Marconi bridged 200 miles between Cornwall and the Isle of Wight, and by December had reportedly spanned the Atlantic. Duddell discovered that electric arcs could produce musical sounds. The absolute zero of temperature remained elusive though Dewar approached within 9–10 degrees; the new element europium was announced; artificial indigo continued to displace the natural product. Physiology saw Metchnikoff’s work on intestinal microbes, investigations of radium’s effects on skin, and a major effort to fund cancer research, alongside Sir Ernest Cassel’s donation of £200,000 for a tuberculosis sanatorium. Professor Loeb achieved parthenogenetic development of sea-urchin eggs using chemical means; Sir Harry Johnston discovered the okapi in the Upper Congo; Koch controversially asserted that bovine tuberculosis was not transmissible to humans. The British Antarctic Expedition (the Discovery) and German and Swedish expeditions were all in preparation.

Art

The year was not marked by major new reputations but saw the inception of the ambitious National Memorial to Queen Victoria, entrusted to architect Aston Webb and sculptor Thomas Brock. The Glasgow International Exhibition featured a remarkable collection of 19th-century works. The Royal Academy’s summer exhibition was dominated by Sargent’s penetrating portraits, though M. Benjamin Constant’s posthumous portrait of Queen Victoria disappointed critics and public alike. The sensational recovery of the stolen Gainsborough portrait of the Duchess of Devonshire — purchased by Pierpont Morgan — was a highlight, though its authenticity was questioned. Record prices were achieved at art sales: a Hoppner portrait sold for £14,752, and mezzotint prices soared. The National Gallery acquired works by Signorelli, Bordone, Bonfigli, and Thornhill; the Spanish exhibition at the Guildhall attracted enormous crowds.

Drama (and Music)

By John E. Talbot

The year opened with theatres closing in mourning for Queen Victoria. Sir Henry Irving’s season of Coriolanus and his repertoire was moderately successful; Herbert Beerbohm Tree followed Herod with a delightful Twelfth Night and later The Last of the Dandies. Pinero’s Iris at the Garrick was considered the year’s most important new play, ranking with The Second Mrs Tanqueray, with Fay Davis giving a beautiful performance. Wyndham had continued success with Mrs Dane’s Defence and later The Mummy and the Humming Bird. Forbes Robertson had an unfortunate season; Alexander produced The Wilderness and The Awakening. Sarah Bernhardt and Coquelin visited Her Majesty’s with Rostand’s L’Aiglon; Japanese actors Sadi Yacco and Kawakami performed at the Criterion. Among musical comedies, Florodora was succeeded by The Silver Slipper; San Toy finally closed after a two-year run. American imports included Sherlock Holmes (with Gillette) and the revived Belle of New York. The Savoy continued under new management after D’Oyly Carte’s death, with Sullivan’s posthumous The Emerald Isle completed by Edward German.

Obituary of Eminent Persons Deceased in 1901

The most significant death was that of Queen Victoria (22 January, aged 81), whose 64-year reign was the longest in British history. The composer Giuseppe Verdi (27 January, aged 87) was mourned across Italy and the world; his operas included Rigoletto, Il Trovatore, La Traviata, Aïda, Otello, and Falstaff. Ex-King Milan of Servia (11 February) died in Vienna after a turbulent career. Ex-President Benjamin Harrison of the United States (13 March). Other notable figures included Dr Mandell Creighton, Bishop of London and historian (14 January); Frederick W. H. Myers, poet and psychical researcher; the Duc de Broglie, French statesman and historian; the scholar Ignatius Donnelly; the painter Boecklin. The musical deaths included Verdi, Piatti (cellist), Rheinberger, Chrysander, and organists E.J. Hopkins and John Stainer. Leading physicists George F. Fitzgerald, H. A. Rowland, P. G. Tait, and Elisha Gray also passed away.
