Imperialism: A Study (1902)

J. A. Hobson’s study of modern Imperialism, designed to give precision to a term then on everybody’s lips, illustrated mainly from British policy of the last generation, and written as a diagnosis of social pathology rather than as a historical narrative.

Source

Hobson, J. A. Imperialism: A Study. New York: James Pott & Company, 1902. Printed by Ballantyne, Hanson & Co., Edinburgh. Dated from the author’s preface, August 1902.

The book is addressed to “the intelligence of the minority who are content neither to float along the tide of political opportunism nor to submit to the shove of some blind ‘destiny.‘” Hobson states that the study is “distinctively one of social pathology, and no endeavour is made to disguise the malignity of the disease.” Statistics in Part I are taken, when the source is not stated, from the Government’s Statistical Abstracts, reinforced by the Statesman’s Year-book. He thanks the editor of the Financial Reform Almanac for a diagram of British expenditure from 1870, and the editors of the Speaker, the Contemporary Review, the Political Science Quarterly, and the British Friend for permission to embody earlier articles. Gilbert Murray and Herbert Rix read most of the proof-sheets.

Part I traces “the economic origins of Imperialism” with such statistical measurements as are available. Part II investigates Imperialism “regarded as a ‘mission of civilisation,’ in its effects upon ‘lower’ or alien peoples, and its political and moral reactions upon the conduct and character of the Western nations engaging in it.” Great Britain “has travelled so much faster and farther along this road as to furnish in her recent career the most profitable guidance or warning.”

Contents

Preface

Hobson writes that Imperialism has been adopted as a more or less conscious policy by several European States and “threatens to break down the political isolation of the United States.” The book seeks general principles, but the illustration is mainly British and proceeds “rather by diagnosis than by historical description.” Readers who think a well-balanced judgment consists in always finding as much in favour of any political course as against it “will be discontented with the treatment given here.”

Introductory — Nationalism and Imperialism

Amid “the welter of vague political abstractions,” Imperialism cannot be pinned down with the rigour of an exact science. The nearest approach to definition is a “certain broad consistency” in its relations to nationalism, internationalism, and colonialism — “equally elusive, equally shifty.”

The nineteenth century’s dominant struggle was nationalism: the establishment of political union on a basis of nationality. It was disruptive in Greece, Servia, Roumania, Bulgaria, and North Italy; unifying in Italy and in Pan-Slavism; federal in Germany and North America. By the close of the third quarter of the century Europe was “fairly settled into large national States or federations,” though Italy still looked to Trieste and Germany to Austria. Hobson quotes J. S. Mill on nationality as common sympathies generated by race, language, religion, geography, and above all “identity of political antecedents.”

It is “a debasement of this genuine nationalism, by attempts to overflow its natural banks and absorb the near or distant territory of reluctant and unassimilable peoples, that marks the passage from nationalism to a spurious colonialism on the one hand, Imperialism on the other.” Colonialism in its best sense — migration to vacant or sparsely peopled lands, carrying citizenship or self-government in close conformity with the mother country — is “a genuine expansion of nationality.” The only distant colonies that clearly meet that test are the self-governing British colonies in Australasia and Canada. Cape Colony and Natal, where most whites are not of British descent and “subject or ‘inferior’ races” vastly preponderate, already show the conflict of colonial and imperial ideas: Lord Rosmead called the permanent imperial factor “simply an absurdity,” and Rhodes spoke of its “elimination.” The normal British “colony” is a small white minority wielding sway over alien subjects under despotic imperial control — and that is “well-nigh universal in the colonies of other European countries.” The German and French “colonial” parties are “identical in general aim and method with the ‘imperialist’ party in England.” Seeley is quoted: when a State advances beyond the limits of nationality, “its power becomes precarious and artificial.”

The novelty of recent Imperialism is “its adoption by several nations. The notion of a number of competing empires is essentially modern.” Ancient and medieval empire imagined a federation covering the recognised world — Rome’s pax Romana, the Holy Roman Empire, Charlemagne, Charles V., Peter, Catherine, Napoleon. Eighteenth-century cosmopolitanism (Lessing, Kant, Goethe, Rousseau, Condorcet, Priestley, Gibbon, Franklin) withered before nineteenth-century nationalism. “Nationalism is a plain highway to internationalism”; Imperialism is its perversion, transforming “the wholesome stimulative rivalry of varied national types into the cut-throat struggle of competing empires.” Aggressive Imperialism also stimulates “a corresponding excess of national self-consciousness” in weaker peoples: “We have welded Africanderdom into just such a strong dangerous nationalism, and we have joined with other nations in creating a resentful nationalism hitherto unknown in China.”

The scramble for Africa and Asia has recast European policy, drawn the United States from isolation, and become “a constant agent of menace and of perturbation to the peace and progress of mankind.” It produces popular doctrines of destiny and “imperial missions of civilisation” while evolving a “calculating, greedy type of Macchiavellianism, entitled ‘real-politik’ in Germany.” The sliding scale of diplomatic language — hinterland, sphere of interest, sphere of influence, paramountcy, suzerainty, protectorate, “lease,” “rectification of frontier,” “concession” — is “the invention and expression of this cynical spirit of Imperialism.”

Part I — The Economics of Imperialism

Chapter I — The Measure of Imperialism

“Quibbles about the modern meaning of the term Imperialism are best resolved by reference to concrete facts in the history of the last thirty years.” European nations, Britain first, have annexed or asserted sway over vast portions of Africa and Asia and numerous Pacific islands. Official lists cannot profess strict accuracy: hinterland slides into protectorate; frontiers are “rectified”; paper partitions remain obscure.

Hobson includes Egypt as a “veiled Protectorate” with its Soudanese claim, Chartered Company territories, and Indian native or feudatory States that admit a British Agent. For so small a nation to add in one generation an area of 4,754,000 square miles with an estimated 88,000,000 people is “a historical fact of great significance.” Accepting Giffen’s estimate of the Empire (including Egypt and the Soudan) at about 13,000,000 square miles and 400–420 million people (about 50 million of British race and speech), one-third of the Empire by area and one-fourth by population has been acquired within the last generation.

Though 1870 is taken as the start of a conscious policy, “the movement did not attain its full impetus until the middle of the eighties.” From about 1884, “the method of wholesale partition” assigned great African tracts; within fifteen years some three and three-quarter million square miles were added. A compiled table for 1884–1900 lists New Guinea, Nigeria, Pondoland, Somaliland, Bechuanaland, Upper Burma, British East Africa, Zululand, Sarawak, Pahang, Rhodesia, Zanzibar, British Central Africa, Uganda, Ashantee, Wei-hai-wei, Kow-lung, the Soudan, and the Transvaal and Orange River Colony — some 3,711,957 square miles and 57 million people.

Britain does not stand alone. “The leading characteristic of modern Imperialism, the competition of rival Empires, is the product of this same period.” The close of the Franco-German war marks a new colonial policy in France and Germany. Hobson prints parallel lists of French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Spanish, Russian, and (after 1898) American acquisitions, to show that the scramble is a system of competing empires, not a British peculiarity. The character of the new lands is tropical or sub-tropical, thinly settled by whites, and held by protectorate, charter, or “paramountcy” rather than by genuine colonisation.

Chapter II — The Commercial Value of Imperialism

The absorption of public interest, energy, blood, and money in colonial possessions and foreign markets would seem to show that Britain lives mainly by external trade. “Now this is not the case.” National income is put at about £1,700,000,000 a year. Import and export trade in 1898 (the latest “normal” year before the war) was £765,000,000; at a liberal 5 per cent. profit that is a little over £38,000,000, “or about one forty-fifth part of our total income.”

It is a fallacy to treat the whole £233,000,000 of exported home produce as income dependent on external trade. Capital and labour need not have remained idle without that outlet. “There is no necessary limit to the quantity of capital and labour which can be employed in producing goods for the home market.” “With everything that is produced a corresponding power to purchase and consume is created.” The pressure for external markets “is not based on any natural economic necessity.”

During the first seven decades of the century, before a continuous imperialist policy, foreign trade advanced faster than home trade. In the three decades of conscious struggle for external markets, external trade has not kept pace with internal trade. Between 1870 and 1898 national income rose from about £1,200,000,000 to £1,700,000,000; income per head rose by as much as 20 per cent., while the value of external trade per head “has actually shrunk.”

Does trade follow the flag? Percentages of trade with foreign countries versus British possessions are “virtually the same” in 1855–59 and 1895–98. Vast additions to British possessions since 1870 have not increased the proportion of intra-imperial trade. Alleyne Ireland’s investigation “strikes a still heavier blow”: colonial imports and exports have increased faster than trade with the United Kingdom. “While Great Britain’s dependence on her Empire for trade is stationary, the dependence of her Empire upon her for trade is rapidly diminishing.”

Closer figures for 1884–98 show imperial trade oscillating without a rising trend commensurate with the new map. The most profitable and progressive trade is with rival industrial nations, not with the new tropical annexations. Recent acquisitions furnish “poor and precarious markets.” Hobson concludes that the commercial case for the new Imperialism fails on the figures.

Chapter III — Imperialism as an Outlet for Population

A “widely prevalent belief” holds that expansion is needed to absorb surplus population. Emigrants are “the strongest and most energetic stuff”; they must settle under the flag; therefore political control must be extended over their new homes. The same process creates an “outlander” nucleus wherever trade and capital go, and the duty of protecting British subjects is identified with protecting “the far larger stakes of the home investors.”

Hobson asks whether England is over-populated. Britain is not so thickly peopled as certain prosperous areas in Germany, the Netherlands, and China. Growth of wealth has outstripped growth of population. Congestion on industrial spots is not over-population in the Malthusian sense. The two latest censuses show a falling rate of increase such that, if the same forces continue, “the population of Great Britain will be stationary by the middle of the century.”

Even if a surplus must emigrate, it is not necessary to seize new territory for it. Official tables of outward passengers of British and Irish origin from 1884 show the great majority going to the United States, not to new tropical possessions. Net emigration, after allowing for travellers and for immigration, averages only 87,224 a year in the last five years. “Other places” — the entire non-European world outside Canada, Australasia, and South Africa — absorb “at most a very few thousands,” and industrial settlers in the new tropical dominions are “a mere handful.”

“No substantial settlement of Britons is taking place upon any of the areas of the Empire acquired since 1870, excepting the Transvaal and the Orange River Colony.” Tropical climate makes genuine colonisation impossible. A few soldiers, officials, engineers, missionaries, prospectors, and overseers get temporary posts. “The new Empire is even more barren for settlement than for profitable trade.”

Chapter IV — Economic Parasites of Imperialism

If the Imperialism of the last three decades is “clearly condemned as a business policy” — enormous expense for “a small, bad, unsafe increase of markets,” and the resentment of other nations — why does the nation embark upon it? “The business interests of the nation as a whole are subordinated to those of certain sectional interests that usurp control of the national resources and use them for their private gain.” More is quoted from Sir Thomas More: “Everywhere do I perceive a certain conspiracy of rich men seeking their own advantage under the name and pretext of the commonwealth.”

Wars are presented to the public as defensive. Irrational from the standpoint of the whole nation, Imperialism “is rational enough from the standpoint of certain classes.” A socialist State that kept honest books, or an intelligent laissez-faire democracy that weighted all interests equally, would discard it. A State in which “certain well-organised business interests are able to outweigh the weak, diffused interest of the community” will pursue their policy.

Direct outcomes include expenditure on ships, guns, and stores; public loans and Bourse fluctuations; posts for soldiers, sailors, and diplomats; substitution of the British flag over foreign investments; markets for certain exports; employment for engineers, missionaries, miners, ranchers. A minimum peacetime armament bill of £60,000,000 flows “directly to the tills of certain big firms” — warships, guns, ammunition, coal, horses, clothing, barracks. Shipbuilding, boiler-making, and gun trades are conducted by large firms “well aware of the uses of political influence.” With them stand export manufacturers of Manchester, Sheffield, and Birmingham, and bodies such as the Imperial South African Association and the China League. Shipping claims State subsidies “for purposes of imperial safety.” The services are imperialist by profession; abolition of purchase enlarged the army as a feeder of imperial sentiment; frontier officers’ “itch for glory and adventure” has been “a most prolific source of expansion in India.” The Indian Civil Service and colonial posts make the colonies, in James Mill’s phrase, “a vast system of outdoor relief for the upper classes.” Sir Charles Crosthwaite on Siam: how to find markets “and also employment for those superfluous articles of the present day, our boys.”

“By far the most important economic factor in Imperialism is the influence relating to investments.” Income-tax figures for identified foreign income nearly double from 1884 to 1900 (£33.8 million to £60.3 million). Giffen put foreign profit, interest, and pensions at £70 million in 1882 and £90 million in 1899; Hobson thinks £120 million nearer the truth if unidentified items keep their old proportion. Mulhall’s series of foreign and colonial investments runs from £144 million in 1862 to £1,698 million in 1893 — about 15 per cent. of the United Kingdom’s wealth, much of it in government loans, railways, and other politically affected services. By the end of the century the total “cannot be set down at a lower figure than £2,000,000,000.”

Two facts follow: income as interest on foreign investments “enormously exceeds” profits on ordinary external trade; and while trade grows slowly, the investment income grows rapidly. “It is not too much to say that the modern foreign policy of Great Britain is primarily a struggle for profitable markets of investment.” Britain is “becoming a nation living upon tribute from abroad.” Cui bono? “The first and most obvious answer is, The investor.” Giffen’s 2 per cent. on a £800 million turnover gives only £18 million as profits on external trade for 1899; set beside £90–100 million “pure profit upon investments,” “we understand whence the economic impulse to Imperialism is derived.”

Still more dangerous is the financier, for whom stocks are material for speculation. Banking, broking, bill-discounting, loan-floating, and company-promoting form “the central ganglion of international capitalism,” “controlled, so far as Europe is concerned, chiefly by men of a single and peculiar race.” “Does any one seriously suppose that a great war could be undertaken by any European State, or a great State loan subscribed, if the house of Rothschild and its connections set their face against it?” Public debts, new companies, and fluctuating values carry them “on the side of Imperialism.” They are “essentially parasites upon patriotism.”

Chapter V — Imperialism Based on Protection

A business nation should set increased costs against increased takings. Comparing armaments and war since 1884 with the value of colonial trade, Hobson saddles the new Imperialism with the whole increase of military outlay. Had Britain practised “the full practice of Cobdenism” — defensive as to the existing Empire, abstinent as to new territory — armaments need not have risen. The new expenditure is “a huge business blunder,” “a millstone which must eventually drag her down.”

The markets forced are “small, precarious, and unprofitable.” The assumption that trade lost to a Protectionist rival is trade lost absolutely “shows a blind ignorance of the elements of international trade.” France in Madagascar or Germany in Shan-tung may cut direct British trade, but roundabout trade and British shipping still share the gain. “The ‘open door’ is an advantage to our trade, but not a necessity.” Recent ventures “conform to a law of diminishing returns.” “Why should we do all the work and get so little from it?”

“Imperialism repudiates Free Trade, and rests upon an economic basis of Protection. Just in so far as an Imperialist is logical does he become an open and avowed Protectionist.” An appendix table of national expenditure on army, navy, war charges, and the total budget from 1895 to 1903 shows the South African and China wars lifting the bill toward £165 million over three years to March 1902, of which some £119 million was added to the Debt.

Chapter VI — The Economic Taproot of Imperialism

Facts and figures will not of themselves dispel “the popular delusion” that force to secure new markets is sound policy. The Imperialist argument is stated at length: we must have markets for manufactures, “new outlets for the investment of our surplus capital,” and employment for an adventurous surplus of population; England’s old manufacturing monopoly is gone; Germany, the United States, and Belgium encroach even in British possessions; new markets lie in the tropics; protectorates and annexation are the safest means; the cost is a capital outlay for posterity. “Imperialism is thus seen to be, not a choice, but a necessity.”

The United States illustrates the “practical force” of this argument: a sudden break with isolation, “risking the principles and practices of liberty and equality.” Not mere “spread-eaglism,” but an industrial revolution of two decades, trusts, and “automatic saving” on an unprecedented scale. “The power of production has far outstripped the actual rate of consumption.” Trusts limit the capital that can be employed at home and increase the profits from which fresh savings spring. Industrial princes “are faced with the dilemma of either spending more than they know how to spend, or forcing markets outside the home area” — dumping surplus goods, or becoming a creditor class abroad. “It is Messrs. Rockefeller, Pierpont Morgan, Hanna, Schwab, and their associates who need Imperialism.” Cuba, the Philippines, and Hawaii “are but the hors d’œuvre to whet an appetite for an ampler banquet.”

The same needs exist in Europe. The 1885 Commission on the Depression of Trade reported capacity for production “in excess of our requirements,” due partly to “the competition of the capital which is being steadily accumulated.” “It is this economic condition of affairs that forms the taproot of Imperialism.” If the consuming public raised its standard with every rise of productive power, there would be no excess of goods or capital “clamorous to use Imperialism.”

“With everything that is produced a consuming power is born.” If goods and capital cannot get employed, “the only possible explanation of this paradox is the refusal of owners of consuming power to apply that power in effective demand.” The root questions are: why consumption fails to keep pace with production; “Why does under-consumption or over-saving occur?” Over-saving is not all saving, but saving in excess of what can be employed in producing goods that will be consumed. The cause is distribution: some have consuming power “vastly in excess of needs,” others lack enough for “physical efficiency.” Wages, based on cost of living not on efficiency, keep the workers’ channel “in rock-bound banks.” The rich cannot spend enough on luxuries; their “overflow” of savings floods production.

“Imperialism is the endeavour of the great controllers of industry to broaden the channel for the flow of their surplus wealth by seeking foreign markets and foreign investments to take off the goods and capital they cannot sell or use at home.” It is not industrial progress that demands expansion, “but mal-distribution of consuming power.” Over-saving consists of “rents, monopoly profits, and other unearned or excessive elements of income.” Divert that excess to workers as wages or to the community as taxes, and “there will be no need to fight for foreign markets.” Rowntree’s figures show more than one-fourth of town population below bare physical efficiency. “Social reform” is the remedy; “the home markets are capable of indefinite expansion.”

Chapter VII — Imperialist Finance

Imperialism “implies the use of the machinery of government by private interests, mainly capitalist, to secure for them economic gains outside their country.” A diagram of national expenditure from 1870 shows growth faster than foreign trade or national income. Ordinary imperial expenditure rose from £87,423,000 in 1888 to £128,600,000 in 1900. Roughly three-quarters of revenue goes to naval and military expenditure and military debts; about five shillings in the pound remain for education, civil government, and grants in aid of local taxation. Army and navy cost less than £24 million of £65 million in 1875, nearly £43 million of £99 million in 1898. Growth from about 25 to 60 millions in a quarter-century “is the most significant fact of imperialist finance.”

The classes who form “the economic core of Imperialism” extract these sums “to improve their investments and open up new fields for capital.” Grants in aid of local taxation are largely “a subsidy to landowners.” The Church is purchased by relief of rates on tithes and grants for Church schools; the liquor trade by “masterly inaction” on temperance. Metal and shipbuilding towns depend on government contracts; Members “freely employ their influence to secure contracts.”

On the tax side, those who use the public purse for private gain must not fill it from their own pockets. A sane system would tax unearned increments and protected profits — “precisely those elements which constitute the economic taproot of Imperialism.” Direct taxation of incomes for Imperialism would be impossible under popular government. “The people must pay, but they must not know they are paying.” Hence Imperialism “makes everywhere for indirect taxation; not chiefly on grounds of convenience, but for purposes of concealment.” England’s income-tax is the exception that proves the rule: Free Trade made cheap raw materials so profitable that manufacturers accepted Peel’s income-tax; the Crimean War made it permanent; after 1886 Harcourt’s progressive tax and death duties were possible because reverting to Protection was still hard and the new democracy had to be conciliated. On the Continent, Militarism and Imperialism thrive on tariffs. In Britain the propertied Government has “almost shaken itself free from the traditions of Free Trade.” “Panem et circenses interpreted into English means cheap booze and Mafficking.”

Part II — The Politics of Imperialism

Chapter I — The Political Significance of Imperialism

A passage from Morris’s History of Colonisation is taken as typical ignorance: that England’s “fixed rule” is to promote colonial interests and “eventually to elevate” dependencies “from the position of inferiority to that of association,” spreading free self-government. Hobson calls this “quite the largest misstatement of the facts of our colonial and imperial policy that is possible.”

Of some 367 million British subjects outside the British Isles, “not more than ten millions, or one in thirty-seven, have any real self-government.” Only Australasia and North America have responsible representative government as a reality — and even there outlanders and servile labour qualify democracy. In Cape Colony and Natal the franchise “will remain virtually a white monopoly.” Modified Crown colonies (Jamaica, Barbados, Trinidad, Bahamas, British Guiana, the Windwards, Bermudas, Malta, Mauritius, Ceylon) elect a portion of the legislature, but the elected never outnumber the official element; the Colonial Office veto is freely used; a high property franchise excludes most coloured people. Their population in 1898 was about 5,700,000.

The overwhelming majority live under Crown colony government or protectorates, with no important political rights of British citizens. In Crown colonies the governor is absolute. In African and Asian protectorates there is “no tincture of British representative government.” Chartered Companies allow “business men, animated avowedly by business ends,” to govern natives. Indian feudatory States leave internal administration to princes under a veto. “Where British government is real, it does not carry freedom or self-government; where it does carry a certain amount of freedom and self-government, it is not real.” Not five per cent. of the Empire’s population possesses the liberties that are “the basis of British civilisation.”

India and Egypt are “in all essential features” to be classed as Crown colonies. Officials who administer the Empire from Downing Street or on the spot do not believe the ruled can be trained for effective free self-government. Indian success consists in “good order and justice administered autocratically.” Experiments on British municipal lines are generally admitted to be failures. Variety of laws and forms — Spanish law in Trinidad, Dutch in Demerara, the Cape, and Ceylon, French in Lower Canada and St. Lucia — is “elasticity” or “haphazard opportunism,” but it is not the spread of distinctively British free government. Government of the vast majority is “based, not on the consent of the governed, but upon the will of imperial officials.”

The new Imperialism thus increases the unfree proportion of the Empire. It reacts on Britain itself: militarism, a growing official caste, party truces on imperial questions, and the weakening of genuine popular control over foreign policy. Representative government at home cannot remain healthy while the nation rules hundreds of millions by methods “antithetic to the methods of government which we most value for ourselves.”

Chapter II — The Scientific Defence of Imperialism

Ambitions of individuals and nations have been the chief conscious motives, but some claim that “larger hidden forces” work toward human progress. Biologists’ hold on sociology makes it natural to apply struggle and selection to man; a reaction against Huxley and Wallace’s separation of ethical from cosmic process leads others to treat lower physical struggle as “sufficient for all purposes of sociology.”

Karl Pearson is quoted: a vigorous race must be kept up by contest, “chiefly by way of war with inferior races, and with equal races by the struggle for trade routes and for the sources of raw material and of food supply” — “the natural history view of mankind.” Edmond Demolins treats European appropriation of Asia, Africa, and Oceania as a law “as indisputable as the law of gravitation.” Giddings, Kidd, and others assign highest “social efficiency” to Teutonic, especially Anglo-Saxon, races.

“This genuine and confident conviction about ‘social efficiency’ must be taken as the chief moral support of Imperialism.” Closely stated, “socially efficient” means “strong in the struggle of life,” “capacity to beat other races.” It is “survival of the fittest” with the question “Fittest to do what?” answered “Fittest to survive.” From “it has been the prime condition of progress” men glide to “it ought to go on.” Imperialism is this doctrine “regarded from the standpoint of one’s own nation,” soon dressed as “imperial Christianity” and a “mission of civilisation” to teach “the arts of good government” and “the dignity of labour.”

“The power to do anything constitutes a right and even a duty to do it” is “the most ‘natural’ of temperamental fallacies.” Carlyle, Kingsley, and Ruskin fostered a “divine right” of force. Hobson is anxious to do justice to “the nobler aspects” of imperial sentiment — Hubert Hervey of the Chartered Company as Imperialism “at its best in action” — but insists that natural-history struggle does not yield a moral title to “work the unutilised resources of earth.” Higher psychical laws, intra-group ethics, and the possibility of substituting industrial and intellectual competition for race war are set against the “crude struggle on the larger plane.” The scientific defence, stripped of ethical finery, is a restatement of force.

Chapter III — Moral and Sentimental Factors

Why does Imperialism “escape general recognition for the narrow, sordid thing it is”? Neighbours are not deceived; they charge Britain with hypocrisy. The charge is false: “no nation sees its own shortcomings.” A genuine, though not large, body wishes to spread Christianity, diminish cruelty, and “do good work about the world.” Churches contain a passionate few and a larger sympathetic number who believe civilisation is a “portable commodity” and that “a certain amount of compulsion is justified.”

Selfish forces “utilise the protective colours of these disinterested movements.” They do not always consciously invent them; they “attach to themselves any strong, genuine elevated feeling which is of service.” Salisbury “really believes” the South African war was undertaken for the people of South Africa; Grey thinks the Chartered Company improves Rhodesia; Leopold claims for the Congo “the moral and material regeneration of the country.” “It is difficult to set any limit upon the capacity of men to deceive themselves.”

“It is precisely in this falsification of the real import of motives that the gravest vice and the most signal peril of Imperialism reside.” Gains alleged for Indian natives are assigned as moral justification of an Empire whose steps were “our own political and commercial aggrandisement.” Egypt was occupied for military and financial reasons; it is now said “we went there” to benefit the fellah, and that it would be wicked to keep the pledge of withdrawal. No one proposes to coerce Russia over Finland, or effectively to protect Armenia, though Britain was pledged by the Cyprus Convention: “the ‘cause of humanity’ and the ‘mission of civilisation’ were powerless.” Aggressive Imperialism “is virtually confined to the coercion” of those “weaker and incapable of effective resistance.”

Hobson then treats the machinery of popular sentiment: the press, the music-hall, Jingoism, “Mafficking,” the education of children in a vulgar pride of possession, the conversion of Christianity into an imperial creed (“a going out to all the world to preach the gospel” in the Archbishop of Canterbury’s sense), and the use of patriotism to “cover domination and the love of adventure.” The moral currency of the nation is debased when the least potent motive is selected for public prominence because it is “the most presentable.”

Chapter IV — Imperialism and the Lower Races

The work of expansion is not complete. Formal partition often “marks rather the beginning of a process of imperialisation.” Intensive tightening of control over spheres and protectorates is “as important and as perilous” as extensive growth. Britain, Germany, France, and Russia have “bitten off huge mouthfuls of Africa and Asia which are not yet chewed, digested, or assimilated.” Persia, Tibet, Siam, Afghanistan, Turkish Asia Minor, the paper partition of Central Africa, South America after the American entrance, and above all China, remain. Modern Imperialism “is not ended but only just begun,” and is concerned “almost wholly with the rival claims of Empires to dominate ‘lower races.‘”

Mere laissez faire is “impracticable” and “ethically indefensible.” Absolute autonomy does not carry far; there is no more absolute nationalism among nations than absolute individualism in a nation. Necessity — a Nile or Niger dammed, famine and unused land across a border — “knows no laws.” From that incline Kidd, Giddings, and “Fabian Imperialists” expand a plea of material necessity for control of the tropics. Western standards depend on tropical products (sugar, tea, coffee, rubber, rice, tobacco). Natives, it is said, will not develop resources: Kidd is quoted that “there will be no development of the resources of the tropics under native government.”

Hobson will not accept that as a blank cheque. If interference is ever justified, it must be for the world and for the inhabitants, not for the private gain of the interfering Power; it must be international in sanction, not the prize of a scramble; and it must not destroy the conditions of self-development. Forced labour, land alienation, drink, and the “educating” of civilised wants as a cloak for markets are examined as the actual methods of tropical empire. Chartered companies combine government with dividends. The “dignity of labour” taught to the black man is too often labour for white concessionaires. Sound trusteeship would be rare, temporary, and accountable; competing Imperialism is the opposite.

Chapter V — Imperialism in Asia

“The great test of Western Imperialism is Asia,” where peoples inherit civilisations “as complex as our own, more ancient and more firmly rooted.” African races can be called savages or children on the same road; it is “not so easy to make a specious case” for India and China. Save in recent physical science and industry, they are not simply “backward”; “arrested” may only confess ignorance of their pace, or even mark a completed adjustment to a stable environment.

The Western claim to civilise the East by supremacy assumes that civilisations share a common moral and intellectual core which the West possesses pre-eminently and can impart by government. India is “the most serviceable test” and “the best record British Imperialism can show.” Peace wider than since Alexander; fairer justice; regulated taxation; schools, missions, roads, railways, canals, irrigation; mines and mills; new crops; some loosening of superstition and caste. “No such intelligent, well-educated, and honourable body of men has ever been employed by any State in the working of imperial government as is contained in the Civil Service of India.”

Yet economic prosperity of the masses is not secured. Critics say one of the poorest countries bears a very expensive government; “one-third of the money raised by taxation flows out of the country without return”; India supports an excessive army and even the cost of wars elsewhere; interest on invested capital is spent abroad. The statistical basis is “too insecure” for the strongest drain theory, but even officials favourable to British rule admit that considerable prosperity has not been given. A long quotation (a peasant Empire, ninety per cent. on the land, millions struggling on half an acre) concludes that “except in the irrigated tracts, famine is chronic—endemic.” A century of able, well-meaning rule “has not materially assisted to ward off the chronic enemy, starvation.” Machine industry is not clearly civilising. Political training for self-government is not the real aim. The experiment does not prove that the West can confer the essentials of its civilisation by political supremacy.

China is treated as the coming field of the same forces: commercial treaties, leases, railway and mining concessions, “a growing process of political interference.” An old, cohesive civilisation, if broken by competing empires, will generate “a resentful nationalism hitherto unknown.” Western control there would not be a civilising trusteeship but a partition of markets and concessions.

Chapter VI — Imperial Federation

British policy since 1870, and especially since 1885, has been absorbed in subjugating territories “where no genuine white settlement of any magnitude is contemplated.” That policy “implies a progressive diminution of freedom in the British Empire.” How does it react on the self-governing colonies? Will they move toward independence, or toward federation of equal States? “The present relations will not be maintained.”

Hitherto the tendency has been “steady consistent increase of self-government.” Seventeen self-governing colonies exist in Australasia, North America, and South Africa. Britain “has in the main learned well the lesson of the American Revolution.” In 1873 the last bond of the old plantation policy was removed by allowing Australian differential duties; the Australian Commonwealth Act of 1900 narrows Privy Council control and permits a federal armed force — “a new substantial basis for a possible national independence.” A future South African Commonwealth, if racial animosities abate, would enlarge self-government further.

Yet the imperial statesmen who favoured group federation — Carnarvon after the Canadian Dominion, Chamberlain later — had in view a closer political association with the mother country. Carnarvon’s forcing process in South Africa failed; twenty years later Chamberlain, “confronted by the same essential difficulties, the forcible annexation of the two Dutch Republics, and the coercion of Cape Colony,” pressed federation again. Group federation is centripetal among the colonies, centrifugal from the Imperial Government. Effective imperial federation would reverse “hitherto dominant tendencies.”

Chamberlain’s interest is dated from the Home Rule struggle of 1886: he looked to federation so that Ireland might “really remain an integral part of the Empire.” A voluntary federation of free British States “is in itself eminently desirable” and might be a step toward wider federation. The real question is what self-interest would induce Britain and the colonial groups “to reverse the centrifugal process.” Hobson finds the new tropical Imperialism a barrier, not a bond: the self-governing colonies will not share the cost and risk of ruling unassimilable tropical millions for the sake of City investments and Downing Street prestige. Protective imperial zollverein schemes collide with colonial manufacturing interests and with Britain’s remaining Free Trade. A white “imperial” federation that excluded the coloured majority would not be the Empire; one that included it would not be free. Inter-Imperialism of a few great federal empires, “each with a retinue of uncivilised dependencies,” is a vision of peace he will not accept as the natural next stage.

Chapter VII — The Outcome

If Imperialism is not “a blind inevitable destiny,” can expansion as chosen policy be stopped? Its power is “a natural outcome of an unsound theory in our foreign policy”: that any British subject who ventures person or property in a foreign State can call upon the nation to protect or avenge him. That doctrine places the military, political, and financial resources of the nation “at the beck and call” of missionary societies, reckless explorers, speculative traders, and mining prospectors. Unscrupulous statesmen seize alleged outrages as pretexts for punitive expeditions. With some four hundred million British subjects, “the prospects of a genuine pax Britannica are not particularly bright.”

Graver still are “modern methods of international capitalism and finance.” The well-to-do derive “a large and ever larger proportion of their incomes from capital invested outside the British Empire,” and tend “to use their political power as citizens of this State to interfere with the political condition of those States where they have an industrial stake.” At home an investor takes political risk as part of the bargain; abroad he claims the army against risks already discounted in the terms of the investment. “Can anything be more palpably unfair?” Recent history, “consisting chiefly of the admissions of the mining capitalists” in South Africa, shows the claim enforced. The stock speculator’s interest “lies in the oscillations of paper values, which require fluctuation and insecurity of political conditions as their instrument.”

Safety requires “an absolute repudiation of the right of British subjects to call upon their Government to protect their persons or property from injuries or dangers incurred on their private initiative.” Failing so complete a reversal, two rules: never interfere on general grounds of foreign misgovernment outside treaty rights, submitting interpretation to arbitration; and if force is used to secure treaty rights, confine it to the specific object.

Imperialism, with militarism, oligarchy, bureaucracy, protection, concentration of capital, and violent trade fluctuations, is “the supreme danger of modern national States.” Remedy lies only in “a genuine democracy, the direction of public policy by the people for the people through representatives over whom they exercise a real control.” Until external policy is “broad-based upon a people’s will” there is little hope. Imperialism is “only beginning to realise its full resources”: a wide franchise and an education that is “an uncritical ability to read printed matter” favour “keen business politicians” who control press, schools, and where necessary churches.

The chief economic source remains “the inequality of industrial opportunities” by which a favoured class accumulates superfluous income that “press[es] ever farther afield.” Imperialism secures private benefits “at the public cost” while “sustaining the general cause of conservatism by diverting public energy and interest from domestic agitation to external employment.” “To term Imperialism a national policy is an impudent falsehood.” “Every enlargement of Great Britain in the tropics is a distinct enfeeblement of true British nationalism.” Forcible suppression of small nationalities is not the road to world-federation. Effective internationalism requires strong, self-respecting nationalities, not competing empires.

Index

A full alphabetical index (pages 391 ff.) lists persons, places, and topics — armaments, Chartered Companies, Egypt, India, investments, Protection, South Africa, tropic products, the United States — and can serve as a map of the book’s concrete illustrations. It is not needed as a separate argument.

Hobson’s 1902 text thus measures the new Imperialism by area and population, denies that it pays as trade or settlement, locates its “economic taproot” in surplus capital and mal-distributed consuming power, names the parasites who live by it, and follows the same forces into autocratic empire, scientific and missionary apology, Asia, federation schemes, and the corruption of democracy at home.
