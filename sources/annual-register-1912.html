The Annual Register 1912

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Before the Session

The political year opened under the shadow of industrial unrest, the approaching operation of the National Insurance Act, and the Government’s declared intention to press Home Rule and Welsh Disestablishment under the Parliament Act. Mr. Lloyd George, defending his finance in the City, insisted that appointments under the Labour Exchanges Act had been made by a mixed committee including a Civil Servant, an ex-Labour member and a Tariff Reform Commission member, that the few hundreds of officials under the Finance Act had been appointed by the Inland Revenue (the vast majority, he understood, Unionists), and that under the Insurance Act the Government had appointed only the Commissioners, officially treating political solicitation as a disqualification. Sir Robert Morant had been associated with an Education Act more hated by English Liberals and Nonconformists than any since the Five Mile Act. The new land taxes, he said, had produced not 20,000l. but 337,000l., collection costing 16,000l.; valuation was not collection. As a financial instrument the Budget of 1909 had been a complete success: faced in his first year with a prospective deficit of 25,000,000l. to 30,000,000l., the Liberal Government had in six years, despite exceptional charges, reduced the debt by 61,000,000l. Unemployment had fallen since the year before that Budget; exports and imports had increased. Consols had fallen, but so had other Governments’ securities; between 1898 and 1905 Consols had fallen twenty-one points, under the Liberal régime twelve. Causes included the South African War, the Irish Land Act, reduction of interest while securities generally rose, extension of trustee securities from some 300,000,000l. before 1899 to 1,800,000,000l. in 1912, and investment in brewery stocks. The fall of Consols demanded judicial inquiry; there had been a campaign to decry British credit; the corner-stone of sound finance was “peace on earth and goodwill among men.”

A few days earlier the general desire for improved relations with Germany had been expressed at a Glasgow meeting called by the Lord Provost (29 January), addressed by Sir Frank Lascelles and heartily supported by Lord Rosebery, and by the Bishop of Winchester at his Diocesan Conference (31 January), who stated from personal knowledge that the events of the past summer had left a deep sense of grievance even among former friends of England. On the industrial side the miners’ demands for a minimum wage already overshadowed the session; the Master of Elibank and the Chancellor of the Exchequer defended the Insurance Act at a great meeting in the London Opera House against Unionist attacks which, the Master said, had in Parliament tried to destroy the measure and in the country misrepresented it, while a Liberal Insurance Committee had been formed to answer those misrepresentations.

Mr. Churchill’s visit to Belfast, opposed by Ulster Unionists who forced him to give up the Ulster Hall, was transferred to a tented meeting which, once the cars had passed the Orange district, received an enthusiastic welcome largely from mill-girls, interrupted only by women suffragists and undamped even by rain. He rested the case for Home Rule on Imperial, House of Commons, and Irish arguments: reconciliation of the Irish people with England would strengthen the Empire and remove a serious obstacle to Anglo-American friendship; the Irish were naturally monarchist, and King George’s warmest welcome had been in Catholic and Nationalist Dublin; centralisation left no time for social legislation, defence, and foreign policy; and any plan for Ireland would be an integral part of Parliamentary devolution, not out of harmony with eventual treatment of Scotland and Wales. Eloquently recalling the conflict of Boers and Irish Fusiliers at the storming of the Tugela heights, he asked whether, having made friends with enemies, Britain could not do so with comrades.

Chapter II — The Session Until Easter

Parliament was opened in state by the King, accompanied by the Queen, on Wednesday, 14 February. Despite damp and cold weather large crowds assembled along the Mall and Whitehall, their enthusiasm enhanced by the success of the Royal tour in India. At St. Margaret’s Church one of the six horses drawing a Royal coach stumbled and became entangled with the traces, but the procession was not interrupted. The scene in the House of Lords lost some of its wonted brilliancy through Court mourning for the Duke of Fife.

The Royal Speech, read by the King, contained no surprises. Nearly one-third was devoted to foreign politics: the Government intended, on any favourable opportunity, to associate themselves with other Powers in mediation to end the Turco-Italian War. In the Commons debates on the Address, Ministers defended informal conversations with Germany as dispelling suspicions of aggression and establishing more friendly relations without impairing special friendships with other Powers. On Home Rule the Premier recalled his 1901 caution against carrying it in dependence on the Irish vote, but the Government now had a majority independent of that vote. The Liberal Insurance Committee, he said, had been rendered necessary by Unionist misrepresentations on which all the bye-elections had been fought; the House of Lords had been free to reject the Act, and who would prevent its coming into operation? Would Mr. Bonar Law, when he became Prime Minister, repeal it? Mr. Bonar Law answered “Certainly.” The admission was welcomed by Ministers as frank, and the charge that public money had been spent on the Committee was denounced as insulting; let the Albert Hall charge of corruption be made by amendment to the Address. The debate was then adjourned—fortunately, it was thought, as party passion on both sides was rising. Mr. Bonar Law at once wrote to the Press that he only meant the Unionists would repeal the Act if they came into office before it became operative; otherwise they could only amend it drastically. It was felt he had made an unfortunate mistake.

Next day Mr. Ramsay Macdonald (Lab., Leicester) moved an amendment ascribing labour troubles to insufficient wages amid expansion of national wealth and a rise in the cost of living, and regretting that the Speech mentioned no measures for a minimum wage and for nationalising railways, mines, and other monopolies. Of 7,300,000 wage-earners, 60 per cent. had incomes of less than 30s. a week; aggregate wages had fallen heavily since 1901 while living costs rose; skilled organised labour had been largely supplanted by unskilled, ill-paid labour; and the Government “supinely allowed monopolists, like the coal ring, to put up prices.” A minimum wage had been experimentally begun by the Trade Boards Act of 1910 and must go on. Mr. Clynes (Lab., Manchester, N.E.) seconded, hoping unrest would continue till justice was done. Sir F. Banbury and others opposed; the labour amendment was pressed as the voice of organised discontent rather than as a party manœuvre.

Industrial crisis soon overshadowed all else. The coal strike began at Alfreton in Derbyshire on 1 March; railway services were reduced; the King’s projected visits to foreign Courts were cancelled through the continuance of the stoppage. On 19 March, in a crowded House, the Prime Minister introduced the Coal Mines (Minimum Wage) Bill. The Government, he declared, had resorted to legislation only after all hope of settlement by agreement had disappeared, and was justified only in proposing legislation limited to effecting that settlement. The Bill simply gave effect to the propositions laid before coalowners and miners on 28 February and accepted by the miners and by 65 per cent. of the coalowners. It was temporary, to continue three years. The district minimum would be recoverable from the employer by civil process; any contract modifying it would be void; when ascertained it would be paid retrospectively from the date the miner resumed work after the Act. Wage, conditions and exceptions would be fixed by district boards—twenty-one districts, possibly identical with existing Conciliation Boards—employers and workmen equally represented, with an independent chairman appointed by agreement or, failing agreement, by the Board of Trade and possessing a casting vote; three persons might act as chairmen instead of one. The aged and infirm would be excepted; there would be safeguards for efficiency and regularity; the Bill had no penal provisions on either side. A legislative declaration in favour of a minimum wage would alter the situation; Parliament would then be justified if, heaven forbid, other measures had to be taken to defend the industries of the country against paralysis and starvation.

Mr. Bonar Law disclaimed party advantage out of so grave a crisis but expressed strong misgivings. The minimum wage had been treated in the Address debate as applicable only to sweated industries; now it was applied where wages were above the average. He feared a precedent for other industries; the only difference was that the miners’ union was strong enough to put pressure on the Government. Guarantees against diminished output were insufficient. Mr. Brace (Lab., Glamorgan, S.) showed that a minimum for abnormal places had been customary for years, but a county court had decided it was not legally binding and employers had refused legal sanction. Mr. Austen Chamberlain blamed the Prime Minister for making light of breaches of agreement, ascribed trouble to the Chancellor’s speeches, called the Bill panic legislation without finality, and complained that Parliament had insufficient information as to its effect on the coal trade and the price of coal. Sir Edward Grey wound up: the House faced a new trouble—the demand that the miner be paid a fair wage when he failed to earn it from causes beyond his control. Before intervention the minimum wage had been accepted by owners in the largest mining area; legislation had to be based on that accepted principle. The miners had agreed that with a minimum wage there should be a minimum output; safeguards were in the Bill. The schedule of hewers’ rates could not, without inquiry, be put into the statute; independent chairmen would have more material than the miners had possessed; the schedule might prove more than the trade in some districts could bear. The coal trade was not a monopoly; there was the export trade and great industries that would shrink if coal rose. To protect those willing to work was an elementary duty. Carried to its extreme end, the strike meant starvation and the complete destruction of national life. A door had been opened for regulation of the minimum wage which could not be closed. The second reading was carried by 348 to 225.

On 26 March, during the Report stage, the Prime Minister, speaking with profound emotion, announced that the new Conference had proved abortive. The rates of 5s. and 2s. were not unreasonable, but the Conference had refused his suggestion of treating them separately from the hewers’ wage; having exhausted persuasion, argument and negotiation, the Government pressed the Bill as the best possible solution of the emergency, willing to accept reasonable amendments on detail. Mr. Bonar Law recognised that the Government had done their best; the Opposition would put no obstacle in the way. But if the men did not return when the Bill passed, the Government might fairly expect members of the Miners’ Federation to obey the law as citizens, and might rightly use the whole resources of society to protect those who desired to work. Mr. Ramsay Macdonald said the miners were law-abiding people. The strike closed on 7 April after the Act’s passage; Cardiff steam coal, which had opened the year at 18s. 3d., risen over 30s. on fear of a national stoppage, and in March realised as much as 45s., began to decline once work resumed.

Meanwhile Army Estimates produced sharp controversy over the service rifle and aviation. Colonel Seely, for the War Office, held that unless generals and military advisers were in league to misrepresent facts the British troops were the best armed in Europe; only in trajectory was the rifle inferior; attacks embarrassed defence by alarming the private soldier; at no time had troops been short of ammunition. The Special Reserve was 1,800 less than in 1911, but the Territorial Force (263,624 of all ranks) showed increase in numbers and, he believed, efficiency. A scheme approved that day by the Prime Minister would create only one Flying Corps, embracing soldiers, sailors and civilians agreeing to serve in war in any part of the world, absorbing part of the existing Air Battalion, with headquarters at Netheravon on Salisbury Plain, 180 officers passed through annually, and 131 aeroplanes purchased—an ambitious scheme to hold the country’s own in the air as on land and sea. Mr. Bonar Law stuck to his charge about the rifle; Mr. Wyndham claimed “absolute victory” for his leader on that question, complained that British aviation expenditure was less than France’s and Germany’s, urged restoration of garrisons in the Pacific, Mediterranean and West Indies, and questioned the Expeditionary Force’s ability to leave in full strength. An academic debate on syndicalism (27 March), moved by Mr. Ormsby Gore (U., Denbigh Districts) and seconded by Mr. Hewins, declared that advocacy of an anti-social policy based on class warfare and incitement to mutiny was a grave danger to the State; Mr. Hobhouse, for the Government, entirely repudiated syndicalism as negativing Parliamentary action and destroying national capital, ascribing unrest to the rise in living costs without corresponding wages and to improved mental qualities of workers without corresponding material improvement, and assenting to a far wider inquiry. The debate was cut short by the eleven o’clock rule.

Navy Estimates, introduced by Mr. Churchill as First Lord, outlined two, possibly three, new destroyer flotillas by 1915 under an “Admiral of Patrols,” promotion of small numbers of younger warrant officers to a rank equivalent to sub-lieutenant, restoration of continuous commissions except for foreign stations and torpedo craft, and inquiries into cadet entry and education and into naval punishments. The naval competition of Christendom would excite the curiosity of future generations; the most hopeful interpretation was that it was a substitute for actual warfare. Mr. A. Lee (Hants, Fareham) said it was the first speech by a First Lord under the present Government to which he had listened with pleasure; Mr. Keir Hardie demanded that Germany be “given a lead” in reduction; Lord Charles Beresford regarded the speech as a menace to Germany and urged a big Defence Bill. The only protest against increased expenditure came from Mr. Murray Macdonald (L., Falkirk Burghs). Mr. Churchill replied that Campbell-Bannerman’s attempt to bring about reduction of armaments had been followed by an increase of shipbuilding by the next strongest Power; Britain could remove uncertainty by forecasting her construction.

Other pre-Easter business included medical resistance to the Insurance Act, suffragist window-breaking in the West End (1 and 4 March), celebration of the Parliament Act, the defeat of the Conciliation Bill for women’s suffrage, Sir Almroth Wright’s views on militant suffragists, Scottish Home Rule debate on Dr. Chapple’s resolution, the Registration (Plural Qualifications) Bill, Housing, and the Budget statement. At St. Rollox, Glasgow, Mr. T. McKinnon Wood, appointed Secretary for Scotland, held the seat by 8,530 to 8,061; at South Manchester the Unionist Mr. P. K. Glazebrook converted Sir Arthur Haworth’s Treasury appointment vacancy into a Unionist gain (7,051 to 6,472).

Chapter III — From Easter to Whitsuntide

After Easter the session’s centre of gravity shifted, in the Register’s chapter outline, to the Government of Ireland Bill and Welsh Disestablishment. Continuous extract narrative for the interval itself is thin; the chapter headings record Easter Conferences and British statesmen at St. Petersburg, an Ulster Unionist demonstration, the reassembly of Parliament, the introduction of the Home Rule Bill with its second-reading division and financial and other details, reception of the Bill and a Report on Irish Finance, the Government of India Bill, a Welsh Disestablishment Church demonstration at Carnarvon, further attacks on the Insurance Act, private members’ bills on cottage homes, land acquisition, school attendance and factories, Scottish sea fisheries and the title of “Bank,” criticisms of Ministerial finance, a Criminal Law (Ireland) Bill, an attack on Mr. Bryce, debate on industrial unrest, Unionist fusion, the Primrose League demonstration, the closing of the Dardanelles, the King’s inspection of the Fleet at Weymouth, the King of Denmark’s death, Mr. Churchill and the Navy, relations with Germany and Russia, the case of Miss Malecka, the Welsh Disestablishment Bill’s second-reading debate and division, Mr. Lloyd George at Carnarvon, continued suffragist agitation, labour troubles and the transport workers’ strike, a deputation on railway nationalisation, and the Post Office and Board of Trade votes.

What the continuous record does preserve at the end of this interval is the judicial climax of militant suffrage and the opening of a new industrial struggle. After a six days’ trial at the Central Criminal Court, Mr. and Mrs. Pethick Lawrence and Mrs. Pankhurst were convicted of conspiracy (22 May) and sentenced to nine months’ imprisonment in the second division, the first two also ordered to pay the costs of the prosecution. A strike of London transport workers was declared as the Commons adjourned over Whitsuntide (22 May). The International Horticultural Show in the grounds of Chelsea Hospital, opened after a visit from the King and Queen—the finest such display since 1866—drew nearly 177,000 visitors and took 10,000l., a civilian interlude amid political and industrial strain.

Chapter IV — From Whitsuntide to the Summer Recess

The transport workers’ strike dominated early summer industrial politics; Ministers inspected the Mediterranean establishment at Malta; Mr. Lloyd George spoke at Swansea; North Norfolk returned a bye-election verdict watched as a test of rural feeling. In the Commons the Home Rule Bill went into Committee: separate treatment of Ulster was proposed and resisted; the constitution of the new Irish Legislature, the supremacy of the Imperial Parliament, and the powers of the Irish Parliament filled successive sittings. An Irish Unionist demonstration in London and Mr. Balfour at Preston stiffened Opposition resistance. The Franchise Bill was introduced and, on second reading, closured by 284 to 216 and read a second time by 290 to 218; three women’s suffrage amendments—to omit “male,” to read “male or female,” and to enfranchise “occupiers and the wives of occupiers”—were handed in, but Committee was postponed to 1913.

Insurance Act controversy reached its symbolic peak on “Joy Day” (15 July), when the Act came into force. Through the hot weather, or through the efforts of opponents, the day was not extensively celebrated; but the Chancellor of the Exchequer addressed a great meeting at the Kennington Theatre and worked it up to high enthusiasm. Expressly exempting Mr. Astor, Mr. Charles Bathurst and Lord Henry Bentinck from his attacks, he declared that the Opposition were using the complexities of the industrial system to obstruct the Act; he was not afraid of the “squalid resistance” to paying 3d. a week that they encouraged, but the beginning of law-breaking was full of menace. He bitterly attacked The Times for predicting shilling fines for offenders, and enumerated other possible penalties. Of 13,000,000 persons compulsorily insurable, 9,500,000 had joined approved societies; in three weeks or a month there would be 12,000,000, and 500,000 Post Office contributors would be in societies also. Friendly societies, previously at a standstill and hard put to keep solvent, would start solvent with a margin; those joining were making additional payments for higher benefits. Critics said the Government could not deliver the benefits—“wait and see.” The money for the sanatoria was there, but “the Act was not an Aladdin’s lamp”; already eleven tuberculosis doctors had been appointed for the whole of Wales out of fifty-seven applicants. Six shillings per member was set aside for the doctors, who would have an interest in keeping patients well and no bad debts; if the doctors would not come to terms, the 4,000,000l. set aside for them would go back to the members, and an actuarial report showed that large additional benefits could then be given. The doctors asked for 11s. per member—an extra 4,000,000l. a year, say 1d. on the income tax and ¾d. on tea. Sir William Plender’s report showed that in Dundee doctors’ incomes worked out at 4s. 2d. per head, including guinea practice. He had been bitterly assailed, but his race had the gift of imagination: he could see the vision of the Old Age Pension Act, the National Insurance Act, and many another Act descending like breezes from the hills into mist-laden valleys to clear away the gloom. There were demonstrations against the Act in a few places—notably Liverpool, where 11,000 dock-workers struck against a scheme under it to “decasualise labour”—but other demonstrations proved abortive. Three days earlier, at the Lord Mayor’s annual dinner to the bankers and merchants of the City (12 July), he had congratulated the country on the unequalled prosperity of trade, renewing old attacks and defences of his financial policy. On 11 July Mr. Grant (U., Cumberland, Egremont) had moved the adjournment to call attention to inability to provide full sanatorium benefit at the outset; Mr. Masterman declared temporary accommodation was being devised and existing dispensaries utilised, with a network over the country by 1 January; the motion was rejected by 259 to 111. Unionists projected a National Defence Fund against the Act; legal and other difficulties were raised; the medical men still stood out, treating Plender’s average of 4s. 2d. (or, adding contract practice, about 4s. 1½d.) as inconclusive.

Census results for England and Wales gave London (County) 4,521,685 (a decline of 0.3 per cent. since 1901), Liverpool 746,421, Manchester 714,383, Birmingham 525,833, Sheffield 454,632, Leeds 445,550; of the enumerated population 17,445,608 were males and 18,624,884 females—an excess of over 1,170,000 women which furnished anti-suffragists with a fresh argument. Decline in the rate of increase was due to emigration. At Crewe a Labour revolt let in the Unionist despite the Liberal candidate’s rather tardy adoption of the new “land policy”; the announcement was an inspiriting prelude to the great Unionist demonstration at Blenheim (27 July), when some 3,000 delegates from political associations, invited by the Duke of Marlborough, were entertained at luncheon and addressed in the courtyard, with some 10,000 people from the neighbourhood, by Mr. Bonar Law, Sir Edward Carson and Mr. F. E. Smith. Mr. Bonar Law denounced the Prime Minister’s visit to Dublin and his acceptance of a welcome from the Dublin Corporation, described the Ministry as a revolutionary Committee which had seized by fraud on despotic power, and declared that the Unionist party would use whatever means seemed likely to be most effective. Ireland was two nations; the only sane method was to make the British House of Commons their arbiter. The Ulster people would submit to no ascendancy, and he could imagine no lengths of resistance to which they might go in which he would not be ready to support them, and in which they would not be supported by the overwhelming majority of the British people. The Government were bankrupt in the constituencies and were trying by unauthorised programmes to regain lost popularity. Sir Edward Carson followed, denouncing the Nationalists and Mr. Asquith’s surrender to them, and emphasising Ulster’s determination to resist.

These speeches had an echo in the Commons on the report of Irish Supply (1 August). Mr. Devlin (N., Belfast, W.) called attention to renewed outrages in Belfast shipyards through which some 2,000 Roman Catholics and 500 Protestant Home Rulers had been compelled to leave their employment, and condemned the Unionist leaders. Sir Edward Carson replied that he had deprecated “these spasmodic rows.” Earlier, on 10 July, a deputation of the Belfast Chamber of Commerce to the Prime Minister had complained of the financial clauses of the Home Rule Bill, of the check given by its introduction to Irish prosperity, and of projected Customs barriers; Mr. Asquith could only regret that they had not condescended to particulars and deplore their uncompromising opposition. At the Boyne celebrations (12 July) in Belfast, despite drenching rain, 70,000 Orangemen turned out and were encouraged by a militant speech from Mr. F. E. Smith to hope for English aid. Liberals were reassured by victory at Hanley; celebrations of Mr. Chamberlain’s seventy-seventh birthday, including Mr. Wyndham at Blackpool (13 July), attracted scant notice and inadequate subscriptions toward Tariff Reform.

Mr. Asquith’s visit to Dublin produced one of the most marvellous demonstrations of popular enthusiasm on record. He offered to consider strengthening safeguards for Ulster if they could be shown insufficient, as he had offered the Belfast Chamber of Commerce, but Ulster would not have Home Rule on any terms. So long as constitutional government and free democratic suffrage lasted, he found it difficult to find any justification for incitement to rebellion; he did not believe in the prospect of civil war. A minority had no right, merely on suspicion of coming injury, to thwart the constitutional demand of a vast majority of their fellow-countrymen. Burke had said that “no reluctant tie could be a strong one”; let the two streams unite for the common enrichment of Ireland and the Empire. At Port Sunlight on the way back he referred to benefits secured by the Old Age Pensions and Insurance Acts and said Ministers would have betrayed their trust had they not used the Parliament Act to give effect to the declared wishes of the majority of the electorate. Suffragist militancy, however, entered a more dangerous phase: two women were found at 1 A.M. on 13 July in the grounds of Nuneham House, the Colonial Secretary’s seat near Oxford, apparently contemplating arson; in Dublin an attempt was made the evening before his speech to burn the theatre, and a hatchet thrown into his carriage during the torchlight procession wounded Mr. Redmond slightly in the ear. Trials and convictions followed; the Prime Minister escaped with nothing worse than a shower of flour at Chester and futile interruptions on the journey back to London.

Attention was partly diverted by Imperial defence. The Canadian Prime Minister, Mr. Borden, arrived at Avonmouth in the Royal George on 4 July, accompanied or preceded by six colleagues, for discussions understood to cover naval contribution and related matters; the visit’s effect on Mediterranean and home-waters dispositions remained a live question through the naval display at Spithead, the shipbuilding vote, and later Canadian debate. Lord Haldane became Lord Chancellor; the Government of India Bill was passed; the King and Queen visited South Wales and Yorkshire; foreign questions, Russia and Persia, Territorial and Special Reserve votes, and a supplementary naval estimate occupied Supply. The transport strike ended; the Finance and Appropriation Bills passed; the Marconi agreement for a chain of wireless stations throughout the Empire, the Mental Deficiency Bill, and rival land policies closed a crowded pre-recess programme. Unionist speeches at Blenheim continued to reverberate as the Houses rose.

Chapter V — The Recess and the Autumn Session

The recess opened with an echo of the Blenheim protests in a lengthy letter from Mr. Churchill to Sir George Ritchie, Chairman of the Liberal party in Dundee. The First Lord declared that Mr. Bonar Law and Sir Edward Carson would be unspeakably shocked and frightened if “all this melodramatic stuff” were suddenly to “explode into real bombs and cannon,” and if, instead of the expectation of office, there were only “the bleak outlook of a felon’s cell or place of execution.” Their “civil war” had merely led to the maltreatment of Home Rule and Catholic workmen in Belfast; the “lynching” of Ministers had only meant howling down the Prime Minister in the House. The serious aspect was that the earnestness and self-restraint which in Great Britain had settled disputes without violence was giving place to a hysterical spirit, and to this they were pandering. Mr. Bonar Law virtually claimed that if the Opposition or any other section decided that the Government had not obtained their majority fairly, violence was legitimate—doctrines that would destroy constitutional government, arrest the consolidating of the Empire if others chose so to assert their “birthright,” and justify labour risings. “The doctrines of Mr. Bonar Law at Blenheim are the doctrines of Mr. Ben Tillett on Tower Hill. But Tillett’s men were starving.” Talk of violence was unprovoked and had come from one side only; Mr. Bonar Law might find himself, if Home Rule were frustrated, repressing Irish Nationalism in the name of the law and order he now trampled down. These exhibitions would not seduce the Government into weakness and violence.

Ulster’s answer was the campaign culminating in the solemn religious services and formal signing of the “Covenant.” At the demonstrations—excepting Ballymena, Dromore and Ballyroney—Sir Edward Carson was the chief figure, popularly styled “the General,” met and escorted by mounted bodyguards carrying dummy wooden rifles and sometimes wearing khaki; at Portadown, an Orange stronghold, they were even accompanied by two dummy cannon and an imitation Red Cross ambulance. At all these meetings the one resolution, carried by acclamation, was “That we will not have Home Rule.” At Enniskillen, famous as one of the places which did most to defeat James II., Lord Erne presided; Sir Edward Carson denounced the Government for thrusting Ulster out of the Union, spoke significantly of the coming Covenant, which he would esteem it an honour to be the first to sign, and of further obligations which might eventually be incumbent on Ulstermen. Lord Hugh Cecil took Mr. Churchill’s Federalist speech as a sign that Home Rule was impossible, argued the case for resistance to subjection to a “despicable sovereignty” and religious tyranny, and Mr. T. Sinclair declared that Home Rule meant a disastrous economic upheaval. The Covenant’s signing produced a profound effect in Great Britain; Mr. Watson’s poem on Ulster and the Duke of Westminster’s Tariff Reform Fund illustrated the emotional and organisational reinforcement of the Unionist campaign.

When the Commons reassembled, Home Rule Committee work resumed under guillotine: clauses on the Irish Legislature, financial resolution, and successive blocks of the Bill (clauses 2–4, 5–8, 9–13, 14, 15–25) filled the timetable alongside the Welsh Church Bill timetable, the Temperance (Scotland) Bill (amended by the Lords), and the Criminal Law Amendment Bill. Mr. Bonar Law spoke at the Constitutional Club, the Nonconformist Unionist Association, and Oldham; Mr. Asquith at Ladybank; Mr. Churchill at Dundee and Sheffield on Admiralty reforms and Anglo-German relations. Debate on the Marconi contract, foot-and-mouth disease, congestion of legal business, and the Wilks case diversified Supply and legislation. A Government defeat in the Commons, a motion to rescind, and a scene in the House produced a temporary parliamentary deadlock later removed; silver purchases for India and Sir Stuart Samuel’s seat complicated the calendar. The National Liberal Federation heard Mr. Asquith and Mr. Redmond; Bolton and Bow and Bromley elections tested Insurance and suffrage feeling—at Bow and Bromley, Mr. Lloyd George later observed, the Tory candidate was in effect his own candidate on the Insurance Act and won, while association of the contest with defence of militant violence alienated East End working people.

In late autumn speeches Mr. Lloyd George at Kirkcaldy (30 November) said that had the suffragists confined themselves to legal agitation their Bill would have been passed in 1912; instead of needful spade work they were doing hatchet work. The House was congested with local business; the Scottish Temperance Bill was being torn to pieces by the Lords; Home Rule debates were conducted in a languid and attenuated House. Minute discussion was well enough for tariffs but no use for great reforms; the great prosperity of the North refuted the crude quackery of Tariff Reform; the Insurance Act was working like clockwork. At Aberdeen great precautions were taken against suffragist interruptions: three young girls were found in the hall armed with toy pistols giving a loud report which might have set up a panic; next day a woman suffragist assaulted a Baptist minister at the railway station with a dogwhip, mistaking him for the Chancellor escaping in disguise; another threw her shoes at the magistrate. These acts, coupled with pillar-box outrages which incidentally destroyed cheques for charitable purposes, seriously injured the cause. Mr. Lansbury, at a demonstration in his honour at the London Opera House (2 December), expressed doubt whether destruction furthered the suffrage. On the land system the Chancellor declared the social and economic condition of the country “root-bound by the feudal system”; from Scotland emigration was larger even than from Ireland; people were driven off the land; the first purpose of the land should be sustenance and shelter for the multitude—talk said to undermine credit, as had been said of the Budget of 1909, after which trade had gone on improving. His reception was compared by Liberal observers with those given thirty years earlier to Gladstone.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. The salient features of the year’s history were the progress of the movement for the reunion of the Established and Free Churches; the boom in trade, in spite of the coal strike, especially in shipbuilding; and the agitation for Scottish Home Rule. To the last-named subject the Liberal candidate in the East Edinburgh bye-election devoted special attention. Dr. Chapple’s resolution was followed by a Bill providing for the delegation of specific powers and subjects of legislation to a Scottish Parliament and for an Executive responsible to that body, together with a readjustment of Anglo-Scottish financial relations. It was not debated, and a deputation of Scottish Liberal members to the Prime Minister (6 May) failed to obtain a pledge that time should be found for it in 1913, though Mr. Asquith’s general attitude was sympathetic. Some Scottish Liberal members were believed not to be very ardent in its support. In other quarters the feeling was eager, and at the end of July the Young Scots Society arranged for joint action with the Liberal party organisations to promote the Bill. The movement, though it had some Labour support, was mainly the affair of the Liberal party; Scottish Unionists remained hostile or indifferent. Shipbuilding yards on the Clyde and elsewhere were employed to their fullest extent; oil-tank steamers were under special demand, with bitter complaints that delivery was delayed and fault ascribed largely to the dilatoriness of workmen agitating not only for higher pay but for shorter hours.

Ireland. Agrarian and commercial life was disturbed by foot-and-mouth disease. In the debate on the Vote for the Irish Board of Agriculture (5 July) some English members thought the Board had not been prompt enough; Mr. Russell defended his action, and an amendment reflecting on him was defeated by 256 to 167. Limerick was reopened for shipment of cattle to certain English ports, to be slaughtered on landing, on 18 July; Dundalk and Newry on 12 August; but import of Irish store cattle into England was prohibited by the English Board of Agriculture. The Irish Board proposed that export be allowed except from the infected districts (Dublin, Meath and Louth), and Mr. Russell, at the Council of Agriculture (17 September), attacked the English Board for its refusal. At the end of that month shipment of store cattle was permitted from certain Irish ports to special landing-places at certain English ports, and the Government was again attacked from both sides in Parliament (18 October). Early in December a further outbreak among imported Irish cattle at Birkenhead led to renewal of the prohibition, and subsequently quarantine for Irish cattle was imposed at all English ports. Some scheduled Ulster counties were, however, again opened for export at the end of the year. The restrictions greatly stimulated the export of dead meat from Ireland to Great Britain.

Friction was also caused by the refusal of the Irish Department of Agriculture and Technical Instruction to give a grant to the Irish Agricultural Organisation Society, a course adversely criticised in Parliament (5 July) by Mr. Haslam (L.). Mr. Dillon then attacked the society as political. The grant had been refused since 1907 because the Department had not adequate control over the society, State aid to co-operative competition with shopkeepers was undesirable, and public controversy hampered the Department’s work. The Development Commissioners in 1912 recommended a grant of 2,000l., subject inter alia to representation of the Department, the Council of Agriculture, and the Congested Districts Board on the Society’s Council. The first two bodies declined; members of the second, invited individually by the Commission to join the Council, declined also. The matter was still in suspense when the year closed.

In their solicitude for the fate of the Universities under Home Rule the militant Unionists went too far for their compatriots. Mr. Campbell (Dublin University) desired to exempt his constituency from the authority of an Irish Parliament; but the undergraduates signed a memorial protesting against any attempt at its severance from the rest of Ireland, and the governing body, by 24 votes to 13, disapproved of his projected amendment (10 November), but asked for a guarantee in the Bill that there should be no alteration in the status quo established by the Universities Act of 1908. This was given. The Senate of Queen’s University was likewise concerned to preserve the settlement of 1908. Politically, Ulster’s Covenant campaign and the Belfast shipyard expulsions of Catholic and Protestant Home Rule workmen marked the year’s resistance to the Government of Ireland Bill, while Nationalist Dublin’s reception of Mr. Asquith and Mr. Redmond’s wound from a suffragist hatchet illustrated the passions surrounding the measure.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1912

By Cuthbert Maughan

In spite of two wars and a serious coal strike, trade was extraordinarily active during 1912. Practically every industry in the United Kingdom was actively employed, prices were high, and large profits were made. Abroad the story generally was one of great trade prosperity, with the Bourses nervous and sensitive to every rumour of war. At home prosperity was measured by the returns of the London Bankers’ Clearing House—the highest ever recorded—the figures of foreign trade, and the statistics of commercial failures. The number of failures in the United Kingdom was 7,385, as compared with 7,815 in 1911 and 8,451 in 1910—actually the lowest recorded during the past twenty-five years.

As always happens in periods of great trade activity, money was at no time cheap, while in the autumn the Balkan crisis created great stringency on the Continent. The Bank of England minimum discount rate opened at 4 per cent. and continued until 8 February; the rate was then reduced, and again on 9 May to 3 per cent., after which no further change was made for a time. Heavy depreciation in gilt-edged stocks and great prosperity in industry were the keynotes of the finance and commerce of the year. Fire insurance companies experienced a generally favourable year, favoured by a wet summer and so escaping the serious claims caused by the drought of 1911; competition remained exceedingly keen, and while the offices naturally benefited from the universal activity of trade their increase in premium income was probably not proportionate to the increase in their liabilities. Life offices indicated very satisfactory conditions as regards new business, while they had to take into account a further serious decline in the value of their Stock Exchange securities. Abroad, Bourses remained nervous and sensitive to every rumour of war even while industrial prosperity was general.

All prices of commodities were substantially higher, with the one exception of cereals. In metals the principal advance was in pig iron and Cleveland warrants, which rose sharply in June and closed at 67s. 5d. per ton as compared with 50s. 8d. at the end of 1911; hematite warrants closed at 83s. as compared with 66s. Staffordshire marked bars rose 30s. to 10l., and steel ship plates 1l. 5s. to 8l. 5s.—the rise in ship plates reflecting pressure in the shipbuilding industry, where output of tonnage could not keep step with demand. As in 1911 every yard was employed to its fullest extent; all types of vessels were under order, oil-tank steamers especially. Copper showed a rise of 13l. 10s. to 76l. 12s. 6d., and tin advanced nearly 25l. to 229l. 10s., the year-end price depressed by a falling off in the activity of the Welsh tin-plate trade. In textiles middling American cotton closed 2s. 10d. dearer; Egyptian was also dearer; manufacturers enjoyed a very prosperous period, and in the woollen industries exceptional activity prevailed. Best Cardiff steam coal opened at 18s. 3d. per ton, rose to over 30s. partly on fear of a national coal strike, and in March actually realised as much as 45s.; as soon as the strike which started in March came to an end (7 April), values began to decline. Speculation was active in Marconi shares on reports of the agreement with the Government for construction of a chain of wireless stations throughout the Empire, in National Telephone stocks pending arbitration on the purchase price to be paid by the Government, and in Nigerian tin and diamond and copper shares—the last declining later on continental liquidation at the time of the Balkan crisis.
