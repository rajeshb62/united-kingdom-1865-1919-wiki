The Annual Register 1913

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The New Session Until Easter

Parliament was reopened by the King, accompanied by the Queen, on Monday, 10 March. Dull weather, ending in rain after the Royal Procession had reached the Houses of Parliament, did not interfere with the popular manifestations of interest. Five women suffragists attempted, opposite St. James’s Palace, to reach the Royal carriage with a petition denouncing the policy of releasing and rearresting the hunger-strikers and praying for the grant of votes to women as the only means of stilling the prevalent unrest; they were stopped and arrested. Later a Jew succeeded in handing to the King’s coachman, for transmission to His Majesty, a petition relating to the sufferings of the poor in East London. In the House of Lords the ceremony derived an added brilliancy from the revival of a custom discontinued at Queen Victoria’s accession: the Crown, which had been borne before her and King Edward on a cushion, was worn by King George, as it had been by William IV. The King read his Speech in clear and distinct tones.

The first paragraph commemorated the fiftieth anniversary of the marriage of the King’s parents and expressed, for the Queen Mother and himself, grateful sense of the devoted affection of the nation which it had been her happiness to enjoy for so many years and which remained to support and console her in her abiding sorrow. Relations with Foreign Powers continued friendly. The Speech referred to the Conference of the Balkan Governments in London, regretting its failure and the continuance of the war; the Great Powers earnestly desired to prevent the war from spreading and to see it speedily terminated; the Government had kept in touch with the other Powers in the endeavours to preserve concert and establish agreements, and a large measure of success had been achieved. Reference was made to the visits of Ministers from Canada and New Zealand, with confidence that the exchange of views would promote the solidarity of the Empire; to the recent gift of a battleship by the Malay States; to the ready consent of the New Zealand Government to the retention in the North Sea fleet of the battleship contributed by them; and to the steady progress towards the establishment of the Australian fleet.

In the Commons debate on the Address, Opposition comment on the Royal Speech followed lines substantially identical with those taken by the Marquess of Lansdowne in the Lords. The Plural Voting Bill would be opposed; the Education Bill was perhaps intended as a rival to the new land policy, though possibly it was not desired to put that policy forward too long before a general election, lest examination should diminish its value. Stress was laid on the omission of labour legislation and on the non-fulfilment of the promise in the preamble of the Parliament Act—an Act which, it was said, had concentrated power in the hands of the Cabinet so that debate might as well be dropped. Opposition to the Home Rule Bill was steadily hardening in North-East Ulster, as was also the organisation of resistance; the Government, it was believed, would not force local autonomy on the people by the sword, but if they did they were drifting towards a disaster of which the magnitude could not be foretold.

Mr. Asquith, after a graceful reference to the realisation of the universal hopes entertained as to the results of the Royal marriage celebrated fifty years ago that day, made an important statement on foreign politics. When the war broke out the Great Powers found themselves compelled to deal with it as it affected the European system founded by the Treaty of Berlin, but were otherwise not directly interested; since then the deliberations of the Ambassadors’ Conference in London under the presidency of the Foreign Secretary had been mainly directed to those considerations. Agreement had been reached as to two vital points: the Adriatic littoral and Servian economic access to it by an international railway; and, in principle, the acceptance of an autonomous Albania, though one or two points were still outstanding as regarded the Albanian frontiers.

Meanwhile the Marconi affair, already the subject of a Select Committee in 1912, continued to overshadow Ministerial credit. Unionists who had welcomed newspaper rumours of Ministerial speculation in Marconi shares had eagerly followed the Committee’s proceedings; the Agreement itself had almost been lost sight of amid the lengthy examinations of witnesses who had made charges of corruption. The Committee had heard evidence from representatives of the Poulsen system that the company had not been fairly treated by the Government, and from Sir G. Croydon Marks, M.P., an electrical expert who had severely condemned the contract; in the middle of January it made an interim report recommending, amongst other steps, the appointment of an expert Committee. This body, consisting of Mr. Justice Parker as Chairman and four eminent electrical experts, was duly appointed on 23 January; but the Marconi Company became impatient, requested the Government to release it from the contract (20 January), and subsequently announced that it could not be bound by it after 1 March. The Select Committee diverged into a sort of “fishing inquiry” into the origin of the rumours affecting Ministers, with mainly negative results. Mr. W. R. Lawson, whose articles in the Outlook had been described as the basis of the charges, stated on cross-examination that he never verified any of the rumours detrimental to Ministers and practically admitted that his charges had no solid foundation; Mr. Oliver, editor of the Outlook, said that if statements made had to be proved political journalism would be impossible; and Mr. Leo Maxse, editor of the National Review, repeated his insinuations against Ministers before the Committee (12 February) but declined to disclose the sources of his statements. His refusal was reported to the House, which eventually decided to take no action. Certain members of the Committee showed marked animus and partisanship, and occasionally its proceedings were stormy.

A motion by Mr. Gwynne, raising questions connected with Indian cash balances and speculation, was negatived after further speeches; the Postmaster-General incidentally explained that he had not only had nothing to do with a purchase under discussion but was unaware of the transaction till after its completion. As to the cash balances, up to May 1909 they had been lent to approved City firms of high standing; at that date they became too large to be thus absorbed and the surplus was lent to leading banks; subsequently the balances had been used to reduce largely the Indian floating debt. The question of balances opened up very important matters of which there should be a thorough investigation by an expert body, preferably a Royal Commission—a declaration which gave general satisfaction.

Unionist fiscal policy was restated by Mr. Bonar Law in terms which the Unionist Press generally accepted, though the Morning Post still held it unwise and The Times deprecated any appeal to the country on a policy of readjustment of food duties determined by the wishes of the Dominions. The Unionists would impose a lower tariff than that of any industrial country on manufactured goods; they would give the Dominions the largest preference possible without food duties—a request urged by all the Colonies for more than a generation; and they would try to establish co-operation throughout the Empire in trade as well as in defence, working out with the Dominions the best scheme and putting it to the electors for their assent. An industrial tariff, he contended against some agriculturists, would increase the value of the home market; the Unionists would relieve agricultural rating, encourage agriculture as in Belgium and Denmark, and improve rural housing. After attacking recent Ministerial speeches and the Insurance Act, he dwelt on the resistance of Ulster as a rock on which the Government Bill would inevitably be wrecked, and asked the Prime Minister how he could justify the Home Rule Bill when his party was not even the largest party in the Commons; why he had not reformed the House of Lords—a “debt of honour”; and whether he would force the Bill on Ulster by British bayonets without obtaining the sanction of the British people.

Earlier the same day, at the inaugural Conference of the Scottish Unionist Association, the Opposition leader declared that the Government were endangering the Monarchy by putting the King in a position, should he be asked to assent to the Home Rule Bill, in which, whatever he did, half the people would think he had failed in his duty. Such a crime would be the greatest ever committed by any Minister; and it would be the duty of the Unionist party to use all means to prevent it—though the occasion would not arise, for the Government would first have to come up for judgment before the electors. The introduction of the King’s name was strongly condemned by Liberal speakers as violating the rule that the Crown stands above party politics.

Three days after Mr. Bonar Law’s explanation the debate began on the Home Rule Bill in the House of Lords (27 January) with every sign of acute interest, even though the interest was now mainly academic. The Marquess of Crewe, in moving the second reading, pointed out that Ireland had never become part of the United Kingdom in the full sense claimed by opponents of the measure.

The Army Estimates were introduced by the War Minister on 19 March. The strength of the Territorial Force on 1 March was 9,290 officers and 252,965 of other ranks—a decrease as compared with 1 March 1912 of 97 officers and 10,659 men, due merely to the abnormal numbers who had joined in 1908 and whose time was now up; the past year had been the best for recruiting since 1909. Attendance at camp for fifteen days and over had been 6,859 officers and 155,055 of other ranks; musketry returns showed further marked improvement; the National Reserve on 1 January numbered 90,000. Arrangements for the provision of horses on mobilisation for all branches of the service were complete. Total Effective and Non-effective Services stood at 28,220,000l., a net increase of 360,000l. The Special Reserve showed an increase of 140 officers but a decrease of about 1,800 men.

Chapter II — From Easter to Whitsuntide

The new development of the Marconi affair arose in the week preceding Easter out of the trial of an action for libel brought by the Attorney-General and the Postmaster-General against Le Matin, a prominent Paris newspaper with an office in London. On 14 February, after Mr. Maxse’s evidence, this paper had stated that he had intimated that the Postmaster-General, whose idea it had been to enter into negotiations with the Marconi Company, had entered into an arrangement with the Attorney-General and his brother, Mr. Godfrey Isaacs, managing director of the Marconi Company, and that all three had bought shares at about 2l. each, their price before the opening of the negotiations with the Government, and had resold them at a profit rising to 8l. per share as the negotiation enabled the conclusion of the contract.

Particulars given during the Matin case and before the Select Committee filled out the story of Ministerial dealings in the shares of the American Marconi Company. The Attorney-General stated that he was under no obligation to his brother in getting the 10,000 shares, and offered to lay his and his wife’s passbooks before the Committee. The Chancellor of the Exchequer, examined on 28 and 31 March, stated that he had been prevented by the coal strike from taking much part in the negotiations on the Marconi contract, and confirmed the Attorney-General’s statement as to the offer of the shares, coupled with the explanation that the American company had no interest in contracts made by the English company, and that Ministers might properly invest in its shares. There was then no question about the importance of the Marconi apparatus; to most people “Marconi” was synonymous with wireless enterprise in Great Britain, and the apparatus had been installed on British cruisers by the Unionist Government and in the mercantile marine. The atmosphere of suspicion about it did not then exist. Judging by precedents, there were two rules of investment for a Minister—he should not use special information received in his capacity as a Minister, nor invest in companies which held Government contracts; under these rules his own investment was perfectly proper.

Besides the 2,000 shares in the American company which the Attorney-General allotted to him and the Master of Elibank (now Lord Murray), of which 1,857 were sold on 20 May 1912 on his broker’s suggestion at a profit to him of 743l., he also bought 3,000 American shares on 22 May, half being for Lord Murray. These were retained as an investment. They were bought with money borrowed from his broker, on which interest was paid; the capital was not paid, owing to the absence of Lord Murray. The net result was that he still held 1,643 shares and had lost, at current prices, about 500l. As he had been described in the Press as enormously wealthy, he gave a full statement of his position, offering to produce his own pass-books, his wife’s, and his brother’s. His savings yielded 400l. a year, and he had built a house at Criccieth at a cost, including the land, of 2,000l. He had had no chance of making a complete disclosure in October, and it was regarded as irrelevant to the Matin case. He was willing to be judged by the principles he had laid down in the Kynoch case. He insisted that his purchase was an investment, not a speculation, though he had sold part at a profit and had bought with borrowed money. He was very severely cross-examined by certain Unionist members of the Committee, and at last declared, in answer to Mr. Falconer, that no one had ever made specific charges to him, but that certain members of Parliament had circulated slanders to a journalist and had disgraced Parliament by not coming forward to prove them.

Budget resolutions were continued on 28 and 29 April and eventually passed without a division. Mr. Pretyman and other Unionist members attacked the land taxes of 1909 on the basis of the official figures, declared that the valuation was useless for other purposes, being neither reliable nor uniform, made much of the taxation of builders’ profits in the Lumsden case, and insisted that the Chancellor’s finance was far too optimistic; but they concentrated their attacks on the land taxes, and only two speakers mentioned Tariff Reform. Liberal and Labour members attacked the expenditure on armaments and the retention of the sugar duty. Mr. Arnold (Holmfirth), in a notable maiden speech, defended the ministerial policy, pointing out that the expenditure of Great Britain in proportion to her estimated national income was considerably below that of France and Germany, while their burden of conscription did not appear in the accounts. Mr. Maurice Healy and Mr. T. Healy laid stress on the burden imposed on Ireland by the Budget of 1909—1,500,000l. instead of the estimated 500,000l. The Chancellor of the Exchequer defended his finance on both days, declaring that he had never expected much immediate revenue from the increment and land value duties, that the effect in the Lumsden case would be met by the Revenue Bill, and that the depression in the building trade was due to overbuilding before 1909 and to the Budget agitation, but it had recovered, at any rate in the North. The valuation was long overdue; the existing assessments for local purposes were unfair as between parishes and individuals; only a very few of the valuations had been contested; by March 1915 the whole would be complete, and a very small adjustment would adapt it to local taxation purposes. Mr. Bonar Law emphasised the criticisms of the land duties; Mr. Austen Chamberlain the charge of over-optimism.

The figures of the total yield and cost of collection of the Land Values duties to 31 March 1913 had been officially stated by the Chancellor in a written reply. The Increment Value duty had produced 23,254l.; the Reversion Duty, 70,878l.; the Undeveloped Land Duty, 129,298l.; the Mineral Rights Duty, 1,234,483l. The total cost of valuation had been 1,393,000l. The officials of the Land Valuation Office numbered 4,153, with total annual salaries of 492,626l.

The second reading of the Plural Voting Bill was debated on 29 and 30 April. Mr. F. E. Smith moved an amendment declining to accept a Bill which professed to remove only one of the anomalies affecting electoral law.

In the House of Lords, on the second reading of the Army (Annual) Bill (17 April), Lord Lovat objected to the limitation of the inquiry of the sub-Committee on Imperial Defence to the single issue of invasion, and to the choice of Mr. Balfour to serve on it as committing the whole Opposition to its conclusions. Most students of European strategy believed that the future of Europe would be decided on the Belgian frontier, and that the duty of soldiers was to get every available man to help the French when that time came. He laid stress on the development of foreign navies and of aircraft. The Marquess of Crewe denied that the inquiry would be confined to the single issue of invasion, but it could not discuss political issues. He urged the House not to consider the “Expeditionary Force” as a force always just about to start, whereas it was really only a part of the Regular troops normally at home, but capable of being sent abroad. The shortage in the Territorials was grave, but was it so dangerous as to require heroic action? A force of 70,000 was regarded as the lowest with which an enemy would care to invade the country with a view to conquest, and the Admiralty held that the Navy could always intercept such an invasion, while raids—descents to do damage or create alarm, but not to remain—could be met by the Territorials and the Regulars remaining after the despatch of the Expeditionary Force. There was no present necessity largely to increase the military forces; the country would prefer to wait for the proposals of the War Office before embarking on the unknown sea of compulsory service. The Marquess of Lansdowne urged that, even on the new view that the 70,000 men could only come by a series of sporadic raids, the Government was not absolved from having an adequate force to meet them, and it was doubtful if the Territorial Force was adequate. He advocated physical and military training in schools, and denied that Mr. Balfour’s selection would commit the Unionist party. Answering questions (16 April), the War Minister said that the 70,000 invaders could only come “by a series of isolated raids at wide intervals of time and space”; his earlier admission that compulsion might conceivably be needed had caused some comment, and a Territorial Forces Bill was talked out by Major Archer-Shee, Opposition members being thus saved from definitely committing themselves to the principle of compulsory service.

Easter-to-Whitsuntide business, as summarised in the Register’s own contents, also included the “Cat and Mouse” Bill for dealing with hunger-striking suffragist prisoners, further suffragist outrages, introduction of the Plural Voting Bill, a Minimum Wage resolution, the Provisional Collection of Taxes Bill, Signor Marconi’s appearance before the Marconi Committee, and debates on aerial defence and on the right of capture of private property at sea.

Chapter III — The Remainder of the Session

The Whitsuntide recess began with a Royal visit to Aldershot, which afforded an opportunity for a series of interesting displays by various branches of the Service. The King and Queen, accompanied by Princess Mary and Princess Victoria, stayed at the Royal Pavilion for a week (9–15 May), and witnessed successively a number of daring feats of horsemanship by cavalrymen, the latest development of aircraft, the Territorial Artillery at manœuvres, tests of the new rifle, and a realistic incident of a supposed foreign invasion; they inspected the Royal Military College in its new home at Camberley, and the King presented new colours to the cadets; and His Majesty attended a reunion of the old comrades of the Munster Fusiliers. The whole visit afforded a fresh proof of his energy in acquainting himself with the work done by the Services, and extended and strengthened the relationship with the Army which had been promoted by that of 1912. The cold, wet weather which accompanied an unusually early date for the movable festivals of spring did not greatly interfere with the success of the visit, though it proved a serious blow to the enjoyment of holiday-makers throughout the country, especially as the stormy Easter had served as a stimulus to the Whitsuntide excursion traffic.

The holiday contribution to politics consisted chiefly of severe criticisms passed at the usual Congresses of Friendly Societies on the working of the National Insurance Act. The Grand Master of the Manchester Unity of Oddfellows (Mr. J. R. Barley) told the society’s conference at Scarborough that the scheme should have been supplementary to the work of friendly societies rather than a substitute for it, and condemned the transfer of the administration of medical benefit from those societies to the Insurance Committees and the admission of industrial insurance societies and collecting societies, as endangering the friendly societies, the pioneers in the work, by State-aided competition. This latter complaint was echoed at the congresses of other friendly societies; the industrial insurance companies had secured over 40 per cent. of the insured persons, as against 48 per cent. obtained by friendly societies. There were complaints also that the hopes raised by the Chancellor of the Exchequer had led to malingering, and that the friendly societies were inadequately treated.

When Parliament was again in full work the great Parliamentary measures under the Parliament Act path—Home Rule, the Welsh Church Bill, the Scottish Temperance Bill, and the Plural Voting Bill—were carried through their stages in the Commons. The Register’s contents record the second-reading debate and division on the Welsh Church Bill; a Hyde Park demonstration; the report stage of the Welsh Church, Home Rule, and Scottish Temperance Bills; the Plural Voting Bill in Committee; the final passage of the Home Rule Bill, Welsh Church Bill, Scottish Temperance Bill, and Plural Voting Bill; and the subsequent rejection of the Home Rule and Welsh Church measures (and of Plural Voting) in the Lords. In the Lords debate on the Established Church (Wales) Bill, the Bishop of London contested the Ministerial estimate of the funds which would be left to the Church and attacked disendowment; the Earl of Selborne attributed the desire for disendowment to sectarian malice. Later in the year, at a demonstration against the Welsh Disestablishment Bill promoted by the Houses of Laymen of the Provinces of Canterbury and York and held in the Albert Hall on 20 November, the Primate laid stress on the protests already made against the Bill and quoted a recent letter from the Irish Protestant Bishop of Ossory contesting the current view that the Irish Church was stronger and its clergy better off than before Disestablishment. Viscount St. Aldwyn said that if the Welsh Church Bill were passed under the Parliament Act in the summer of 1914 it would not come into operation for six or twelve months, and before the end of 1915 there must be a general election; individuals and corporations on whom the Bill imposed duties should delay its operation as much as they could, so that time would be given for the verdict of the country; if that verdict were adverse, the Unionist Government would be called on by the unanimous voice of Churchmen to repeal the Bill. The Bishop of St. Davids asked that a special fund of 20,000l. should be raised to oppose the Bill.

The Marconi contract was at last settled when an amendment was rejected by 221 to 140 and the Agreement approved by 210 to 138. Little more was heard of the scandals during the year except in speeches from lesser lights of Unionism and in the National Review. The firm of jobbers who had introduced American Marconis to the Stock Exchange was suspended in November for reasons unconnected with politics, and a “Clean Government League,” formed by independent politicians, became active at bye-elections. Another alleged scandal seemed likely to be used against the Government—the “Piccadilly Flat case,” in which a woman named Queenie Gerald had been convicted of keeping a disorderly house. It was rumoured that a graver charge had been hushed up for fear of revealing the names of certain M.P.’s and others, and the matter was taken up by Mr. Keir Hardie and other Labour members and by the Globe. The Home Secretary, however, was able to state in the debate on the Appropriation Bill (12 August) that none of the names found in the woman’s books were those of Ministers or members of Parliament, and that, as they were in her handwriting only, it would be most improper to disclose them.

Some of the suffragists had used this matter against the Government; meantime the “Cat and Mouse” Act was almost invariably rendered futile by hunger-striking. A nurse unsuccessfully attempted to petition the King on the subject during his inspection of National Reservists at Chichester (28 July); and on Sunday, 3 August, the Litany was interrupted in Westminster Abbey at the supplication for “prisoners and captives” by a number of women who chanted a verse of prayer for “Emmeline Pankhurst”—a prayer repeated subsequently, for her and other victims of the Act, at St. Paul’s Cathedral and elsewhere. A deputation of clergy, organised by the Rev. L. F. Donaldson of Leicester, obtained leave for some of their members to present a protest against the Act to the Prime Minister at Downing Street (7 August); the Bishop of Lincoln was among the signatories, and, while not approving the militants’ methods, he reserved his condemnation (Times, 8 August) for the anti-suffragists and for the Act and similar methods of repression. The Prime Minister had been interrupted at the Tuberculosis Conference (4 August), and a “free speech” meeting at Trafalgar Square (10 August) led to the arrest of Miss Sylvia Pankhurst while she and her supporters were endeavouring to force their way to Downing Street, and to prolonged disorder in Whitehall.

On the other hand, the Prime Minister received a deputation from the National Union of Women’s Suffrage Societies on 8 August. Mrs. Fawcett, the President, urged that the Government should themselves bring in a Government franchise measure containing a women’s suffrage clause. Wellington and Peel had remained in the Government which proposed Catholic Emancipation; the constitutionalist women pressed for a similar ministerial lead.

The Insurance Act Amendment Bill, which had been before a Standing Committee, was debated on Report on 5 and 6 August. Many new clauses proposed were ruled out; but one, moved by Mr. Ramsay Macdonald, was accepted by the Government and passed. It provided that, where the panel system had proved inadequate in the provision of medical attendance, the Insurance Commissioners might make other arrangements or allow the insured persons to make their own. The most interesting struggle arose next day on the question—which divided all parties—whether maternity benefit should be payable to the husband or to the wife. The original Act had allowed the husband to receive the benefit; the women’s societies had protested against this, the trade unions had regarded the protest as an insult to the vast majority of their members; but in the Standing Committee the benefit had been made payable in every case to the mother. Mr. G. Roberts (Norwich) moved an amendment making the benefit payable alternatively.

Probably it was owing to the new grouping of the Powers since 1904 that an attempt was made to revive the Channel Tunnel project, which had been opposed by every Government since the adverse Report of Lord Lansdowne’s Joint Committee in 1883. A deputation favourable to the tunnel, representing about ninety members of Parliament of all parties, was received by the Prime Minister on 5 August, but, while admitting that there were new factors in the matter since the adverse pronouncement of Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman in 1907, he could give no decided reply. Much was said at a subsequent “Franco-British Travel Congress” and elsewhere in favour either of the tunnel or of a train-ferry like those in the Baltic, but no definite advance was recorded.

The appointment of a Royal Commission on venereal disease was definitely promised by the Prime Minister in the Commons on 11 August; the actual appointment followed in October. Experiments on living animals were formally declared indispensable at the medical congress of the season, and great stress was laid on the dangers of syphilis and the need for precautionary measures and further enquiry.

In the later months Home Rule and Ulster again dominated public speech. The Prime Minister, defending the constitutional case for applying to the Home Rule Bill in the next session the provisions of the Parliament Act, asked whether the action of statesmen ought now to be deflected by the menace of forcible resistance. The doctrines preached in the North of Ireland and countenanced in responsible quarters in Great Britain were the negation of Parliamentary and democratic government. If the Ulster minority were entitled to resist Home Rule by force, what answer could be made to the mass of the Irish people, were they frustrated in a purely Constitutional demand, and then claimed to paralyse the Government of Ireland? Speaking at the Alexandra Palace, North London, on 15 November, the First Lord of the Admiralty began by saying that, in spite of the Ulster threat of civil war and the desire among Liberals to put it to the proof, there was a general feeling in favour of a settlement by consent—that Nationalist Ireland should have self-government with the means and money to make it a success. Plans for the exclusion of Ulster multiplied: an elaborate scheme of “administrative devolution” was sketched by Lord Macdonnell in the Daily Chronicle of 13 November, including an Administrative Council for the four counties, fair representation therein of all parties being secured by proportional representation and by empowering the Lord-Lieutenant to nominate additional members, with delegated administration of certain agreed services, especially education and local government, and a corresponding share of the transferred revenues. Four days later The Times published a “forecast” that the operation of the Home Rule Bill in Ulster would be suspended for a term of years, and Mr. William O’Brien (at Mitchelstown, 16 November) again pleaded for settlement by consent. But the temper of the Ulster extremists was shown by a letter published in the Irish Churchman (13 November) and attributed to a prominent Protestant, intimating that the Ulstermen had the promise of aid from “a powerful Continental Monarch”—obviously the German Emperor—who would be welcomed by the Protestants of Ireland if the King broke his Coronation oath by signing the Home Rule Bill.

Mr. Bonar Law replied to a Prime Ministerial speech at Leeds by a great Unionist demonstration in the Theatre Royal, Dublin, on 28 November—described as the most impressive Unionist demonstration ever held in Ireland south of the Boyne. As he entered, the whole audience rose simultaneously and waved Union Jacks. A message was read from Lord Lansdowne, saying that even the Government had come to realise that the Home Rule Bill in its existing form could never become law, and that the Unionists, while waiting for a sign from the Government, would insist on a general election. Earlier that day he had described the Leeds speech as a full acceptance of Mr. Redmond’s orders, contested the Liberal contention that Home Rule had been an issue at the last general election, and referred the Prime Minister to the past history of the country—to the fall of James II., to the American Revolution when Lord North unquestionably had the people behind him, and to Walpole’s words on the declaration of war with Spain in 1739: “They are ringing bells to-day; they will be wringing their hands to-morrow.” On the day of his speech an important contribution to the Home Rule controversy had been made by the publication (27 November) of a manifesto signed by nearly 150 leading business men of the “three Southern Provinces” of Ireland, headed by Lord Kenmare and Lord Iveagh, and comprising railway chairmen and directors, bankers, stockbrokers, and representatives of all the industries and trades. It was a reasoned document of great length, concluding that if the Bill passed the progress of social reforms would be checked; capital would be deflected from Ireland and business credit granted only on more onerous terms; Irish Government loans would be impossible except at high rates; local rates would rise; the natural increments of revenue from Imperial taxes would not belong to the Irish Government; expenditure must rise and new taxes must be imposed by uneconomic methods or by reducing the scale of old-age pensions; and manufacturing industries would go to other countries—against all of which there was only the satisfaction of national sentiment.

Liberal discontent at the prospect of increased naval expenditure was somewhat abated by the reply given by the Prime Minister to an influential deputation (17 December) representing about 100 Liberal members of Parliament, among them Messrs. Molteno, Leif Jones, Ponsonby and Bryce. The proceedings were confidential, but the Prime Minister was stated to have indicated that there would be no departure from the previous programme, that the increased expenditure was its automatic result, and that the Ministry was united on its policy. At the Lord Mayor’s banquet the First Lord of the Admiralty had already startled many ardent Liberals by announcing, in response to the toast of the Navy, that, should he still be at the Admiralty in 1914, he would have to present substantially increased Naval Estimates, though the Government would embrace and work for every opportunity of abating the competition in armaments.

The effect of any division on this subject in the ranks of the Ministerialists seemed likely to be more than counterbalanced by the rift set up by Tariff Reform in those of the Opposition. At a meeting following the Tariff Reform Conference at Manchester, the Earl of Derby, accounted a Free-Trade Unionist, said that he was maintaining the compromise made in January by presiding at a meeting of the other section of the party; and Mr. Austen Chamberlain, after describing his submission to the conditions laid down at Edinburgh as the bitterest sacrifice he had ever made, said that he would abide by them, but spoke strongly in favour of Tariff Reform, saying that within the limits of the Edinburgh programme the principle, at any rate, of Imperial Preference might be established. The Spectator treated the Conference as indicating that the Union would be the sole issue at the general election; but the Observer next day vigorously repudiated the notion that Tariff Reform would be kept in abeyance, and Mr. Page Croft, M.P., whose name had been prominent among the “Confederates,” issued a statement that he and his friends would tolerate no further betrayal of their cause, “however disastrous the consequences may seem to the enthusiasts for other causes who would betray us”; and they were rumoured to be opposing the selection by the party of candidates adverse to their own views.

Chapter IV — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Apart from labour unrest and the progress made towards the reunion of the Churches, the year was not very eventful, and its chief feature was the extensive emigration, chiefly for Canada from the Clyde. From 1 January to 24 May the emigrants by this outlet numbered some 36,000, an increase of 50 per cent. on the number in the corresponding period of 1912. Farm hands were said to be scarce in many districts, but the total acreage under crops and grass showed only a slight diminution. The exodus was, of course, made much of in the land campaign, as was the effect of sport in the past in the depopulating of the country; and complaints were made that legal difficulties obstructed the application of the Small Landholders Act. On the other hand, the traditional congestion in the islands continued, notably in Lewis, and attention was called to it in the House of Lords on 2 July. It appeared that there was not land enough in the island to provide holdings for the 1,200 applicants, and it was intimated that the Government would apply to the Land Court for a scheme, and that relief might be afforded by facilitating migration to the mainland.

The land campaign itself, prosecuted by the Chancellor of the Exchequer and others in Great Britain, collected startling instances of urban land prices—200 years’ purchase for land required by the City of Leeds for waterworks; 677 years’ purchase for land bought by the Hastings Corporation; a price equivalent to 1,000,000l. per acre for a small bit of land needed to widen a street at Oldham—and costs of compulsory acquisition which in cases quoted were more than the price. The first condition of urban land reform was held to be the establishment of a cheap and expeditious procedure by which municipalities could acquire land, not merely for specific public purposes but in anticipation of development, with Land Commissioners to adjudicate on market value; the Imperial Exchequer should also take a larger share of certain burdens; there should be a national survey of housing including an inventory of all the slums; facilities for the erection of new houses and transit; and, for the poor who could not pay for a better house, an extension of the Wages Boards Act, while the problem of casual labour was to be met by opening up the resources of the land. Addressing a meeting later, the Chancellor, after ridiculing the Tariff Reformers’ remedy of a tax on food, spoke of the importance of the land question as affecting the supply of food and as reducing emigration. In Newcastle, Tynemouth and neighbouring towns one-third of the population was overcrowded; the great municipalities had struggled manfully to meet these conditions, but their main difficulties were the prices of land and the burden of rates.

Ireland. The Home Rule conflict remained the dominant interest. Mr. Redmond, insisting that the ideal of Nationalists was a blend of all races, classes and creeds, and that what animated opponents of Home Rule was fear of the loss of ascendancy, declared that Ireland would never submit to their threats; but it would be worth paying a large price to obtain a settlement by consent. Let these men say what they wanted, and there was no demand that the Nationalists were not prepared to consider carefully, so long as it was consistent with the principle of a settlement based on national self-government for Ireland. But they would not be intimidated or bullied into the betrayal of Ireland. They did not desire the exclusion of any Irishman from British citizenship; what they demanded was admission for themselves to the British Constitution. At an overflow meeting he endorsed the words of the chairman (Sir Walter Runciman)—“Full speed ahead!”—and many Liberals agreed with him. Speaking at Alloa he told an audience that religious persecution was out of the question in Ireland; the settlement of the University question on lines satisfactory to the Roman Catholics had broken the barrier between Catholic and Protestant students. There were more Catholic students in Trinity College, Dublin, than ever; and in the University of Belfast 25 per cent. were Catholic. Galway College, whose students were mainly Catholic, had elected two Protestants over Catholics to professorships. He would not have been a Home Ruler unless Home Rule meant a free Ireland.

Meanwhile Captain Craig was telling an Ulster Unionist audience at Ballymoney that the Ulster Volunteer Force now numbered 88,000, that an indemnity was guaranteed of more than 1,000,000l. for loss of wages and the like sustained by the volunteers while serving, that homes in England were being found for the women and children by an association under the Duchess of Abercorn, and that a scheme of provisional government was complete. Fresh Unionist efforts were made both in Ulster and in Great Britain to rouse the electorate of the latter to a sense of the impending danger; a new anti-Home Rule campaign was planned in the North of Scotland, and Mr. Balfour addressed its opening meeting of 3,000 persons at Aberdeen (3 November). He laid stress on the gravity of the situation in Ulster, which he thought busy Englishmen and Scotsmen had been slow to realise. He described the Home Rule Bill as an experiment in Federalism of the most impossible, unexampled and preposterous character, as it disregarded the necessary principle of Federalism that all the separate units should be treated alike. It could not be an instalment of Home Rule all round, nor could it relieve congestion in the Imperial Parliament. But every argument for the separate treatment of Ireland implied a further argument for the separate treatment of Ulster. Never yet had the full measure of Ulster’s unalterable detestation of the Bill been thoroughly appreciated in Ireland, Scotland or England; it was totally different from ordinary political or religious differences; the Bill not only drove Ulstermen into a connexion which they hated, but it left them with the same burdens of taxation for all Imperial objects as England and Scotland, without giving them their full share of Parliamentary representation—a course avoided in the Scottish Home Rule Bill as being inconsistent with the dignity of Scotland.

Labour unrest in Dublin produced a separate drama. After the conviction of Mr. Larkin, the statements for the prosecution were to the effect that the speaker cared as much for the King (in whose name a meeting had been forbidden) as for the magistrate who proclaimed it: people could make Kings or unmake them; “I never said ‘God save the King’ but in derision. I say it now in derision.” Some Liberal papers compared this trial bitterly with the immunity enjoyed by Sir Edward Carson, and even the Unionist Spectator said that Mr. Larkin had been condemned on a charge of lèse majesté. In Labour and advanced Liberal circles the indignation was intense, and a meeting in the Albert Hall, London (1 November), for admission to which there were 40,000 applications, was addressed by Mr. Ben Tillett, several suffragists male and female, and Mr. Bernard Shaw, and the majority of those present pledged themselves to attend every meeting addressed by a Cabinet Minister and heckle him till Mr. Larkin was released—and the pledge was kept. The municipal elections of that day threw little light on the political situation, but they showed an increase of the Labour strength: a net gain of one seat for the Unionists (after serious losses in 1910), and a considerable loss of Liberal seats to Labour and Socialist candidates. In the whole of England and Wales there were but five women candidates; three were elected and two defeated—Miss Dove, who had been on the Council at High Wycombe for some years and had nearly been Mayor, and Miss Guinness, Vice-Principal of the Cheltenham Ladies’ College, at Cheltenham.

Speaking at Bromsgrove on 19 November, Mr. Austen Chamberlain, while favouring the proposed conversation between Ministers and the Unionist leaders, said that the supremacy of the Imperial Parliament must be beyond challenge, and that in any further extension of local government all parts of the United Kingdom must share alike. There must be some court of appeal to hold the balance between Nationalists and Protestants in Ireland; and that court was the Imperial Parliament.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1913

Great industrial prosperity, dear money, and continued severe depression on the Stock Exchanges of the world were the outstanding features of business in 1913. Great Britain fully participated in the general commercial activity, as was made clear in such signs as the large figures of the Bankers’ Clearing House returns, railway traffic receipts, and foreign trade. High rates for money were due partly to the heavy demands of trade and partly to the continuance of the fighting in the Balkans with all the fears it caused of a possible extension to the Great Powers.

Shipbuilding had good reason to be satisfied with the results for the year. Scotch ship-plates fell from 8l. 7s. 6d. to 6l. 7s. 6d., and North-East Coast plates from 8l. 5s. to 6l. The cost of a principal factor in shipbuilding was thus considerably reduced, though another important item—the labour cost—which had been advancing steadily in recent years seemed unlikely to show any falling off in future. On the whole, the production of tonnage exceeded the great output for 1911.

The land-values duties, as already noted in the Budget debate, continued to yield little immediate revenue apart from the Mineral Rights Duty, while the cost of valuation remained heavy. Ministerial finance was charged by the Opposition with over-optimism; the Chancellor maintained that he had never expected much immediate revenue from the increment and land-value duties, that building depression was due to pre-1909 overbuilding and to Budget agitation and had recovered in the North, and that by March 1915 the whole valuation would be complete and adaptable, with a very small adjustment, to local taxation purposes. Retention of the sugar duty was justified by the need of paying off South African war debt; spending of revenue as it came in, even though it was really arrears, was defended from the Treasury bench.

In the wider political economy of the year, the land campaign linked industrial overcrowding and high urban land prices with rural exodus and emigration, while Tariff Reformers within the Unionist party continued to press Imperial Preference within the Edinburgh compromise, even as the Union and Ulster threatened to monopolise the electoral issue. Dear money, Balkan uncertainty, and Stock Exchange depression coexisted with the unmistakable signs of commercial activity at home—a combination which the Register treated as the distinctive business character of 1913.
