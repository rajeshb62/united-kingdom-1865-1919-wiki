Naval Operations, Vol. IV (1928)

Sir Julian S. Corbett’s official history covering the period from the aftermath of Jutland to the German decision for unrestricted submarine warfare — Home Waters, the Mediterranean campaigns, the outer theatres, the German raiders, and the transformation of German naval strategy.

Source

Corbett, Sir Julian S. History of the Great War: Naval Operations, Vol. IV. London: Longmans, Green and Co., 1928.

This volume covers the period from June 1916 to early 1917, focusing on the strategic consequences of Jutland, Home Waters operations, the Mediterranean naval war, the outer theatres, the German surface raiders, the German decision for unrestricted submarine warfare, and the opening of that campaign.

Chapter I — After Jutland

Admiral Jellicoe’s Battle Fleet (1st, 2nd, 4th and 5th Battle Squadrons), with the 4th Light Cruiser Squadron and the 11th, 12th and part of the 4th Destroyer Flotillas, reached Scapa between 10.30 a.m. and noon on June 2, 1916. That evening he reported the Grand Fleet re-fuelled, at four hours’ notice, and ready for sea. Beatty, answering an inquiry on the night of June 3, reported New Zealand, Indomitable and Inflexible ready for action and Lion, Tiger and Princess Royal available if necessary. Strategically Corbett sums up that Scheer had failed to cut off and overwhelm part of the British advanced forces, and had been unexpectedly entrapped into meeting the Grand Fleet; he extricated himself at considerable expense, yet both before and after the main action inflicted more serious losses than he could have contemplated. Jellicoe had outmanoeuvred and surprised the High Seas Fleet; the net result was to increase the Grand Fleet’s large margin of superiority as a combative force, a defence against invasion, and an instrument of blockade. Control of the North Sea remained in British hands, and any expectation that it might be weakened or taken away was finally dissipated. Merchant vessels that had been lying up put to sea on June 2 without hesitation.

Public judgment, however, was not purely strategic. Britain had looked for a decisive encounter that would mark a turning-point and give spectacular proof of maritime supremacy; Germany, without experience of naval war, was moved by ambition and apprehension. A second Trafalgar was, among possible results, the only one that would not have surprised either side; what actually happened was unexpected by both. In the German navy the severe injuries and the necessity of retiring from close action caused some dismay, but among officers the marked feeling was relief and congratulation at having met the Grand Fleet and escaped without something like complete destruction. Guidance of public opinion differed greatly: German censorship and propaganda were practised with premeditation and mechanical efficiency; British practice treated candour as the most effective psychological influence in the long run. Admiral von Holtzendorff’s dictum that “in life it is not things as they are which decide, but the images people make of them” is set against the First Lord’s first communiqué of the evening of June 2—candid, accurate and restrained, listing heavy British losses (Queen Mary, Indefatigable, Invincible, Defence, Black Prince, Warrior abandoned, and destroyers Tipperary, Turbulent, Fortune, Sparrowhawk, Ardent and others unaccounted for) while claiming serious German losses without offering the nation any estimate of the measure of success or of the resulting naval position. The announcement caused dismay and even indignation because it disappointed high hopes without guidance.

The German Official Report, dated June 1 and published on the 2nd, reached neutral countries a whole day before English news. It claimed destruction of the battleship Warspite, battle cruisers Queen Mary and Indefatigable, two armoured cruisers, a small cruiser, destroyer leaders and many destroyers, and admitted only Wiesbaden and Pommern lost, with Frauenlob and some torpedo-boats missing. Corbett notes the overestimates—no large British battleship or light cruiser had been lost, and no British battleships damaged by German gunfire except three of Beatty’s advanced squadron—and the deliberate concealments: nothing of the grounding of Seydlitz, nor of the abandonment and sinking of Elbing, Rostock and the battle cruiser Lützow. British newspaper comment was at first a chorus of disappointment, moderated by responsible voices pointing to the enemy’s retreat. On June 5 a further Admiralty report stated that when the main body of the British fleet came into contact a very brief period sufficed to compel the severely punished High Seas Fleet to seek refuge in protected waters, the manoeuvre rendered possible by low visibility and mist. The chapter thus frames professional opinion and the effect on fleet tactics against the dual reality of strategic British control and public misunderstanding of an indecisive general action.

Chapter II — Home Waters, June to October 1916

The chapter opens with the decision to send Lord Kitchener to Russia at the head of a military and financial mission, to explain British credit policy and stiffen Allied cohesion after the Russian defeats of 1915. The Tsar welcomed the choice; Kitchener declared himself ready on May 26. The Admiralty ordered Jellicoe to convey the party to Archangel. They lunched with him at Scapa on June 5 and embarked in the cruiser Hampshire (Captain H. J. Savill). In a north-easterly gale Jellicoe rejected the eastern Orkneys route (submarines reported; destroyer escort would not keep up) and the Pentland Firth–Cape Wrath route (submarine report; sweeping incomplete), and chose the western side of the Orkneys, ordinarily used by fleet auxiliaries, less exposed, and thought free of mines so far north since the Moewe’s previous raid. Hampshire got under way at 4.45 p.m.; destroyers Unity and Victor met her off Tor Ness but were ordered back when the head sea proved too much. At about 7.40 there was an explosion that seemed to tear the centre of the ship out; she sank in minutes about a mile and a half from shore between the Brough of Birsay and Marwick Head. Fourteen men reached the shore on Carley rafts; two died before rescue. She had struck one or more mines laid by Lieutenant-Commander Kurt Beitzen from U 75 on the night of May 28/29—one of the submarines watching British bases during the German fleet’s sortie before Jutland—twenty-two mines in several groups about seven metres below high water, without interference from patrols.

After Jutland Jellicoe’s first business was to set North Sea control in motion again; cruiser sweeps resumed, four times in the month with nothing to report, though false raider alarms occasionally altered dispositions of the 10th Cruiser Squadron. In the southern North Sea the enemy was more active: early in June the Admiralty learned that the 2nd German Destroyer Flotilla had been sent to Zeebrugge to menace communications with Holland and shipping in the Downs. On the morning of June 8 twelve destroyers appeared east of Dunkirk and were engaged by the monitor Lord Clive and Tribal destroyers; they turned back before Harwich forces could reach them. Tyrwhitt was ordered to detach two light cruisers and eight destroyers as permanent reinforcement for the Dover Patrol. Forces in the Flanders Bight were distributed between Dover, Dunkirk and Harwich—at Harwich the 5th Light Cruiser Squadron, 9th and 10th Destroyer Flotillas; at Dover Attentive, Swift, twenty-four destroyers, eight “P” boats and fourteen monitors; Dunkirk drawn from Harwich and Dover. Nominally eight light cruisers, three flotilla leaders, sixty-eight destroyers, eight “P” boats and fourteen monitors covered the Downs and Flanders Bight, but available striking force rarely exceeded thirty destroyers and four light cruisers. Toward the end of June a permanent Dutch patrol of five to ten destroyers and two light cruisers was instituted from the Sunk to the Schouwen Bank and the Maas, to cover a trading agreement diverting Dutch trade from Germany. Flanders Flotilla minelaying increased—fifteen submarines operating as minelayers and commerce raiders; approaches to Lowestoft were particularly visited, fifty mines swept up, eight merchantmen and an armed trawler lost. When Jellicoe visited the Admiralty late in the month to discuss Jutland, the conference agreed that dispositions had stood the test; disasters were attributed to technical faults of material and design, amounting practically to a vote of confidence in the Commander-in-Chief’s leadership and policy.

Chapter III — Home Waters, October 1916 to February 1917

After Jutland the High Seas Fleet was for some months unequal to covering a serious attack on the Dover Straits, and Scheer’s and von Holtzendorff’s opinions behind the scenes brought the official decision restricting fleet action to support of the submarine campaign. Yet anti-submarine dispositions were becoming oppressive to the U-boats; a limited offensive by unsupported light forces under cover of darkness might interfere with those dispositions and cause apprehension among transport and merchant shipping. On those principles Captain Andreas Michelsen, Commodore of the High Seas Fleet flotillas, left for Zeebrugge with the 3rd and 9th Flotillas during the night of October 23. The Admiralty assumed a Flanders Bight movement, ordered Jellicoe short notice for steam, and directed Tyrwhitt to assemble east of the North Hinder; Michelsen hugged the coast, passed the Maas light-vessel about 2 a.m. on the 24th, and contact was never made. Warning telegrams then went to Vice-Admiral Sir Reginald Bacon that a destroyer flotilla had arrived at Zeebrugge and that the French reported concentration of armed barges on the Ostend–Bruges Canal—a surprise after two years of Belgian ports used only as submarine bases. Tyrwhitt reinforced Dover with Carysfort, Laforey, Liberty, Lucifer and Laurel on the 25th.

Bacon had to distribute inadequate forces among the seaward flank of the Allied armies, the Beachy Head–Downs route, shipping in the Downs anchorage (food supplies of the capital), the barrage and its unarmed, wireless-less drifters, and the Folkestone–Boulogne transport route. He ranked the Downs first—comparing a serious raid to the Dutch Medway expedition of 1667—and the barrage second; the cross-Channel route he put last. U-boat activity the previous week had doubled escort and patrol demands; Attentive’s boilers were open. He could not go to sea himself but must remain at the telephone centre. On the 26th he sent Laforey’s division to Dunkirk (with Swift, Syren, Racehorse, Falcon, Myrmidon), Lawford’s division to the Downs, Zulu and two “P” boats to the traffic route, Flirt to support the drifters, six Tribals (Viking, Mohawk, Tartar, Nubian, Amazon, Cossack) as striking force at Dover, and 30-knotters as reserve. Michelsen sailed at dusk: 9th Flotilla to raid the Dover–Calais transport line, 3rd against the drifters and barrage, half-flotillas assigned distinct zones divided by Colbart–Sandettie and Varne lines. At about 9.20 the 18th Half Flotilla passed Laforey’s division unseen by the British; at 9.35 Flirt challenged the same half flotilla, received a repeated signal, and Lieutenant Kellett held on without reporting, thinking he had met Laforey turning back. Shortly after ten Michelsen’s 5th Half Flotilla attacked the 10th Drifter Division; Spotless Prince, Datum and Gleaner of the Sea sank under searchlight and gunfire. The raid thus opened with heavy losses among the barrage watchers after repeated failures of contact and report—setting the pattern of German night destroyer raids against the Straits that the chapter follows through the winter into early 1917.

Chapter IV — The Outer Theatres (East and West Africa, Mesopotamia, Baltic) and the Mediterranean

The destruction of Königsberg in July 1915 removed the last menace to Indian Ocean trade routes but did not end the East African campaign. Von Lettow-Vorbeck still held practically the whole colony; on Lake Tanganyika German armed steamers remained unopposed. Vice-Admiral H. G. King-Hall at the Cape, stripped of Weymouth, Pyramus, Laurentic and with Hyacinth and Pioneer needing refit, was left with Challenger, Laconia, the tug Duplex and a few whalers, only partly successful against German supply ships though he put Präsident out of action up the Lindi river. In April 1915 the Admiralty determined to treat Tanganyika as an outlying sea of British naval power. Two fast motor boats, Mimi and Tou-Tou (40 feet, 15 knots, each with a 3-pounder and Maxim), under Commander G. Spicer-Simson with twenty-eight officers and men bound to secrecy, were shipped from Tilbury on June 11, railed from the Cape to Fungurume, drawn 140 miles by steam tractors to Sankisia (nearly 200 bridges built; water sacrificed to the traction engines), then by rail, Lualaba River (barrels lashed under the boats when the river was low) and final rail to Lukuga/Albertville by October 28. A harbour, Kalemie, was blasted out under the fort guns. Launched on December 23 and tried next day at 18½ knots, the boats on December 26 cut off and captured the wooden steam gunboat Kingani (7 knots, 37 mm Hotchkiss); she sank from gunfire but was raised, rearmed with a Belgian 12-pounder, and commissioned as Fifi by January 15, 1916. On February 9 the flotilla sank Hedwig von Wissmann; thereafter the Germans burned Wami and blew up Graf von Gotson in harbour at Kigoma. The smallest and most distant naval expedition of the war had cleared Central Africa of German naval forces.

Home government then reinforced East Africa: Vengeance was ordered out to support attack on Dar es Salaam; in February 1916 General Smuts relieved the command after Smith-Dorrien, and Rear-Admiral E. F. B. Charlton assumed naval command with Vengeance, monitors and cruisers. The chapter’s remaining sections treat the Cameroons, Mesopotamia (including operations for the relief of Kut), the Baltic entrance and central Baltic, and Mediterranean episodes—the evacuation of the Serbian army, the first cruiser action in the Adriatic (December 29, 1915), and Salonica from January to June 1916—binding the outer theatres to the wider naval war while the Grand Fleet held the North Sea.

Chapter V — The Mediterranean, June 1916 to January 1917

The latter half of 1916 was a period of suspense and preparation: the Allies approached a danger zone at sea whose nature could not be foreseen with exactness. In Greece bitter antagonism between political parties complicated every calculation; a great combined squadron was occupied for six months in a naval operation that did not involve, on the British part, the firing of a single shot, yet depended at every step upon diplomatic change. British naval force acted as subordinate partner in a mixed command; diplomatic action was conducted by Ministers in concert with the French Commander-in-Chief. The British aim was to attain the end without actual use of force or even open threat; the French Government held that the time had come for sternness. Admiral Dartige du Fournet and Sir Francis Elliot were both men of ability and high sense of duty, but when French determination cut short the admiral’s opportunities for tact and pushed him forward by definite unconditional orders, British diplomacy was left behind and the naval commander deprived of consultative power. Failure followed: the last note’s demands related to domestic affairs of Greece, which foreign diplomats could supervise only with tact and time. M. Zaimis, heading a non-party ministry after Skouloudis, demobilised the army slowly and remained conciliatory, but the Allies’ insistence on a business ministry and new elections had already intervened on behalf of the constitutional party against King Constantine’s autocratic theory of government.

Events in the general theatre reshaped the Greek problem. On July 19 the Turks’ second attack on the Suez Canal was repulsed at Rumani while British and French naval detachments bombarded Marmarice, Makri, Phineka and Mersina. On August 2 General Sarrail, reinforced by the Serbian army and Russian and Italian detachments, launched his Macedonian offensive; the Bulgarians replied on both flanks. On the right, Greek forces between Demir Hissar and Kavala could not resist; the Kavala garrison surrendered to the Germans with all material (formal surrender September 19), and by late August practically all Macedonia east of the Struma was in Bulgarian hands. Wild rumours—Venizelos telling Elliot that retirement was preliminary to putting Greece into Bulgarian and German hands; French Naval Intelligence reporting rolling-stock at Ekaterini and German cavalry at Larissa—drove clamour for “strong measures.” British military experts showed the danger imaginary; the Government told Elliot that Athens could not be occupied until Salonica was defeated. Yet Paris, and to a lesser degree London, held that surrender of a fort and munitions gave the Allies right to corresponding concessions. On urgent French request the Vice-Admiral Eastern Mediterranean detached a force to Milo under Rear-Admiral Hayes-Sadler to co-operate with Dartige du Fournet; Admiralty instructions of August 26 forbade offensive action against Greece without explicit orders. French orders of the following day directed immobilisation of the Greek fleet and General Staff, seizure of enemy ships at Eleusis, control of telegraphs, wireless, coal, petrol and railways, and cutting railway communication between Athens and the Peloponnese. On the day those orders issued Rumania entered the war; Zaimis told Elliot the King thought the time had come to abandon neutrality and join the Allies, and Elliot warned that a demonstration and landing would be ill-timed. The British Government had already committed itself. The chapter follows further demands upon Greece, the landing parties at Athens, and submarine warfare through January 1917.

Chapter VI — The Raiders

Late in the autumn of 1916 the German High Command decided to attack outer trade routes with surface raiders, timed to start from two months to a month before unrestricted submarine warfare, and probably designed as a preliminary to that venture—combining cruiser attack on outer routes with submarine onslaught on home approach routes. Three raiders, if able to keep continuous attack in Atlantic, Pacific and Indian Oceans, might pin cruiser forces that would otherwise convoy near the British Isles. The Admiralty and Jellicoe had taken elaborate precautions: cruiser forces under standing orders to take up patrol stations in all North Sea exits as soon as a raider was known out. The raiders nevertheless eluded at the start because sailing dates were never known within seven days.

On the outer routes Rear-Admiral Sydney Fremantle took command of the 9th Cruiser Squadron on September 22, 1916 (King Alfred flag, Swiftsure, Donegal, Sutlej, Ophir, Avenger), with French 6e escadre légère under Rear-Admiral Jaurès (base Dakar) to the east; southward Highflyer and Marmora, then Glasgow, Amethyst, Macedonia, Orama and Edinburgh Castle off the south-east coast of America. Portuguese entry into the war had freed Funchal and the Cape Verde Islands, but U 53’s western Atlantic cruise had shown Madeira, Cape Verde and the Canaries within range of new German submarines, so Sierra Leone became squadron headquarters. Fremantle’s war orders provided for passages through thick shipping zones, never more than two ships at base, a ship continuously in the Azores, and frequent visits to the African coast; against raiders he would organise sweeps on a broad front with French co-operation on the eastern wing, and against a battle-cruiser raid either scout for battle cruisers from England or keep concentrated to force the enemy to burn coal—without accepting action unless circumstances were favourable. In November he conferred with Jaurès at Dakar and drew emergency orders for a submarine attack on Sierra Leone.

Count Nikolaus zu Dohna-Schlodien, retaining command of Moewe since his March 1916 return, slipped into the North Sea on November 22. About a week later Captain Nerger left in Wolf for the Indian Ocean; on December 21 Count von Luckner left in Seeadler for the Pacific. Dohna worked up the eastern North Sea in bad weather, turned west about the latitude of the Faroes, and by November 30 steered south. On December 2 he captured the British cold-storage vessel Voltaire about half-way on the great-circle track between Cape Clear and Cape Race—using his armament freely against her after gun—and a Dutch vessel that had heard the firing asked by signal whether the crew had been taken up, yet made no further report. Holding the North American trade route, he stopped the Belgian relief ship Samland on December 4 (safe-conduct from the German Embassy in New York), destroyed her wireless and let her go—limiting secrecy to three or four more days. Between the 6th and 12th he captured five British steamers (Cambrian Range, King George, Georgic, Yarrowdale, St. Theodore), sinking three and keeping Yarrowdale and St. Theodore. On December 9 Bermuda wireless warned of a raider. The Admiralty received Samland’s report on the 7th, informed Paris and Atlantic commands, held transports at the Cape, Dakar and Sierra Leone, and ordered five transports at sea back to Sierra Leone. Fremantle ordered a sweep toward Madeira and Josephine Bank with Jaurès on his right flank; an Admiralty telegram then suspended action pending a co-ordinated Atlantic search under Vice-Admiral M. E. Browning on the North American and West Indies Station and the other squadrons. The chapter continues with Leopard, Seeadler and Wolf and the long counter-measures against the raiders.

Chapter VII — German Naval Policy, 1916–1917

Although the concessions to America in April 1916 had been much criticised in Germany, the nation had on the whole thought them wise; less than a year later clamour for unrestricted warfare was so violent and unanimous that no Government could have resisted it. Ministers changed opinions as radically as the populace. Four causes are named: the Naval Staff’s unswerving conviction that the submarine fleet could end the war in German victory; the 1916 campaign against commerce so successful that additional freedom seemed hard to refuse; the failure of the peace negotiations; and the indefinite, hesitating expression of the few political opponents. The April 1916 decision had followed an acute struggle between Chancellor and military leaders. Bethmann Hollweg and Karl Helfferich headed a peace party, supported by Jagow and by Count Bernstorff, who knew from Washington that America could make war effectively—a point German professional soldiers never grasped. These four foresaw that unrestricted submarine warfare would turn America from probable mediator into certain enemy. Falkenhayn, still Chief of the General Staff but with reputation shipwrecked at Verdun, continued to advocate unrestricted warfare as the only measure to break military deadlock, yet with declining influence. Powerful associations (Bund der Landwirte, Zentral Verband Deutscher Industrieller), Prince Salm of the Navy League, and Tirpitz out of office drove propaganda; Erzberger likened Tirpitz’s influence over the masses to the piper of Hamelin, yet the navy’s vigorous campaign throughout the year proved large sections still unconvinced.

Naval censorship, first under the Admiralty then the Naval Staff, was ruthlessly one-sided: Tirpitz memoranda circulated privately; contrary industrial warnings were held up; inaccurate figures given to the Bundesrat forced the Chancellor to telegraph contradictions across Germany. The constitutional question—whether resumption was political (Chancellor) or military (Chief of Staff)—produced deadlock only the Emperor could break. Scheer, sailing to raid Lowestoft on April 24, recalled all submarines from trade routes and refused commerce warfare according to international law, though only those rules were sanctioned by the promise to Washington. After Jutland he pressed the Emperor that the “moral impression” on neutrals created a favourable atmosphere for resuming the U-boat campaign in all its intensity—an extreme perversion, Corbett remarks, of the Bismarckian theory of imponderabilia; when first reports vanished, the true result was as clear as a well-drawn balance sheet: the High Seas Fleet had inflicted loss on a battle-cruiser squadron and escaped with its life, but control of the sea was irrevocably in British hands. Tirpitz likewise failed to distinguish words from realities. The Naval Staff had a better reason: failure of the High Seas Fleet left no other weapon but the submarine. Müller remonstrated with Scheer’s “everything or nothing” on June 23; the Chancellor visited Scheer a week later and stated he was against unrestricted campaign because it would place the fate of the Empire in the hands of a U-boat commander. Scheer yielded in practice to Holtzendorff’s restricted warfare in the autumn. The Chancellor’s best issue seemed American mediation—knowing the Naval Staff and High Command were only temporarily silenced—until the failure of peace negotiations and the rising domestic clamour carried the decision for unrestricted submarine warfare in January 1917.

Chapter VIII — The Mediterranean, January to August 1917

Three Allies divided the Mediterranean into zones of control, co-ordinated but independent: French around Algeria and Tunis and west and south of Greece; British between Malta and Egypt and in the whole Aegean; Italian east, west and south of Italy. Differences of nationality, language and outlook interfered with perfect co-operation; ocean stretches such as Messina–Egypt or Marseilles–Salonica could be protected only by fixed patrolled routes or direct escort as vessels crossed zones. Troop transports and ammunition ships received destroyer escort for whole voyages when arrangements could be made, but occasionally arrangements broke down. In February the Italian troop transport Minas, escorted by an Italian destroyer only to the limit of the British zone, was left alone because Admiral Ballard at Malta had not been told she would need British escort; she was torpedoed and sunk with the loss of 870 lives. The disaster brought to a head the demand for centralised command and an international conference of Mediterranean naval authorities, eventually fixed for Corfu at the end of April.

Sinkings remained heavy: in February fifty ships (101,000 tons); in March thirty-six ships (72,000 tons), including the French battleship Danton, zigzagging under one destroyer’s escort, sunk by U 64 (Commander Morath) with two torpedoes in three-quarters of an hour—the escort’s depth charges failing as the submarine dived deep. Fixed patrolled routes had already been discredited by the Admiralty, which approached the French for dispersal on open stretches. From January 15 British ships east of Malta sailed independently on prescribed tracks; in six weeks only four British vessels were lost there, all torpedoed without warning. Heavy losses on the French-patrolled Algerian coast in late February and early March—seven defensively armed vessels torpedoed without warning by day—led from March 7 to abandonment of that route for British ships, which hugged the Spanish coast to Cape San Antonio then proceeded on varying large-zigzag tracks never less than thirty miles from Africa till south of Sardinia, so that a submarine meeting one ship would wait long before another appeared. No more British armed vessels were sunk in the western basin in March, though submarines remained on the Algerian route. East of Malta three British ships were sunk in March; after sixty-eight peaceful days Alexandria was raided by U 63, which torpedoed two British ships within fifteen miles of the port (one a collier under escort of four auxiliaries that saw nothing) and destroyed an Egyptian sailing vessel by gunfire.

April intensified the effort: at least twenty-four separate cruises, twice the hours of March; Mediterranean steamers destroyed increased threefold, tonnage a quarter of world losses for the month. U 52 (Lieut.-Commander Walter Hans), homeward from Cattaro, sank Italian sail off Malta, torpedoed the Italian liner Ravenna off Genoa, sank the United States Missourian, then the Ellerman liner City of Paris (general cargo from India, thirteen passengers) some fifty miles south of Nice after she had sheered out on successive “Allo” submarine warnings—only to meet the enemy; of crew and passengers 122 perished, boats found after thirty-six hours with lascars dead from cold. Hans continued off Catalonia and toward Gibraltar, sinking further steamers. The chapter then treats the attack on the Otranto barrage and the Adriatic action of May 15, and submarine warfare from May to August 1917 under the still imperfect Allied zone system.

Chapter IX — Unrestricted Submarine Warfare, February to April 1917

Jutland, though not fought to a finish, had ended one stage of the naval war and defined the form of the final struggle. German naval chiefs realised instantly that if Britain was to be overthrown at sea the blow must be struck by submarines, not the High Seas Fleet; unrestricted U-boat war remained the only alternative. The British Admiralty and War Cabinet could not foresee the moment of decision at Pless between counsellors of violence and of prudence, but diplomatic strain between Germany and America was visible to all, and no responsible person in Britain had not for some months weighed the coming danger. Late in October 1916 Jellicoe wrote that losses in British and neutral merchant ships might by early summer 1917 so affect imports of food and necessaries as to force peace terms the military position on the Continent would not justify. Beatty spoke of danger jeopardising the fate of the nation. Invited to confer with the Government on November 2, Jellicoe stressed that submarine attack was being extended steadily westwards into open waters at the outer end of the Channel. The Admiralty memorandum added that of all problems no doubt the most formidable was submarine attack upon merchant vessels: “No conclusive answer has as yet been found to this form of warfare; perhaps no conclusive answer ever will be found. We must for the present be content with palliation.”

A subsequent Admiralty conference of highest naval opinion agreed unanimously only on the need for an officer of high rank to co-ordinate all anti-submarine operations. Suggestions ranged widely: attack on German coasts (Bayly at Queenstown long before; Captain Percy Royds on Zeebrugge; Tyrwhitt on the Zeebrugge locks—declined after conference with Jellicoe, Bacon and Oliver); intensive mining; and a far-sighted group who realised merchant shipping would have to be placed under escort. The decision was to create a special Admiralty division for anti-submarine operations under Admiral Duff, late Second in Command of the 4th Battle Squadron, who with Jellicoe (now First Sea Lord) left the Grand Fleet for Whitehall in the first days of December. Shortly after Duff took office the Admiralty surveyed methods then in force and future policy: special hunting patrols of destroyers and “P” boats based at Portsmouth and Devonport; air patrols on the south coast; hydrophone ships in experimental detachments; deep minefields on known U-boat tracks with patrol craft to force diving; motor-launch mining close to German river mouths; a mine barrage across the Heligoland Bight; and special submarine patrols at the northern exit of the North Sea.

Review of British submarine employment since 1914 showed older boats allocated to coast defence and newer “D” and “E” boats as Harwich overseas striking force; offensive patrols in the Bight had torpedoed five German warships between August 1914 and July 1915, yet when Boedicker bombarded Yarmouth and Lowestoft in April 1916 the submarines were not put to practical test as coast defence. By August 1916 the North Sea was divided into four submarine patrols (Maas, Terschelling, Horn Reefs, Skagerrak). Beatty, as the new Commander-in-Chief, criticised the organisation forcibly: ten of eighty-six available boats patrolled enemy waters; the remainder waited on an imaginary invasion danger; isolated cruising without specified objectives had achieved nothing commensurate with losses. He urged re-grouping—forty newer boats attached to the Grand Fleet for continuous specially devised operations. The chapter then turns to German estimates of British endurance at sea, the achievements of submarine warfare 1914–1917, the opening of unrestricted warfare on February 1, 1917, the successive attacks on the Dover Straits (February 25–26, March 17–18, April 20–21), and the submarine campaign of April 1917—the months in which shipping losses rose to crisis and the Admiralty still sought palliation while the measure that would save the situation, convoy, remained the foresight of a minority.
