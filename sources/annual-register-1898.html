The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1898

The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1898

Sep 15, 202615 min read

The Annual Register 1898

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Political Situation; Platform Speeches; Bye-elections

When the year opened, public attention centred on foreign affairs. The European concert had kept the peace but deepened jealousies and shown itself unable to enforce counsels on Turkey beyond the retrocession of Thessaly; Great Britain was described as for the moment the best-hated country in the world. The struggle for supremacy shifted to the Far East: Russia pressed claims as heir to Manchuria; Germany demanded a Far Eastern port; France sought a southern counterpoise to Hong-Kong; Pekin became the centre of intrigue while British prestige in Asia was loudly taunted abroad and little defended by the home press. In Africa, French outposts were said to push beyond Nigerian frontiers, and a transcontinental French expedition was believed to aim at the upper Nile against British–Egyptian advance from the north. The north-west Indian frontier campaign was practically over (Afridis recognising futility of resistance), though Beluchistan clans remained restive. Crete stayed deadlocked over a governor; Russia suggested Prince George of Greece; Turkey (and German influence) demurred to any but a Mussulman ruler. Austria–Hungary revived race animosities; Spanish Carlists stirred war feeling against the United States over Cuba; in France the Army grew impatient of civil government.

At home, seven bye-elections (five English, two Irish) tested the new registers. Mr. Bryce at Manchester (7 January) was dispiriting to Liberals; Mr. Balfour (10 January) reviewed Voluntary Schools and Workmen’s Compensation at length, dwelt on the cost of Irish local government (some £700,000 a year as a price for interesting Irishmen of all classes in local affairs), admitted the concert’s Cretan failure, restated Indian frontier objects (protect those within the frontier; keep tribes free of external interference; maintain routes needed to defend the Ameer), and defined China interests as commercial not territorial. The next day he endorsed army attention alongside naval strength. Lord Kimberley at Wymondham largely endorsed China policy and the concert estimate, criticised frontier provocation, and opposed female suffrage while leaving it an open Liberal question.

Bye-elections mixed results: Plymouth and York vacancies after Radical deaths did not yield easy Unionist gains; Mid Staffordshire saw a narrow Liberal Unionist win (Gibbons 4,115, Thorne 4,004); South-East Durham converted a Unionist majority of 114 into a Radical majority of 275 (Richardson 6,286, Lambton 6,011). Sir Michael Hicks-Beach at Swansea (17 January) told the Chamber of Commerce the Government wanted no territorial acquisition in China but would keep the door open “at whatever cost, even… if necessary, at the cost of war”; Chamberlain at Liverpool endorsed firmness for free markets; Hicks-Beach at Bristol (19 January) praised naval strength and promised sailors and army votes without new tax burdens, while softening China language. Chamberlain mocked the National Liberal Federation’s Derby programme of eleven constitutional changes as a cycle of dissolutions and Lords conflict; Morley at Stirling (27 January) challenged his free-trade-under-the-flag claims. Salisbury’s remarks on manageable municipal areas were read as a design to break up the London County Council — a charge that coloured metropolitan politics into Chapter II.

Chapter II — Parliament Opens; Address; China, West Africa, Irish Local Government

Public and parliamentary apathy marked the opening (8 February). Conflicting Cabinet influences left dangerous problems unsolved; a divided Opposition lacked a common leader. The Queen’s Speech stressed friendly relations; Greco–Turkish peace with territory practically unchanged; Cretan autonomy still delayed; Khalifa advance prompting British troops to Berber; Abyssinian treaty; West Indian sugar depression and bounty conference hopes with interim relief; Indian frontier fanaticism and plague, famine nearly ended; Estimates framed amid foreign armaments. Irish Local Government was the programme’s prominent feature.

Debate on the Address ranged over China, West Africa, Irish grievances and a Catholic university. Kimberley and Salisbury spoke on foreign affairs; Harcourt criticised and Balfour defended. French encroachments in West Africa and “dismemberment of China” dominated foreign anxiety. The Local Government (Ireland) Bill was introduced; the Benefices Bill advanced against simony and improper presentation. Army, Navy, and Civil Estimates followed. London County Council elections (3 March) gave Progressives 68 seats to Moderates’ 48 (from a 59–59 tie in 1895), felt as a Progressive municipal triumph that echoed into the Stepney parliamentary contest. Chamberlain defended West African policy and a frontier-force vote; Abyssinia mission and Chartered Company business appeared. Sir E. Ashmead-Bartlett’s resolution (1 March) on maintaining Chinese territorial independence was treated awkwardly: Harcourt found the mover and seconder contradictory; Curzon treated it as endorsement yet deferred full discussion while negotiations continued — the episode fed charges of dilatory Far Eastern policy. West Indian relief votes, Lord Roberts on frontier policy, private members’ bills, Indian currency debate (29 March), public-buildings vote, second reading of Irish Local Government, and further China debates (Port Arthur lease to Russia; Wei-hai-wei as later British counter-lease) closed the early session narrative.

Chapter III — South Wales Coal; Spain and America; Vaccination; Budget; Gladstone

Wei-hai-wei and the Atbara victory briefly revived ministerial fortunes, but a South Wales coal strike darkened Easter: some 100,000 miners struck against the sliding scale (standard 10s. per ton since 1876), demanding a 10 per cent rise with a floor of 1s. per ton and output limitation the owners refused amid world competition. Men withheld plenary powers from delegates; deadlock ensued.

Attention shifted to the United States and Spain over Cuba. British sympathy ran with America; when Austria sounded France and Germany for joint representation at Washington contingent on British co-operation, Balfour (acting Foreign Secretary) instructed Pauncefote that under no circumstances would Britain adopt a policy Washington might deem unfriendly — collapsing the scheme. Sir Edward Grey at Hartlepool censured Chinese dilatoriness (Port Arthur; withdrawal of British ships; Wei-hai-wei still uncertain); Courtney at Bodmin surveyed China to Peru, hoped West African calm amid Atlantic events, and defended Salisbury’s experience. British South Africa Company and Rhodesian railway talk featured; Vaccination Bill second reading (19 April; carried 9 May, 201–50) abolished arm-to-arm vaccination for calf-lymph, extended age to twelve months, allowed home vaccination, and ended repeated penalties for conscientious objection.

Hicks-Beach’s Budget (21 April) reported booming trade: total State revenue including local taxation account £116,016,000; estimated 1898–9 revenue about £108,615,000 against expenditure £106,829,000 (surplus ~£1,786,000 before remissions). He could not cut income tax generally but extended abatements on £400–£700 incomes; cut unmanufactured tobacco duty by 6d. per pound (cigars excepted); left a thin margin (~£281,000) after remissions. Irish Local Government advanced in committee; Salisbury at the Albert Hall and Chamberlain at Birmingham spoke colonial policy; Lords debated colonies; bye-elections hurt Liberal Unionists (West Derbyshire majority cut; South Norfolk lost).

Gladstone died on Ascension Day (19 May). Commons adjourned; both Houses unanimously prayed for burial at public charge. Salisbury eulogised him as guided by a high moral ideal — “a great Christian statesman.” Harcourt and others joined tributes; the public funeral at Westminster closed the chapter’s domestic climax.

Chapter IV — Kau-lung; Dilke Debate; West African Convention; Session’s Close

Whitsuntide brought the ninety-nine-year lease of mainland opposite Hong-Kong (Kau-lung / Kowloon) and Lantao — strengthening Hong-Kong against mainland batteries, yet criticised as joining the dismemberment scramble and for exempting the native city from British jurisdiction. American friendliness grew: Queen’s birthday celebrations in the United States; a U.S.–Canada joint commission; London banquets and a committee for closer Anglo-Saxon relationship without formal alliance.

An Indian loan of £10,000,000 followed plague, famine, and frontier costs; Lord George Hamilton refused despondency (famine ~Rx.18,000,000; frontier war ~Rx.5,300,000; plague Rx.500,000). Sir Charles Dilke’s Foreign Office vote attack (10 June) and Asquith’s challenge to Chamberlain’s Birmingham “alliance” language drew Curzon and Chamberlain replies; Dilke’s reduction lost 254–128. Almost at once (15 June, just after midnight) the Anglo–French West African Convention was signed in Paris: France evacuated Boussa and Lower Niger places up to Ilo; boundary adjusted around Sokoto and toward Lake Chad; France excluded from the navigable Niger but leased two bonded reserves for thirty years.

Benefices Bill debates turned into Harcourt’s Protestant assault on ritualism and “conspiracy” against Reformation principles; Balfour tried to keep the bill to patronage and simony reform. Education vote and Gorst on voluntary schools; South Wales dispute continued; old-age pensions committee reported none of the schemes free of grave disadvantages. Egyptian “loan” of £798,000 was remitted as a gift so Egypt could borrow again for the Khartoum advance (reconquest already ~£1,805,000; further £750,000 anticipated) — Harcourt called it unsound finance; remitted 155–81. Irish Local Government passed; Vaccination in the Lords produced open conflict: Lister and Salisbury backed the conscience clause; peers rejected it 40–38; Commons reinstated it 129–34; after further peer resistance the bill’s compromise stood. Supplementary Navy Estimates for accelerated building amid Russian programmes passed despite Harcourt’s “flaming programme” charge. West Indian loan/aid (Chamberlain after earlier £120,000 vote) addressed bounty-fed sugar distress. Session closed early August with Eastern-policy echoes and a Board of Education bill floated for recess discussion.

Chapter V — Recess: China Rebuffs; Curzon; Omdurman; Fashoda; Harcourt

An exciting recess followed a dull session. August telegrams reported Russian success forcing revision of the Hong-Kong and Shanghai Bank Niu-Chwang railway loan (no mortgage, no foreign control even in default) and ratification (12 August, prorogation day) of the Pekin–Han-kau “Belgian” (French/Russian-financed) contract with arbitration by the minister of the largest financial interest — a double rebuff to Macdonald at Pekin. Curzon, appointed Viceroy at under forty, vacated Southport; the Radical Sir Henry Naylor-Leyland won 5,100 to Lord Skelmersdale’s 4,828 (Curzon had held it 5,163–4,399 in 1895), read as Lancashire dissatisfaction with Far Eastern policy despite Wyndham’s defence.

The Czar’s peace/armaments-conference proposal won respectful British credit despite Far Eastern rivalry, though practical hopes faded. South Wales strike ended 1 September on masters’ terms: sliding scale restored, 5 per cent immediate advance, Mabon’s Day abolished, scale to 1903 with limited early-notice if wages fell below 12 per cent above the 1879 standard — no true minimum wage. Federation-area wage settlements and the Trades Union Congress ran in parallel.

Omdurman (2 September): Kitchener’s Anglo–Egyptian army destroyed the Khalifa’s power; 21st Lancers’ charge and Macdonald’s Egyptian Brigade drew especial praise; Khartoum flags and Gordon memorial service (4 September) closed a debt “since the murder of General Gordon in 1885.” Cordial American relations continued. Cretan crisis peaked at Candia when British troops seizing the customs house were attacked for hours before Turkish soldiers intervened; after German and Austrian withdrawal from the concert, the four admirals’ regime and later settlement toward Prince George moved forward.

Fashoda: Marchand’s French party on the upper Nile after Omdurman produced published despatches and national unanimity. Rosebery at the Surrey Agricultural Association (personally responsible for Grey’s 1895 “unfriendly act” declaration) vowed no government could palter a week; Asquith backed the national position; warlike preparations underlined resolve until France withdrew Marchand. Chamberlain at Manchester (mid-November) treated Fashoda as symbol of Nile-valley control for Egypt, restated China “open door” objects, and warned agreements hold only when interest or strength enforces them. Monson’s Paris speech, country oratory on Far East and Home Rule, and Church ritual troubles filled the platforms.

On 14 December letters between Harcourt and Morley announced Harcourt’s retirement from Liberal Commons leadership — a party “rent by sectional disputes” made contested leadership impossible; Morley sorrowfully agreed. National Liberal Federation at Birmingham and year-end China modus vivendi rumours closed the recess.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Imperial soldiering was conspicuous: Highland and Lowland brigadiers (Macdonald, Wauchope, Maxwell) in the Soudan; Scottish regiments at Candia; Kitchener and Dufferin freed of Edinburgh; Gordons returned from Dargai fame. Rosebery (Associated Societies president) and Balfour (Chancellor) shared an Edinburgh University platform (25 October). No bye-election tested parties; Campbell-Bannerman and Asquith were named among possible Harcourt successors; Glasgow drafted ground-values legislation amid tramway and telephone ambitions. Equivalent-grant money (~£35,000) went to secondary/technical education; Scottish Education Department became sole secondary supervisor; St Andrews reunited with University College, Dundee. Post-engineering-strike Clyde shipbuilding set output records. Free and United Presbyterian union basis debated; Shorter Catechism 250th anniversary joint service in St Giles’s; Archbishop of Canterbury addressed the Church of Scotland Assembly on temperance (unprecedented).

Ireland. The Local Government Act was the absorbing event (first working deferred to 1899). The Fry commission on the Land Acts (unanimous) urged abolishing Civil Bill Court land jurisdiction, permanent well-paid assistant commissioners/valuers, case-stated powers, slower and reasoned appeals, and clarified “fair rent” practice. Distress, university, and Nationalist tactics at Westminster remained the backdrop already traced in earlier chapters.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. “The Dreyfus year”: Esterhazy’s acquittal; publication of the 1894/1897 charge reports; Zola’s J’accuse (7 January) in L’Aurore; street disorders and provincial anti-Semitic outbreaks; Zola–Perreux prosecution; ministry crises and Army–Republic tension through revision struggles that regrouped parties without finishing the drama by year-end.

Italy. Coalition and Triple Alliance continuity; domestic fiscal and social pressures in the Register’s usual Italian survey — secondary to the French crisis for European attention.

Chapter II — Germany and Austria-Hungary

Germany. Navy Bill drove the year: Reichstag Budget Committee fixed active and reserve squadron strengths; three vessel classes (line, large and small cruisers); large-cruiser designs (~8860 tons, high speed, triple screws); personnel and expenditure rules; clause restraining new consumption taxes if naval spend exceeded a mark ceiling. World-policy and Far Eastern stakes (Kiao-Chow) framed Anglo-German feeling.

Austria-Hungary. Dualist strain, race questions, and Balkan vigilance continued; Cretan concert withdrawal with Germany noted in English History.

Chapter III — Russia, Turkey, and Eastern Europe

Russia. Far Eastern advance (Port Arthur lease; northern railway pressure) and the Czar’s Hague peace initiative coexisted; gold and armament finance underwrote both prestige and the conference proposal.

Turkey and the East. Cretan administration under the admirals after Candia; path toward autonomy under a Christian high commissioner (Prince George). Armenian and Macedonian questions remained subordinate to Crete and the Egyptian reconquest’s diplomatic shadow.

Chapter IV — Minor States of Europe

Belgium, Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, and Norway receive the annual political survey. Spain’s year was consumed by the American war and colonial collapse; other states show army, franchise, and fiscal routine.

Chapter V — Asia

India. Frontier submissions early in 1898 (Lockhart ultimata; Afridi sections complying after Bazar Valley fighting and officer losses); plague and currency/loan politics (see English History); quiet Afghanistan under Abdurrahman despite Afridi refugee and raid frictions.

China, Hong Kong, Korea, Japan, Siam. Leasehold scramble — Russian Port Arthur/Talienwan, German Kiao-Chow, British Wei-hai-wei and Kau-lung extension, French southern claims — plus railway concessions and open-door controversy dominate; Japan and Korea in the balance-of-power narrative; Siam in Franco-British adjustment continuity.

Chapter VI — Africa

Egypt and the Soudan. Domestic progress under Cromer (Assouan/Assiout reservoir contract with Aird; budget equilibrium ~£E.10,440,000); Atbara then Omdurman destroyed Mahdist power; Fashoda confrontation and French withdrawal; Egyptian loan remission for the final advance (English History Ch. IV–V).

South Africa. Transvaal and Cape politics after the Raid years; presidential election atmosphere (Kruger and rivals); Uitlander and suzerainty tensions as unfinished business.

East, West, Central. West African Convention with France (Boussa/Niger/Lake Chad); continuing partition administration elsewhere.

Chapter VII — America

United States. Spanish–American War: Maine destroyed in Havana (15 February, 259 dead); court found a submarine mine without fixing blame; Cuban independence resolution; war finance and forces; Hawaii annexed (transfer 12 August); peace brought new Pacific and Caribbean responsibilities. Behring Sea award payments to Britain; bankruptcy act; Queen’s birthday anglophile demonstrations.

Canada, Newfoundland, Latin America, West Indies. Joint high commission with the United States; Laurier-era preferential and imperial themes; West Indian sugar distress mirrored in Chamberlain’s relief measures (English History).

Chapter VIII — Australasia

Equable progress without political earthquake; federation again stalled — Melbourne convention debated Senate basis, money bills, rivers, defence, and capital, then colonial jealousies (especially New South Wales free-trade suspicion) left the Commonwealth Bill unaccomplished, as in 1897.

Chronicle of Events in 1898

Daybook companion to Parts I–II: January opens with Faure’s carriage collision, engineering-dispute conferences, Cretan ambassadors’ plan, Miners’ Federation at Bristol, Lincolnshires to Wady Halfa, Transvaal presidential voting, and colonial/European oddments; thereafter month-by-month through China leases, Spanish–American war telegrams, Gladstone’s funeral, South Wales strike milestones, Omdurman, Fashoda despatches, and autumn platform crises — to be mined for dated entity pages.

Retrospect of Literature, Science, and Art

Output slightly declined; wars distracted readers yet also spawned Far Eastern and Egyptian campaign books. Biography flourished (Busch’s Bismarck secret pages; Bismarck’s own Man and the Statesman, both translations). Fiction decrease noted with some relief; history, philosophy, science, and criticism held up; poetry abundant in form if not commanding power. Science and art sections follow the Register’s specialist year-notes (Lister’s Vaccination Lords speech marks the medical culture of the moment).

Obituary of Eminent Persons Deceased in 1898

Standard Register necrology — among early entries Sir Robert Meade (Colonial Office Permanent Under-Secretary) and Charles Pelham Villiers (Anti-Corn Law veteran); Gladstone’s death and funeral dominate national memory though treated at length in English History Ch.
