The Party System (1911)

Hilaire Belloc and Cecil Chesterton’s joint pamphlet on the working of the British two-party machine after the 1910 elections: representation inverted, ministries filled by co-option, the Commons’ time-table in ministerial hands, secret funds, and the caucus that selects candidates and programmes.

Source

Belloc, Hilaire, and Cecil Chesterton. The Party System. London: Stephen Swift, 10 John Street, Adelphi, 1911.

A short political book, issued immediately after the December 1910 general election, written to “expose and ridicule as it deserves, to destroy and to supplant the system under which Parliament, the governing institution of this country, has been rendered null.” Belloc sat as Liberal M.P. for South Salford (1906–1910) and had already raised the secrecy of the party funds in the House; Cecil Chesterton (brother of G. K. Chesterton) was a journalist of the New Witness circle. The Cornell / Internet Archive scan used here is the first Swift edition (printer’s colophon: Neill and Co., Edinburgh).

Preface — A Word on the Late Election

The book opens in the dust of the second 1910 election. Both sides have proclaimed that “the Will of the People must prevail,” with only “slight variations as to the direction” of that will. Lloyd George and Winston Churchill appear on one poster as “patriots confronting a haughty aristocracy” (Churchill’s cousin the Duke of Marlborough standing in for the order), on the other as “low-born demagogues hallooing on their ragged and illiterate associates to the plunder of the wealthy.” Conservatives profess fear that John Redmond “should buy up the whole Liberal Front Bench with the sum of £40,000 (or $200,000, which sounds at once larger and more insidiously wicked)”; Liberals sing “One with us is He who leads us, / Asquith, God and right!”

The authors treat the preceding year as a stage-play. In 1909 the Lords threw out Lloyd George’s Budget. Asquith at the Albert Hall pledged he would neither assume nor retain office without “guarantees” that the veto would be limited. He won the January 1910 election, assumed office, retained office, and produced no guarantees. Instead he entered a “conference” with his alleged opponents, six months supposedly spent bridging the “unbridgable gulf” which “one of his humbler salaried followers discovered, in a notable speech, to exist between the views of his uncle on the one hand, and of his first cousin on the other.” Both sides then announced that the Conference had failed, and another election was declared necessary, “though, as matter of fact, there was absolutely nothing to vote about.”

The key, they write, is that “The Conference did not fail. It did exactly what it was intended to do. It saved for a moment the life of the moribund Party System.” The 1906 mandate had been squandered (Chinese Labour, the Licensing Bill, “the collapse and absorption of the Labour Party”); “Down with the Lords!” was raised to rally supporters, then smothered when a handful of Radicals demanded fulfilment. The so-called Opposition’s refusal to attack Asquith’s broken Albert Hall pledge is offered as evidence of collusion: “practically every move in the silly and dangerous game is arranged beforehand by the confederates on the two Front Benches.” Governments abandon causes because they consult “the sense of the Governing Group”; Oppositions tolerate the abandonment because they wish to preserve the system that will, “by the connivance of those rivals,” bring them office in turn.

I — The Representative System

The Idea of Representation. Democracy is defined not as votes or assemblies but as “government by the general will.” “Wherever, under whatever forms, such laws as the mass of the people desire are passed, and such laws as they dislike are rejected, there is democracy. … That is to say, there is no democracy in England to-day.” Pure democracy is the meeting of elders under the village tree; representation is an indirect substitute, and it requires three conditions: free selection of representatives, strict responsibility to constituents, and independence of the Executive. None obtain. The Executive controls the assembly; the “Ministry” settles what is discussed. The force that “turns into an engine of oligarchy what was meant to be an organ of democracy” is named in two words: the Party System. The “Will of the People” dispute of the last two elections is reduced to whether it is “best represented by the Duke of Sussex or by his son-in-law, the Right Honourable James Blagg.” A representative who votes opposite to Bethnal Green is “merely an oligarch.”

What the Public Thinks. Englishmen long believed they had self-government without a republic. A “dim suspicion” has arisen that something alien stands between electors and elected. The public that the machine exploits is still, in the main, honest: a Conservative leader must denounce land taxes his followers sincerely detest, even if “he has no intention of repealing them.” Enthusiasts remain — Conservatives who think the Liberal Government is only held from “spoliation” by the Opposition; Liberals who see Asquith and Churchill as “tribunes of a people rightly struggling to be free.” The old lady at a funeral who, seeing Gladstone, hoped “he hasn’t come to make a disturbance,” is still alive, and so is the Radical who believes Churchill “has become an outcast from his order.” Single-issue men (Tariff Reformers, churchmen, teetotalers, the Irish “to whom nothing matters but the cause of their nationality”) matter at elections. The mass is compared to the Oxford and Cambridge Boat Race: crowds with no connection to either University take sides year after year, “always ‘Oxford’ or ‘Cambridge,’” and “just as they vehemently take sides with a University to which they have never been, so they take sides as vehemently with a party which they do not control.” An uneasy minority notices laws they detest passed by both parties, desired measures never mentioned, the Jameson Raid inquiry stifled by both Front Benches, Balfour’s “hesitations and equivocations” on Tariff Reform, and last year’s “meaningless ‘Conference.‘” They blame Asquith for being “too Whiggish” or Balfour for being “weak,” not seeing that Balfour is “strong — strong in the traditions of party, the complex system of relationships and alliances that cover English politics like a net.”

Past and Present. The system was not always “the organised imposture which it is to-day.” Seventeenth-century conflict was real because men “were willing to fight and kill and be killed.” 1689 was not a democratic triumph: it was made “not only by but for a group of wealthy intriguers with an object in the main financial.” The commentary is enclosure of “more than 6,000,000 acres of common land” after Hanover. Parliament represented a narrow class (landowners, merchants, recruited wealth) but within that class there were real differences; Pitt and Fox “did not speak to each other” and “did not, like Mr Asquith and Mr Balfour, belong to the same set.” Direct bribery of the legislature has ceased, perhaps because of “the growing impotence of the House of Commons” rather than virtue. Industrial wealth and French Revolutionary ideas produced 1832; later Reform Acts enlarged the electorate while power silently passed from the Commons to the Cabinet. Unreal issues, “defined neither by the people nor by the Parliament, but by the politicians themselves,” now give “a semblance of reality to their empty competition.” The House has been “rendered null, and the people impotent and without a voice.”

II — The Governing Group

The Making of Ministries. Ministers are not nominated by the Crown or by Parliament: “Ministers nominate themselves. They form a self-elected body, filling up its vacancies by co-option.” The two Front Benches are “one close oligarchical corporation.” Admission costs “complete surrender of independence.” Recruits fall into Relations (blood and marriage, high or low born — “not to be confused with … the old aristocratic privileges of the Great Houses”), Private Secretaries (sons of the new rich or of party subscribers; Montagu and Herbert Samuel, related to Lord Swaythling), and make-weights (lawyers, an ex-workman, anyone who has given “annoyance” while independent). Asquith in 1892 was “unconnected by any direct tie”; “Now he and his are connected by a dozen such ties.” Office, “unlike the kingdom of heaven, is not taken by storm.”

The kinship list is the book’s most cited passage: Norfolk as Conservative Postmaster-General; Devonshire’s nephew expected in a future Unionist Cabinet; Austen Chamberlain inheriting rank from his father; the Prime Minister’s brother-in-law Tennant on the Treasury bench; Churchill with “a double right”; Selborne, son-in-law of Salisbury, governing South Africa while first cousin Arthur Balfour is Prime Minister and brother Gerald enjoys office and pension; Lord Gladstone; Portsmouth and the Peases; Liberal Whip Sir John Fuller brother-in-law of Hobhouse, both grandchildren by blood or marriage of Conservative Chancellor St Aldwyn (Hicks-Beach). Cross-bench ties: Masterman nephew by marriage of Conservative Colonial Secretary Lyttelton; Asquith and Lyttelton married sisters; Beauchamp brother-in-law of Ampthill; the two Pease Whips cousins; Churchill cousin of Marlborough and his successor as Under Secretary for the Colonies.

The Romeo and Juliet analogy follows: if Lady Capulet were a born Montague, Lady Montague first cousin of old Capulet, Mercutio nephew of a Capulet and brother-in-law of a Montague, Paris related to both houses, Tybalt Romeo’s uncle’s stepson, and the Friar Juliet’s uncle and Romeo’s cousin, “we should probably conclude that the feud between the two houses was being kept up mainly for the dramatic entertainment of the people of Verona.” Tolerance is extended only to those who “play the game,” not to Parnell or Victor Grayson. Concealment of the relationships is “sound moral proof that their motive is a motive of imposture.”

The Placemen. The Placeman is “as prominent … as he was in Walpole’s day,” only more decently veiled. He votes as the Whips dictate, tables blocking motions, or plays at independence without ever touching what is “inconvenient to both Front Benches.” Masterman is the exhibit: independent Liberal critic of Burns and of the Education Bill; 13 March 1908 votes for the Labour Right to Work Bill; May 1908 accepts office at £1,200 (later £1,500); 20 April 1909 votes against the same Bill. “This remarkable example of the ‘peril of change’” produces no astonishment; he is regarded as a man of “exceptionally high ideals.” Barristers abound because prizes are in the gift of the group; Buckmaster moved the amendment that shelved secrecy of the Party Funds. Party funds, unaudited, are “immediately withdrawn from any member who has voted against the directions of the Government,” even the richest members being supported from them. The situation is compared to a King threatening to fine every Commoner a thousand pounds and imperil his seat if the Commons disapprove an addition to the standing army.

The Secret Alliance. “Discipline” is a military word and “fatal to criticism.” Rank and file are held under a rigidity nothing short of civil war could justify, while commanders “concocted their plan of campaign in concert” and treat the war as “a friendly game of mixed chance and skill between themselves and their friends and relations on the other side.” The 1910 Conference was “only one of the hundred conferences” of leaders, secretaries, Chief Whips, “confidential hangers-on,” and “principal official paymasters.” Agreement “is the normal method of governing the country.”

Three cases of halted victory: the Jameson Raid committee, always “on the very verge of some startling revelation,” stopped “like a newspaper serial” — Harcourt, Campbell-Bannerman, and Ellis as eager as any Tory to hush the Colonial Office, “Because they also were Front Benchers”; Chinese Labour, the issue of 1906, not followed by immediate repatriation — “anxious negotiations … to propitiate the South African Jews,” 1,300 Chinese admitted after the change of government, and the new House “not allowed to divide on the question”; the Conference itself, which “openly branded as a farce” the rhetoric of five months before.

A prediction is offered as a test of the thesis. After “ridiculous sham demagogy” and “sham indignation,” Lords’ powers will be altered so that (1) the Lords cannot stop measures concerted between the two Front Benches, and (2) they can stop end-of-Parliament “swing of the pendulum” bills. The reconstituted House will drop the country squires “who are in one sense really representative of England” and keep purchasers of peerages, professional politicians, and bishops; new elements will be “nominees of the machine, or, as the pretty phrase goes, ‘of the Crown.‘” The two Front Benches “are not two but one.” Each wants to win the prizes of office, but that aim is “subordinate to the essential aim of both Front Benches, the maintenance of the Party System.” Speaker and Chairman of Committees, impartial between the two sides because they know the fight is sham, consult with both and support them when they agree. This governing group allots the time of the House and what may be said.

III — The House of Commons as It Is

The Control of the Time-Table. Fossils of forgotten reality include re-election on taking office and the dummy bill given a first reading before the King’s Speech, which once affirmed that the Commons choose their own business. “The symbol is still visible, but, alas! the fact it represented is gone.” Five-sixths or more of time is at the Government’s disposal under Standing Orders; closure ends discussion automatically. Opposition protest is “merely ceremonial”; Balfour “has shown a disposition to drop it.” Private members have one afternoon a week, insecurely held, precedence by ballot; no opposed private member’s Bill has in recent years become law without ministerial facilities. Resolutions can be killed by the Speaker refusing closure or by a shelving amendment.

The authors’ own motion on secrecy of the Party Funds is the instance. After consultation with the Opposition Front Bench, officials approached Liberal barrister Buckmaster to “nullify the debate.” His amendment confined the evil to the Tariff Reform League; Unionists moved a twin amendment on the Free Trade Union; the division became “an ordinary party division.” A Radical threatened to fight Buckmaster at Cambridge; Buckmaster lost his seat and “the two Front Benches were no doubt relieved … they had thus escaped from their bargain.”

The “blocking motion” (notice of a motion never moved prevents anyone else discussing the subject) is illustrated by Rees in the 1906 Parliament, especially the blocking of India. Adjournment on “a matter of urgent public importance” requires forty members; with Front Benches united this is hard to find, “the House of Commons is usually attended by a dozen or twenty members at the most.” Foreign affairs get “a few hours a year.” Question time allows “nothing but a bare answer,” which “may be refused”; it is “the only — though paltry — procedure left” to a member acting in a representative character (a note credits questions by Wedgwood and others with exposing the Conference).

How It Works. The repressive machinery is “most infrequent in its action” — applied only to rare revolts — which is why the public does not see it. The authors imagine a mandate as intense as Chinese Labour in 1906: “overwhelming majority” and “overwhelming intensity” that the Chinese “should go at once,” mixed with desire to chastise “those South African Jews who had compelled our politicians as their servants to exploit for financial ends the popular enthusiasm in the matter of the South African war,” and fear of an experiment in moving labour “in large batches … arranged by capital for the interests of capital alone.” The machinery (control of time, blocking, refusal of divisions, absorption of Labour by compact) prevents any such popular initiative from acting at Westminster. Questions advertise (the Ayrshire Foundry scandal, “in which Campbell-Bannerman was mixed up”) but produce no punishment. “There can be all the sham fight you will upon the sham issues which the bosses have arranged … but there can be no initiative on the part of the electorate.”

A Concrete Example. A supposed Distress Relief Bill of 1910, money needed by a date, conflict over whether relief covers only South Lancashire or also the West Riding. Clause I sets the date; Clause XV confines the area. In Committee the first amendment is verbal (“to every person … there shall be paid” for “every person … shall be entitled to receive”); four pages of Hansard, a division, defeat. Further verbal amendments are withdrawn. An amendment that the law shall last “until Parliament otherwise determines” occupies nearly nine pages, including a page and a half from the Leader of the Opposition; it is lost. “Leave out the words ‘under this Act’” is debated and withdrawn. After a day, seven lines have been “debated” and nothing changed. Before the vital Clause XV amendment is reached, the Front Benches closure the rest. “The people of the West Riding are left without relief.”

Then the substitution: for South Lancashire, destitute people over seventy; for the Bill, the Old Age Pensions Bill of 1908; for the West Riding, destitute people between sixty-five and seventy. “You have an exact and literal account of what took place … in the summer of 1908.” The procedure “had been used simply to cheat the people.”

The Tone of the House. The atmosphere is “stupid and rather a degraded one, much lower than that of the House of Lords,” “death to rhetoric,” an air in which it is “much easier not to bother.” It is “a far smaller factor in the Party System” than visitors suppose. A dozen determined men “could” break the Front Bench arrangements from within. Labour’s absorption was not caused by Tone but by “a definite compact with the Executive by which places, advantage in moving motions, etc. — ultimately, perhaps, Cabinet rank — should be the price of compromise.” The real grip is “secret Party Funds, the control of elections, and the choice of candidates.”

IV — The Secret Funds

The Unmentionable Truth. “The most important fact about English politics is the fact that nobody mentions.” Two “huge war-chests” are “secretly subscribed” and “secretly disbursed.” Light on collection comes from the Honours List; on expenditure, from the division list. They are subscribed “by rich men who want some advantage, financial or social, from the Government,” and spent “in paying the expenses of members of Parliament — in other words, in corrupting the legislature.” Organisers proclaim “perennial penury” and issue “bogus appeals to ‘working men’”; there will be no lack of funds “so long as … the supply of peerages and baronetcies is unchecked.” Expenditure is Walpole’s Secret Service, refined: instead of buying votes after election, the gang ensures “no one shall be elected … who is not prepared so to vote,” “eliminating the chance of an incorruptible member.” The member answers to the caucus that pays him. “Not one man in thirty knows that there are such things as Party Funds; not one man in a hundred has the faintest idea of how they are raised and spent.” Politicians “love darkness rather than light — for a reason mentioned in Scripture.”

The Sale of Powers. “There is a market for peerages in Downing Street, as he knows that there is a market for cabbages in Covent Garden.” Libel law and inability to subpoena party accounts prevent naming prices. A peerage “conveys not only dignity but legislative power. A Peer is a Senator.” Among ten creations in twenty years, a few (Peel, Kitchener, Curzon, Morley, Fisher) fit the senatorial idea.

The 1910 Liberal Birthday Honours, issued while the Government denounced “the Peers,” created seven peers: Causton (Southwark), Foster (Ilkeston), Kearley (Devonport), Pearson (Cowdray), Holland (Rotherham), Freeman-Thomas (Willingdon), Furness. Ilkeston, a distinguished medical man and President of the National Liberal Federation, is said to have been elevated mainly because he gave up a safe seat for Colonel Seely, rejected by his own constituents. Causton, unpaid Paymaster, rejected at Southwark in January 1910, received “a consolation prize” that was “a right to make and unmake laws.” Furness, unseated on petition for agents’ errors, “left the court without a stain” and “promptly received a coronet.” Holland: “enormously rich cotton-owner,” no reason “except his great wealth.” Kearley: importer, chairman of the Port of London Authority. Freeman-Thomas: grandson of Hampden, son-in-law of Brassey, Liberal League. Pearson: contracting company to which “Mr Lloyd-George, junior, son of the Chancellor of the Exchequer, has recently been articled. He is a very rich man, and there is an end of it.”

Conservatives are no cleaner; some families pay both chests. The Guests, Conservative territorial power in Dorset, received Wimborne in 1880; crossed in 1904; the Hon. Ivor Guest did “social” work for Liberalism and in 1909, “just when the politicians were loudest in their denunciation of the Peers,” became Lord Ashby St Ledger. A Conservative Government made Alfred Harmsworth Lord Northcliffe; a Liberal Government gave a brother (Harold) a baronetcy — “what the Conservative Party managers have planted, the Liberal Party managers are only too ready to water.”

The Sale of Policies. Worse than the sale of honours is “a much more insidious traffic in policies.” Cecil Rhodes’s letters to Schnadhorst offered “sums running into tens of thousands” on condition Egypt remain British and the Cape-to-Cairo railway be viewed favourably. Money was paid; Gladstone, publicly for evacuation, did not evacuate. “Gladstone, though nominally leader, was at the mercy of Schnadhorst, and Schnadhorst was at the mercy of anyone who would give him money.”

In 1903 Dr Rutherfoord Harris, contesting Dulwich, “announced publicly that he had sent £10,000 to the Conservative Party Funds.” Shortly afterwards a Conservative Government sanctioned Chinese labour on the Rand. The authors do not suppose £10,000 alone bought the decision; other mine-owners were “equally generous and more discreet.” Liberals, winning 1906 on the issue, still refused a division and negotiated so that Chinese stayed “until they had done their work in reducing the wages of the Kaffirs.”

Drink is the standing case. The public never hears municipalisation or free trade in liquor; the only choice is Balfour’s Licensing Bill (“endowment of brewing and distilling firms out of public funds”) versus Asquith’s (“irritating restrictions … leading logically to ultimate prohibition”). Conservatives need brewers of tied houses; Liberals need cocoa manufacturers, grocers who sell drink retail (the fewer and worse the public-houses, the more they sell), and rich faddists who would prohibit. The people choose “between the brewer and the cocoa-manufacturer. Not unnaturally, they usually prefer the brewer.” Liberal politicians themselves “have quite an adequate taste in alcohol.”

V — The Control of Elections

The Party Caucus. The Central Office is “not representative of the people” nor even of active party members. National Liberal Federation and National Union of Conservative Associations pass resolutions that have “the smallest effect on practical politics until … ratified by the Central Office.” The Office is the hinge between the Front Bench group and local organisations, themselves “largely dominated by the local rich men” and “too democratic to be trusted … with the reality of political power.” Its head, nominated by the Governing Group, “holds the purse-strings” of the secret sums and makes arrangements with subscribers “of which the Leader is conventionally supposed to know nothing, though he obediently carries them out.”

The Selection of Candidates. Free local choice does not happen. Either the richest local man pays the local organisation and is confirmed by the machine, or an outsider approaches Central Office: a rich man “put[s] down a subscription” and the seat’s safety “vary in security with the amount”; a poor man fights hopeless seats as a “useful gladiator,” preferably via a private secretaryship, especially if a lawyer. “The Fortunatus Purse of the Party Funds is placed unreservedly at his disposal” if he sacrifices “freedom (and honour).” A footnote: a rich but stupid man may be sold an apparently safe seat after the two machines have “winked at a third independent candidature.” Local committees insist on “loyalty to the party,” which “nullifies all others.” Independents lack money; there is no second ballot; electors fear to “waste” votes. Only three types normally enter: local rich men, outside rich men who have “suborned” the centre, and poor men willing to be “obedient and submissive servants of the caucus.”

An Election. Salaried officials decide who stands where: purchasers first; university speakers; heredity (“a Cecil, a Howard, a Churchill, or a Rothschild”); poor advocates sent first to seats “safe” for the other colour. The test is willingness to defend not only the existing brief but “any future decision.” Locally there is no Westminster collusion: “prominent Liberals” really differ in type from “prominent Conservatives.” The official organisation is a few dozen men — “never a hundred,” often twenty or thirty — who “elect” big-wigs, then send one to London to fetch “someone on the list.” In ninety-nine cases they accept the nominee. The candidate may “nurse” the seat but must stop in time or it is bribery (“Six months is perhaps the maximum,” depending on the judge). Campaign points come from professionals; pledges made under popular excitement are “expected to [be] break[en] … always in the spirit, and even, if necessary, in the letter.” Nomination requires cash “equivalent to the full year’s income of a well-paid skilled artisan”; avowed expenses average £1,000 and “come in reality … to nearly double.” The last nights are a “rally,” “ritual” more than persuasion. Canvassers theoretically unpaid extract pledges (many electors pledge both sides); without the “costly effort” of fetching voters, turnout “would be negligible.” The system requires the two sham sides to be “fairly equal” nationally. Winning by 10 per cent of the electorate is “enormous”; by 2 per cent is not even “close.” The winner goes to Westminster “to vote for anything whatsoever that the machine may give him orders to vote for.”

The Selection of Programmes. Two programmes are “drawn up by the politicians, usually after consultation with each other.” Framing the question “is to go a long way towards framing the answer.” Free Trade in the 1840s was forced on the legislature by a popular demand; Tariff Reform began when “a Cabinet Minister … declared for Protection” and professional politicians “who had never in their lives before doubted the validity of Free Trade” converted overnight. The two authors “decidedly disagree” on Free Trade versus Protection. Drink and Education Bills are framed for sectarian and trade subscribers, not for parents who “would as soon think of sending their children to be educated in Nigeria as at a public elementary school.” In November 1910 the Front Benches agreed the Osborne Judgment should not be an electoral issue and that payment of members should “side-track the demand of the workers”; “The Labour Party of course gave way.” The Unemployed are not allowed to vote on the Right to Work Bill; they might be offered “Mrs Webb’s policy of imprisoning working men in compounds.” “Non-controversial measures” are defined as “all violently unpopular proposals for the oppression of the poor, which happen in no way to affect the professional politicians” (Children’s Bill, Prevention of Crimes Bill). Licensing clauses of the Budget, “certainly unpopular,” will be continued by Conservatives “when by mutual arrangement their turn of office comes.” Legislation has passed to “that Standing Committee of Professional Politicians which is called in the House of Commons ‘The Two Front Benches.‘”

VI — The Defence

The Excuses. Beneficiaries say states are organic; Britain has had unbroken institutional continuity; under the Party System England was recently the wealthiest and almost the strongest nation; “Leave well alone” unless a concrete alternative exists. Abstractly: every question has a yes and a no; an unchecked Executive is worse; a deliberative body cannot govern; two salaried teams arguing Protection versus Free Trade produce “open discussion.” The system supplies an alternative government without violence (contrast France’s religious schism). Minor pleas: a real English cleavage (Chapel/Church, Liberal/Conservative); higher poll percentages than abroad; and the claim — “least rational and the most ignorant” but “with politicians the most powerful” — that the system has made “this country everything that the citizens of any country can possibly desire.”

The Real Support. The last argument rests on “ignorance and vanity.” Britain no longer has the offensive strength, economic position, or social condition it claims; continental peoples regard the English poor with “pity and horror.” One cannot argue from “the excellence of conditions in England to-day” because those conditions “are not good, but bad.” The old Whig/Tory philosophical division was real into the last quarter of the nineteenth century — “Doctor Johnson did hate a Whig” — and an elderly man may still smell it; now “not the memory, not the savour of a real distinction remains.” Tory squires and suburban Liberals are not represented by the parties they vote for. Between wage-earners for Jones and for Smith there is “no line, not even the vaguest dotted line.” The system cannot supply an alternative government for a vital change, because it “depends upon the very conception that there cannot be any vital or considerable change.” Hanky and Panky frequent the same drawing-rooms. Real movers (Catholics, Socialists, Irish) are met with “violence of vituperation.” Debate occurs only on subjects the two teams have “decided to have debated.” Gladstone’s Home Rule and Chamberlain’s Protection were launched against the rules, then “turned, by a process of digestion, … into unreal issues.” Irishmen discuss thirty years of statutes “with a complete impartiality” as to Westminster labels. “Not one great national issue since the repeal of the Corn Laws” has had genuine party debate; real disagreement produces “stalemate,” because a permanent victory “would be the death of the system by which both sets of politicians obtain their bread-and-butter.” Continuity is a strong argument for wigs, the Lords’ jockey-cap at the Royal Assent, Inns of Court, Oxford colleges; it is not an argument for a machine that has “lost both the externals that bound it to the life of the State, and the internal vitality which gave it a real meaning.”

The Grave Perils. The later pages of the chapter treat the system as not a quaint relic but a decaying institution that still “affects the greatest interests of the nation.” Because it is no longer a real contest of ideals, it cannot register or resolve the conflicts that actually press on the country — poverty at home, “foreign insult” abroad. The professionals live by “bamboozlement of the public” with the complicity of “nobodies content to write M.P. after their name,” lawyers, and business men whose rewards rise “in proportion to their indifference to their representative duties.”

VII — Can It Be Mended?

Scholarship and history would say an institution in this condition is “past praying for” (Rome and the Empire). Yet “Nothing is appearing that can take the place of Parliament.” Most statutes are the work of permanent officials; some clauses are politicians’ (a note ascribes Budget land clauses to Grey and licensing clauses to the Chancellor). The Commons, “fallen into a universally recognised decay, is still our only instrument for making laws.”

Franchise extension would only drill twenty thousand where ten thousand are drilled now. Abolishing plural voting would be met by a new cry to rebalance the teams. Forbidding canvassing would cut the poll (most men vote after “several days of heavy badgering”) but not change the actors. Payment of official election expenses leaves the independent disadvantaged against the “Fortunatus” fund; payment of members would not stop larger future bribes (£800 to £15,000 legal posts). Auditing party funds would spawn a second secret fund; proof of secret bribery is hard when “all parties to the crime are concerned in defending it.”

A short, indissoluble Parliament (four years at most) would be more to the purpose: a vote of censure would not dissolve the House, so members could vote freely. Even an “adumbration of independent voting” forced Campbell-Bannerman to give way on the Trades Disputes Bill. F. W. Jowett’s penny Clarion pamphlet What is the Use of Parliament? proposes replacing collective Cabinet responsibility with departmental committees of the House, the Minister as chairman, no resignation or dissolution on defeat. Without a fixed-term Parliament the Minister would still threaten crisis. In a House still packed, committees would be packed; Grayson asked for a Social Reform committee and was put on the Irish Linen Marks Bill.

Primaries would be valuable but hard to graft. Balfour’s Referendum, “obviously insincere,” would let the bosses submit questions “in which [the people] take no interest” or bury a subscriber’s fad. Only Referendum plus Initiative would matter, and that requires an alert people.

Machinery fails unless the democracy refuses party hacks. “The first need is exposure.” Naming a particular job risks bankruptcy and prison; general exposure, even in “few and ill-capitalised papers,” spreads because truth, once suggested, “tends to turn that suggestion into a conviction.” Tell a provincial that “English Prime Ministers sell peerages”; he disbelieves; then he reads of a “howling nonentity” ennobled. Ridicule is a second weapon: present the hypocrite as buffoon. Example: a young Front Bencher told poorer voters the gulf was “unbridgable”; he held his place by marrying a lady whose uncle had made thousands in one half of the team and whose first cousin had made more in the other.

The second line is pledges inside the machine: not vague promises, but a pledge to vote against whatever Government unless scheduled measures pass the Commons by a date. Had members in 1906 been pledged to vote against the Government unless the first Chinese left by 1 March 1906 and the last by 31 December, “your politician would have been caught.” Weakness: “the mass of men have so despaired of the House of Commons” that they will not organise. If they will not, “the law-making institution of England … is done with.” The body “will jerk, perhaps — it will not revive.” No successor organ is forming. Until one appears, the country faces “one of those evil spaces, subject to foreign insult and domestic misfortune,” when nations “lose grip over their own destinies.”

Notes

A Note on Co-option. Compared with Germany (the Emperor chooses responsible heads in public view), France (ministries must satisfy a fluctuating Chamber, with no automatic dissolution and no standing compact of two Front Benches), and the United States (a President, though elected on a party ticket, personally chooses heads excluded from congressional party rigidity; Washington, Jefferson, Lincoln, Cleveland, Roosevelt are named), Britain vests recruitment in “a dozen or so” who co-opt continuously. Parliament has “no actual check”; Crown influence, if any, is secret and rare. Past success of similar machinery depended on three factors now gone: a real aristocracy selecting within a known area; a Crown with determining influence; effective parliamentary control. Today “it is impossible to conceive of any appointment … however monstrous or absurd, which would lead to remonstrance from the drilled voting machine.”

A Note on Collusion. Collusion is not the absence of all conflict. Even a real party would preserve the rules of debate as golfers preserve the rules of golf, or capitalists the legal order of capitalism. The present line is drawn far from that ideal. What the opponents preserve is “the conditions under which they and their clique shall retain the opportunities for large salaries.” They prevent division on the sale of honours, connections between antagonists, procedure, private members’ time, salaries, public committees. “No one important policy for now fifteen years has been allowed … to form a clear division … with the exception of … Women’s Suffrage” — and even that reaches law only if the Front Benches permit; they lean toward a well-to-do franchise, not adult women’s suffrage. Administration, especially where “great financiers” and “some cosmopolitan banker in Egypt or Ceylon” are concerned, is concerted. Notes are tossed across the table; Whips confer openly to prevent majority decisions; unofficial amendments to the Address are excluded by open agreement; new salaried posts are announced to the opposite Front Bench, “his own side” ignored. A proposed rise from £40 to £100 a week is debated as a question of “dignity”; payment of members is opposed by a man who has “already lifted thousands out of the taxes” lest it create “the professional politician”; the referendum is praised as cheap, attacked as un-English, renamed “poll,” then dropped “for ever.”

A Note on the Press. The daily press is “more devoted to [the Party System] than is any part of the State, except the professional politicians themselves.” Liberal advocacy: Star, Daily News, Manchester Guardian, Westminster Gazette. The other team (in January 1911 it “has no fixed name”): Times, Daily Telegraph, Birmingham Daily Post, Pall Mall Gazette. Writers on a “Conservative” paper may be Socialists; a “Free Trade” paper may be owned by a Protectionist. The politicians’ only regular lever is honours to proprietors, now “rather a matter of routine.” Direct money, jobs, and contracts hardly touch the press. The motive is fossilised public expectation of the farce — “magnetic personality” of Campbell-Bannerman, “arresting eloquence” of a Churchill or Cecil. The attitude is “not wicked, it is merely stupid.” Reformers should attack the parliamentary game, “that ‘machine for grinding wind,’” not expect to purify newspapers first. An independent journal giving both sides of a hops duty and a wheat duty, denouncing sale of legislative power on either team, or combining an urban land increment tax with contempt for “Form Four,” would find “many, many thousands, especially in London.” The Daily Mail already admits some adverse discussion. “The Press has long ceased to affect opinion” on party; it still affects it by selection of foreign news. Purchase of papers to influence the party game has “proved financially so disastrous that we shall probably not see it repeated.”
