The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1897

The Annual Register: A Review of Public Events at Home and Abroad for the Year 1897

The Annual Register 1897

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Opening of the Session to Easter

Seldom did a year open more tamely than the sixtieth of Queen Victoria’s reign. At home the financial relations of Great Britain and Ireland dominated platform debate: Lord Farrer’s memorandum and speeches by Sir Edward Clarke and others revived the claim that Ireland was over-taxed relative to “taxable capacity,” while Mr. Balfour at Manchester insisted that any settlement must await parallel inquiry into Scotland and warned that universal accident insurance would ultimately come out of workmen’s pockets. Lord Penrhyn’s long dispute with the Bangor quarrymen was the year’s most watched labour quarrel. Abroad, the European concert still failed to extract Armenian guarantees from the Sultan; Mr. Rhodes, returning from the Matabele campaign to “face the music” of the South African Committee, made a progress at the Cape that deepened Afrikander hostility; and an Anglo-American general arbitration treaty stood drafted and on the eve of signature.

Parliament opened on the Address with amendments ranging over Merchandise Marks, Irish Nationalist grievances, and foreign policy. The South African Committee was reappointed to pursue the Jameson Raid inquiry. A Military Works Bill authorised loans for fortifying western harbours (Berehaven, Lough Swilly, Falmouth, Scilly), barracks, camps, and London defensive positions. The Voluntary Schools Bill — aid from imperial funds for denominational schools — was brought in and read a second time amid Nonconformist and Opposition protest that it relieved subscribers and strengthened clerical schools without popular control. A Women’s Franchise Bill also secured a second reading, a parliamentary landmark though it did not become law. Egypt and the cost of the Dongola / Soudan expedition, Welsh disestablishment talk, Army and Navy Estimates, and bye-election results filled the early session. By February–March the Cretan rising forced repeated Commons debates; Lord Salisbury defended concert diplomacy while critics mocked its impotence. Financial-relations debates continued in both Houses; the National Liberal Federation met; and the South African Committee’s early sittings ran in parallel with the Cretan crisis through the approach to Easter.

Chapter II — Easter to Whitsuntide

The Easter recess let parties take stock. The Church side had won Voluntary Schools relief, but Necessitous Board Schools were also to receive an Exchequer grant (Sir John Gorst sought about £110,000), so Opposition critics denounced the Government for parsimony while Conservatives feared the board-school rivalry would never end. Abroad, Indian famine deepened: the Indian Government had discouraged a national relief fund until the Lord Mayor of London’s appeal raised some £500,000; plague in Bombay paralysed business and inflamed feeling against sanitary measures. Eastern Europe remained unsettled.

Sir Michael Hicks-Beach’s Budget (28 April) reported a prosperous year — 2¾% Consols around 114, revenue above forecast (£103,950,000 Exchequer receipts against a £100,480,000 estimate) — yet offered little tax remission despite the surplus, given immense expenditure and large debt reduction. Necessitous Board Schools legislation and the Compensation for Accidents / Employers’ Liability measure advanced; Lord Salisbury spoke at the Albert Hall; Sunday closing in Ireland and other Irish bills occupied time; Mr. Balfour made further announcements on Irish and committee business. Public interest in the Greco-Turkish struggle, the South African Committee, and the U.S. Senate’s emasculation of the arbitration treaty remained curiously muted. The Opposition, still scarred by Rosebery’s retirement and short of a unifying programme, struggled to rally the country while Unionist critics taxed Salisbury with unused majority.

Chapter III — Diamond Jubilee, Compensation Act, and the Raid Committee

Long before Whitsuntide the public mind fixed on the Queen’s sixty-year jubilee. London preparations almost suspended ordinary business along the processional route; the Queen agreed to traverse the City and return by the south bank so poorer districts shared the fête. Politics and even foreign rumours were sidelined, yet the Register notes a quiet shift in European alignments: after the Kaiser’s Kruger telegram, British feeling cooled toward Germany and warmed toward the peace-minded Franco-Russian pairing. Colonial Premiers gathered (Liverpool hospitality and later Colonial Office conference); coal-owners revolted against the Compensation Bill; Parliament presented jubilee addresses; the Queen issued a letter to her people.

The Workmen’s Compensation for Accidents Bill — pressed by Liberal Unionists and disliked by many Conservative employers and coal-owners — passed the Commons with enough cross-bench support that rebel Conservatives relied on the Lords; it became the session’s major social statute. Irish landlords protested land policy; Unionist strain and peer unease surfaced. The South African Committee climaxed over “missing telegrams” linking Rhodes, Flora Shaw of The Times, and Cape planning for the raid; majority and minority reports and a bitter Commons debate followed, clearing Chamberlain of complicity in many eyes while leaving Rhodes under heavy censure in Afrikander and Radical opinion. Colonial Premiers discussed imperial preference and defence; Church and State questions, foreign affairs, Irish landlord grievances, the Compensation Bill in the Lords, and the Indian Budget closed the session.

Chapter IV — Recess: Frontier War, Engineers’ Strike, Platform Speeches

The recess brought no Liberal revival: at Brightside (Mundella’s old seat) the Liberal majority fell from 1,277 to 183 (6 August). Home politics were dull while Constantinople peace talks dragged and the Indian north-west frontier war grew serious. The engineering strike and lock-out, begun in London over an eight-hours day, spread nationwide; a telegraphists’ / Post Office strike collapsed; Penrhyn quarrymen returned (21 August) after eleven idle months on terms long available. Jubilee echoes lingered — Sir Wilfrid Laurier among the last Premiers to leave, receiving the Cobden Club gold medal for Canadian free-trade direction.

Colonial Office conferences with visiting Premiers continued. On the frontier, some 40,000 troops under Sir William Lockhart (succeeding Sir George White) scoured Tirah and the routes to the Khyber in guerrilla fighting; Dargai (21 October) stood out, the Gordon Highlanders storming a height under deadly fire. Bye-elections (East Denbighshire, Barnsley), Morley and Asquith in Scotland, Sir Edward Grey on foreign affairs, and charges of “breach of faith” against the Indian Government (countervailing sugar / tariff controversies) filled autumn platforms. Rosebery at Stirling, Chamberlain at Glasgow, Balfour at Norwich, and the Chancellor on Army weakness marked the speaking season; the “breach of faith” cry faded; Salisbury at the Albert Hall and Harcourt in Scotland closed the year amid National Liberal Federation programme-making and renewed Army debate. Trade held up despite engineering orders lost abroad; Consols stayed high.

Chapter V — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. Sentiment for saying “British” rather than “English” for joint achievements animated advanced Home Rule talk more than any solid grievance. West-of-Scotland butchers tried to stop co-operative stores undercutting meat prices; the engineering strike reached the Clyde and retarded shipbuilding without crippling the large firms. Overall trade and outlook were described as not gloomy.

Ireland. Financial-relations agitation and Nationalist tactics at Westminster remained the political keynotes already traced in Chapters I–III; landlord protest against land legislation and Sunday-closing and other Irish bills marked the session. Distress in parts of Ireland featured in autumn platform controversy alongside the larger imperial and labour questions of Chapter IV.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. The year’s domestic note was l’esprit nouveau — Moderate Republicans reconciling with the Catholic Church — while the Russian alliance raised awkward questions of friendliness toward Germany and cooler relations with Britain. Senatorial elections disappointed Radicals (Léon Bourgeois’s campaign failed outside a few departments). General elections forced clearer party lines. Franco-British friction over Egypt and the Nile (Hicks-Beach’s Commons language; Hanotaux’s measured reply) simmered without rupture.

Italy. Coalition politics under di Rudinì, Socialist congresses, tax revision, and student unrest occupied the kingdom; Italy remained in the Triple Alliance while managing Mediterranean interests that Britain still valued.

Chapter II — Germany and Austria-Hungary

Germany. An imperial rescript against duelling opened the year. Prussian state finance under Dr. Miquel showed large ordinary surpluses (railways contributing heavily); Miquel defended using exceptional surpluses for extraordinary productive outlays rather than treating them as permanent revenue. Naval and world-policy undercurrents and press exchanges with Britain after the Kruger telegram kept Anglo-German feeling raw.

Austria-Hungary. Dualist politics and Balkan vigilance continued; compact with Russia helped localise the Greco-Turkish war and prevent a general Balkan conflagration.

Chapter III — Russia, Turkey, and Eastern Europe

Russia. A financially and politically strong year: gold coinage forced into circulation beside paper; ukase rules for gold backing of the note issue; land-transfer statistics; a census. Far Eastern preoccupations made St. Petersburg prefer quiet in the Near East.

Turkey, Crete, Greece. The Cretan question absorbed the Powers. Armenian prisoners were released early in the year as attention shifted to Crete; February saw the island given over to pillage and communal fighting. Greek popular pressure produced intervention and then open Greco-Turkish war; Thessaly fighting went against Greece. The European concert blockaded and occupied Cretan waters/points to contain the conflict; Russia and Austria worked to keep other Balkan states out. Peace terms and evacuations dragged into the autumn Constantinople negotiations noted in English History.

Chapter IV — Minor States of Europe

Belgium debated personal versus substituted military service (King Leopold publicly patient but insistent; War Minister crises). The Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, and Norway receive their usual political and legislative notices — army and franchise questions, Iberian instability, and Scandinavian defence and constitutional issues among them.

Chapter V — Asia

India. Famine, plague, sedition fears, earthquake, and frontier war defined the year. The Lord Mayor’s fund and government relief operations; Bombay plague measures; the Tirah campaign under Lockhart (see English History Ch. IV). Burma was raised to a Lieutenant-Governorship (Sir Frederick Fryer); jubilee remissions freed thousands of prisoners; a Mandalay Fort gate attack by armed Burmans was crushed.

Afghanistan, China, Hong Kong, Korea, Japan, Siam. The Ameer Abdurrahman’s realm was mostly quiet; Durand-line frictions eased in places. Far Eastern notices cover continuing scramble politics, treaty-port and leasehold consolidation, and Siamese adjustments with France — standard Register survey chapters for the year.

Chapter VI — Africa

Egypt and the Soudan. French and Russian protests against Britain’s £500,000 advance for the Dongola campaign had no practical effect. Hicks-Beach’s statements on British rights and the mixed courts angered the French press; the Khedive’s Turkish leanings irritated British officials, while his ministers and an enlarged Legislative Council worked more smoothly with Cromer’s administration. Revenue for 1896 was the largest yet, with a large paper surplus largely absorbed by international debt machinery. Military consolidation after Dongola prepared further Nile advances.

South Africa. Sir Alfred Milner succeeded Lord Rosmead as Governor of the Cape and High Commissioner (appointed February; arrived early May to warm welcome). The Afrikander Bond congress congratulated the Queen but vowed Rhodes should never sit in Parliament again. Raid-committee politics and Transvaal tension remained the unresolved background.

West and elsewhere. The Benin disaster (3 January): an unarmed British mission massacred on the Oba’s orders, precipitating the punitive Benin Expedition. East and Central African notices continue the partition narrative.

Chapter VII — America

United States. The Olney–Pauncefote arbitration treaty, signed under Cleveland and recommended as a new epoch, was reported from the Senate Foreign Relations Committee (30 January) with amendments requiring two-thirds Senate consent for every submission, substituting “jurists of repute” for Supreme Court judges, and altering umpire selection — provisions that emasculated the treaty; the Senate adopted the committee amendments (23 March). McKinley’s inauguration opened a Protectionist chapter (Dingley tariff politics).

Canada. Laurier’s Liberal ministry carried a preferential tariff (Fielding, St. George’s Day) favoring reciprocal partners — practically a gesture toward Britain once Belgian/German treaty obstacles were faced. Laurier knighted at the Jubilee; Klondike / Yukon gold rush fever from August. Friction with U.S. labour and canal/cruiser incidents noted.

Newfoundland, Mexico, Central America, West Indies, South America. Routine colonial and Latin American surveys.

Chapter VIII — Australasia

No single political earthquake; colonies progressed industrially and fiscally, ministries surviving want-of-confidence motions. Premiers’ Jubilee visit strengthened rather than weakened them at home. Federation again headed Australian politics as the lasting constitutional project.

Chronicle of Events in 1897

New Year honours raised Sir Joseph Lister to the peerage — noted as the first medical peer, for antiseptic surgery. Rhodes’s Cape ovation contrasted with politicians’ aloofness. The Benin massacre (3 January) opened the colonial year. Continental and imperial items run month by month through plague and famine news, Cretan and Greek war telegrams, Jubilee day (procession and illuminations), Tirah fighting, engineering-strike milestones, and the autumn platform season — a daybook companion to Parts I–II.

Retrospect of Literature, Science, and Art

Publication languished early under foreign crises and Jubilee distractions; the last quarter recovered. The retrospect surveys history, biography, fiction, poetry, and the literature of research; science and art sections follow the Register’s usual specialist notes for the year (Lister’s honour in the Chronicle marks the medical culture of the moment).
