Naval Operations, Vol. II (1921)

Corbett’s official history of Royal Navy operations from late 1914 into summer 1915 — Home Waters redistribution after the Falklands, the Yorkshire raid and Dogger Bank, the defence of Egypt, and the Dardanelles from naval attack to the Gallipoli landings.

Source

Corbett, Sir Julian S. History of the Great War: Naval Operations, Vol. II. London: Longmans, Green and Co., 1921.

Covers late 1914–1915 naval operations, dominated by the Dardanelles from initial naval attack through the Gallipoli landings; also Dogger Bank, Egypt, Home Fleets redistribution, the German war-zone declaration, and the Lusitania.

Chapter I — Redistribution of the Home Fleets and Operations on the Belgian Coast

With the destruction of Admiral von Spee’s squadron at the Falklands the war in its naval aspects entered a new phase. The enemy had no organised squadrons outside his home waters, and command of the sea, judged by traditional standards, was practically done. Yet the Dresden’s escape and the unsolved mystery of the Karlsruhe still occupied cruisers; and in Home waters mining and submarines of increasing range made free movement precarious. Over 150 trawlers and drifters formed the nucleus of the Auxiliary Patrol; the Grand Fleet itself now depended on fishing craft for free movement near its bases. Still the key fact remained that the Grand Fleet held its controlling position. The High Seas Fleet, under the Emperor’s absolute control, was confined to submarines and destroyers for offensive action, with only occasional sorties of heavy cruisers; outside the Baltic Britain enjoyed a command as complete as in the old blockades of Brest. Until Fisher’s new programme of monitors and fast heavy ships for inshore work was forward, the enemy might use the long winter nights for some sudden blow to diminish the blockade or deter further troop movements to France.

Chapter II — The Raid on the Yorkshire Coast, December 16

By December 10 news of the Falklands had revealed the weakening of the Grand Fleet. Now if ever was the enemy’s moment to strike, and by the 14th reports of an impending attack were so far confirmed that the fleet was put in motion. It appeared to be a cruiser raid, with or without transports; without undue prejudice to concentration it was possible to ensure that the enemy should have no time to land any serious force, or if a mere naval raid, that subject to the chances of the sea he should never get back. The 5th Battle Squadron stood at shortest notice; Tyrwhitt with his Harwich flotillas was directed to get touch and shadow; Beatty was to take the four remaining battle cruisers from Cromarty, joined by Goodenough’s light cruisers from Scapa, with one battle squadron in support. Ingenohl, fretting at the inaction imposed since Heligoland, took the Kaiser’s limited permission for big-cruiser sorties as a hint and devised a coast raid — stretching his instructions by supporting Hipper with three battle squadrons. Early on December 15 Hipper left the Jade with Seydlitz, Moltke, Von der Tann, the newly completed Derfflinger and Blücher; the battle squadrons left Cuxhaven in the afternoon and after dark took supporting station while Hipper proceeded on his errand toward the Yorkshire coast.

Chapter III — Home Waters, December 18 to January 18 — Further Preventive Measures

Although the expected activity had taken the form of a purely naval and not a combined operation, intelligence made it impossible to relax measures against an attempt to land troops. The southern areas had been weakened by the loss of Bulwark. On December 20 Beatty was ordered from Cromarty to join the 3rd Battle Squadron and 3rd Cruiser Squadron at Rosyth; Princess Royal, delayed hunting the ghost of the Karlsruhe, was already homeward bound. The new 10th Cruiser Squadron under de Chair in Alsatian completely relieved the Grand Fleet of the north-about trade-route watch. Christmas Day tested the new stations with an air raid on the Cuxhaven Zeppelin sheds — nine seaplanes from Engadine, Riviera and Empress, supported by Harwich forces, the whole Grand Fleet concentrated in the middle of the North Sea in hope of provoking a fleet action. Dense frost fog frustrated location, but in Schillig roads seven battleships and three battle cruisers weighed in such hurry that Von der Tann fouled another cruiser and both were severely damaged. Of the returning airmen three were rescued by Nasmith in E 11 off Norderney under the approach of a Zeppelin — founding the reputation he completed in the Sea of Marmara.

Chapter IV — The Mediterranean During November and December, 1914

The Mediterranean began to regain the prominence it had never long been denied in any great war. Since Carden’s short bombardment of the Dardanelles entrance when Turkey was driven into the war, it had attracted little attention. In the Adriatic the French found effective action against Austrian submarines impossible; their fleet based at Malta maintained a strict blockade of the outlet and escorted convoys to Egypt. In the Black Sea Ebergard mined the Bosporus, bombarded Zungaldak and Trebizond, and on November 18 fought a brief action with Goeben and Breslau in which the Russian flagship Evstafi was hit four times before German speed took them out of reach. Spy reports of severe damage to Goeben tempted the Admiralty to hand the Dardanelles blockade to the French, but the Minister of Marine insisted one battle cruiser remain; Indefatigable, Dublin and three British submarines stayed under Carden, other British ships being replaced by French forces under Guépratte. On December 13 Lieutenant Holbrook in B 11, fitted with mine-guards, cleared under five rows of mines, torpedoed the cruiser Messudieh at 800 yards, and after nine hours under water returned to base — Holbrook receiving the V.C. Carden asked for a latest-class submarine to go up to the Golden Horn; with the Scarborough raid just past, none could be spared. On the Egyptian side Peirse re-hoisted his flag in Swiftsure at Suez on December 1 in view of the expected invasion of Egypt.

Chapter V — The Dogger Bank Action, January 24

Since Christmas, when the test of the new North Sea distribution had terminated in the collision between Monarch and Conqueror, reports of restlessness in the German naval ports had never ceased, and the comparative impunity of the Yorkshire coast raid was expected to tempt a repeat. With Formidable lost and Conqueror disabled, Jellicoe could count on only eighteen Dreadnoughts against seventeen German; Beatty had five battle cruisers against four, Queen Mary docking, Invincible at Gibraltar and Inflexible in the Mediterranean. Distribution: Dreadnought battle squadrons at Scapa; battle cruisers with the 3rd Battle and Cruiser Squadrons at Rosyth; Tyrwhitt’s Harwich force with Arethusa, Aurora, Fearless and Undaunted, and Keyes’s oversea submarines. On January 15 Seydlitz and Derfflinger were reported clear of the Jade; E 10 left Yarmouth for Heligoland and never returned. That night two Zeppelins bombed King’s Lynn, Yarmouth and Sheringham.

On the morning of the 23rd intelligence set the whole North Sea machinery in motion. Great activity was reported in the Bight; another coastal raid seemed probable. The Germans afterwards gave out that their object was to clear the Dogger Bank of fishing vessels and patrols which they persuaded themselves were there for observation. Hipper’s force was Seydlitz (flag), Moltke, Derfflinger and Blücher with light cruisers Stralsund, Rostock, Kolberg and Graudenz and a strong destroyer flotilla; Von der Tann was still unrecovered from Christmas Day collision damage. Admiralty inference was a reconnaissance in force to the Dogger Bank. Orders went out shortly after noon. That night Keyes with Firedrake, Lurcher and four submarines felt out of Harwich for Heligoland and the Ems; Tyrwhitt made for a Dogger Bank rendezvous; Beatty came down south-east with five battle cruisers, Goodenough’s light cruisers turning south from north of the Bank; Bradford took the 3rd Battle Squadron thirty miles north of the Dogger; Jellicoe, clear of Scapa by 9 p.m., made for a rendezvous midway between Aberdeen and Jutland, intending to pass through at 9.30 a.m. on the 24th. Ship for ship the British were more powerful — Lion, Tiger and Princess Royal with 13.5-inch against Derfflinger’s 12-inch and Seydlitz and Moltke’s 11-inch, New Zealand and Indomitable carrying 12-inch against Blücher’s 8.2-inch — and the intercepting dispositions were designed to ensure Hipper should not get back unscathed.

Chapter VI — Abandonment of the Offensive in Belgium and Final Decision to Attack the Dardanelles

The effect of the Dogger Bank action was to demonstrate the efficacy of the new distribution and materially to reduce the chances of a military raid upon the coasts. It was on January 28, four days after the action, that the final decision to attack the Dardanelles was taken. Although Carden had been informed that his plan was accepted, the matter was still far from settled. Events had been tending toward direct action in the Balkans by way of Salonica; Greece’s reply of January 27 required Bulgarian co-operation or, failing that, Roumania’s, and if Bulgarian neutrality could not be assured, two Entente army corps in addition. Under those conditions nothing could be done from Salonica for the present, and opinion solidified in favour of the Dardanelles as the quickest way of fixing Bulgaria’s attitude. Preparatory work had already ships on their way; Russia was particularly eager after the heavy defeat of the Turks at Ardahan and the annihilation of their Vth Corps at Sarakamuish. Serious opposition came from the best British naval opinion, with Fisher at its head: a purely naval attack on a strongly fortified base was a departure from established doctrine, and once only had such an attempt succeeded, when Rooke seized Gibraltar by coup de main. The First Lord’s relative-strength memorandum of January 27 argued that superiority was sufficient for a subsidiary operation without prejudicing North Sea command. When the War Council met on the 28th the Board was not in a position to give a firm opinion; all that was clear was the political necessity for action and the decisive advantages success would bring.

Chapter VII — The Attack on Egypt, January 27 to February 11

A noteworthy feature of the deliberations of January 28 was the little concern expressed regarding Egypt, yet it was known that the long-expected attempt on the most sensitive point in Imperial communications was materialising. Unusual rainfall in Sinai had facilitated the Turkish advance; they were already approaching the canal. Kitchener felt no uneasiness — his hope was that the enemy would come on, for with a strong naval force on the spot it would be in our power to strike at their communications from the sea at Gaza — but no such orders appear to have been sent. The canal defences under Major-General Alexander Wilson were organised in three sections: Suez against the southern Nekhl–Akaba route, Ismailia against the central Maghara route, Kantara against the coast route through Katia from El Arish. Of about a hundred miles of canal, twenty-four were impassable lakes and inundation had further reduced the front to about fifty. Under Peirse were Swiftsure (flag) at Port Said, Ocean at Suez, the French Requin in Lake Timsah, cruisers Minerva and D’Entrecasteaux, sloop Clio, armed merchant cruiser Himalaya and R.I.M.S. Hardinge. About mid-January French seaplanes located 8,000 to 10,000 men at Beersheba; by the 22nd advanced troops showed at Moiya Harab, Bir el Dueidar and Ain Sudr. Ships entered the canal to assigned stations and New Zealand battalions reinforced El Kubri and Ismailia. The Turkish approach, forced prematurely, found the defence ready; naval mobile heavy artillery gave the garrison what it most needed, and the attack was repulsed without the Gaza counter-stroke Kitchener had had in mind.

Chapter VIII — Salonica and the Dardanelles — Modification of Plans

Though the collapse of the canal attack did something to clear the air, the Turks had escaped without the punishment Kitchener had intended, and the need for more resounding success in the Near East was more pressing than ever. Egypt could not yet be considered safe; considerable bodies still occupied frontier posts at Katia, Bir el Abd and Nekhl. A small force investing Tor Harbour was dislodged on February 11 by half a battalion of the 2/7th Gurkha Rifles under Lieutenant-Colonel Haldane from Minerva, joining Dufferin — over 100 prisoners, about 60 killed, our loss one Gurkha killed and one wounded. On the Syrian coast Philomel took over the Alexandretta patrol from Doris; a landing party was compelled to retire with loss, and Bacchante found activity increasing when she took over on the 16th. Far more disturbing was the Russian Front: the Germans continued their offensive against Warsaw; Russia had nearly exhausted munitions, and until the Dardanelles could be opened there was little hope of renewing the offensive. Ferdinand of Bulgaria accepted a German loan; Venizelos’s negotiations at Bucharest for joint Balkan action to save Serbia had broken down. For Kitchener the Dardanelles was essentially diversionary — the view that appealed to the French, who now promised four battleships and a minesweeper flotilla and agreed the whole Allied force should be under the British Admiral. Augagneur declared the plan permitted operations to be broken off at any stage without loss of prestige if insurmountable difficulties were encountered. Ministers were coming to feel that at least in the existing phase the decisive theatre was rather to be looked for in the Balkans than in France. On February 6 Delcassé came over to discuss an alternative objective with a view to saving the Balkan situation.

Chapter IX — The Dardanelles — Opening of the Naval Attack

During all the difficult discussions concerning military assistance Carden had been pushing preparations for a naval attack without interruption except for adverse weather. All January except three days it had blown a gale from the south-west; February brought gales from the north-east. Since January 15 no active operation had been undertaken; that day the French submarine Saphir, against strict orders, attempted to repeat B 11’s exploit, ran aground inside the Straits and was lost. The magnitude of the force required two additional flag officers: Rear-Admiral Rosslyn Wemyss to take charge of the Allied base at Mudros, and Rear-Admiral de Robeck as second-in-command, shifting his flag at Gibraltar to Vengeance on January 22. Commodore Keyes became Chief of Staff. Carden’s flag flew in Inflexible, still bearing Falkland scars, which had arrived January 24 to relieve Indefatigable for her Malta overhaul. Owing to German North Sea activity both battle cruisers were required home — Bulwark and Formidable gone, Agamemnon and Irresistible already sailed, Conqueror still under repair. Carden, learning that Queen Elizabeth’s accident greatly reduced her speed and that Goeben was active again, was allowed to keep Inflexible as the only ship able to bring Goeben to action.

Ships gathered from home and from distant commerce-protection stations freed by the Falklands and the destruction of the Emden: Albion from the Cape, Triumph from China, Vengeance from Cape Verde, Canopus from South America, Ocean from the Persian Gulf, Cornwallis, Majestic and Prince George from home, and nearly all Bethell’s 5th Battle Squadron. The French contributed Suffren, Bouvet, Gaulois and Charlemagne under Guépratte, bringing the Allied fleet to sixteen capital ships. Command presented difficulty under the Convention of August 6, 1914, which gave France general direction in the Mediterranean; the French were willing the Dardanelles operations should be under the British Admiral but wished to take over the Syrian coast as far south as Jaffa, and on those lines the matter was amicably adjusted. On February 19 the bombardment of the entrance forts opened — Inflexible, Cornwallis, Vengeance, Triumph, Suffren, Bouvet, Gaulois and Agamemnon running and spotting against Helles, Orkanie, Kum Kale and Sedd el Bahr through a long day of deliberate fire, the first test of whether long-range naval gunfire could silence modernised works without an observing station ashore.

Chapter X — The Dardanelles — Resumption of the Bombardment

By February 24 the weather had so far improved that Carden saw his way to completing the first day’s work. General Trotman had arrived with the Plymouth and Chatham battalions of Marines for covering fleet demolition parties. On the 25th, though weather moderated enough for renewal of bombardment, no landing was ordered and the Marines remained in transports at Mudros; seaplanes also could not be used. The object was destruction of the outer forts at close range and sweeping as far as the entrance. Four ships working in pairs were to run up to the jaws of the Straits, engaging Helles and Orkanie with secondary armament down to 3,000 yards, aiming “to destroy each individual gun by a direct hit.” First run: de Robeck with Vengeance and Cornwallis; second: Guépratte with Suffren and Charlemagne. In support Agamemnon, Queen Elizabeth, Irresistible and Gaulois would fire deliberately at long range into Forts 1, 8, 4 and 6.

Queen Elizabeth anchored 11,600 yards south-west from Helles lighthouse with Dublin spotting; Carden in Inflexible about 11,500 yards north-west from Helles; Agamemnon between Queen Elizabeth and the shore. At 10.17 Helles opened on Agamemnon; both its 9.4-inch guns were working under skilled control, and within a quarter of an hour it was straddling her. Ordered to weigh, she was hit seven times with armour-piercing shell in ten minutes — three killed, five seriously wounded, a hole six feet above the water-line, hydraulic engine and main derrick damaged — yet nothing that could not be put right. Queen Elizabeth took on Helles and with her sixteenth and seventeenth shots got direct hits, one dismounting a 9.4-inch; between them both guns of the battery were put out of action and the barracks so wrecked that the garrison streamed out down the hill. By noon not one of the forts was firing. De Robeck led in at 12.15; Guépratte’s closer run from 2.10 concentrated on Sedd el Bahr, Orkanie and Kum Kale down to 3,000 yards, the only reply a single shot from Kum Kale. After 3.0 the minesweeping trawlers closed the entrance, Albion and Triumph going in to 2,000 yards — one Orkanie gun firing once more before being silenced. The outer defences were practically reduced; the way into the Straits was opening.

Chapter XI — The Dardanelles — First Attack on the Narrows and the Smyrna Operations

While officers on the spot were beginning to realise the full measure of the enterprise, everywhere else the initial operations were taken to promise success. Venizelos was trying to induce the King of Greece to offer his fleet and one infantry division; Bulgaria began to trim her sails; on March 4 Italy made her first approaches for joining the Allies. Russia, invited on February 28 to co-operate, replied on March 3 that a whole army corps was being prepared for embarkation at Odessa and Batum within a fortnight or three weeks of the forcing of the Dardanelles. When the Dardanelles next came up in the War Council on March 3, discussion was mainly occupied with the plan of campaign after the Straits were forced, a new line of operation by the Danube; the original monitors Severn, Humber and Mersey were ordered to prepare for Danube work. Kitchener’s despatch to Birdwood on March 4 still held that the fleet was to break through alone — expected in the Sea of Marmara by the 20th — with over 60,000 men concentrated at Lemnos by then. Till large-scale military assistance was certain, troops were not to be used in the Gallipoli peninsula at all; the real objective was Constantinople. Birdwood again pointed out little chance of the fleet getting through alone, and even if it did the transports would be exposed to severe loss from concealed guns; his preference for a landing at Sedd el Bahr was inadmissible under the instructions. So for the next fortnight operations were purely naval. On March 5 bombardment of the inner defences began, Queen Elizabeth for the first time filling the leading part. The main defences consisted of five forts closely grouped on either side of the Narrows — Rumili Medjidieh, Hamidieh II and Namazieh on the European side; Hamidieh I (two modern 14-inch, manned the Turks say entirely by Germans) and Chemenlik on the Asiatic. The lack of an observing station ashore declared itself with new distinctness; Queen Elizabeth was to anchor on the west side of the peninsula and fire across the land. Parallel Smyrna operations occupied part of the force while the main fleet worked up toward the decisive test at the Narrows.

Chapter XII — The Dardanelles — Further Development of the Plan

March 10 was the date by which Kitchener expected to decide whether the XXIXth Division was to be employed at the Dardanelles — recognised alternative line of operation or mere diversion. On the Western Front force and ammunition were still insufficient for a break-through; Sir John French’s attempt at Neuve Chapelle on March 10–13, with naval co-operation from Venerable and Excellent off Westende, stormed the village but could make no further progress against German reserves. Russian readiness to promise an army corps gave better hope of far-reaching results: an Allied concentration that might paralyse Turkey, open vital communications with Russia, and keep Bulgaria off Serbia. Accordingly on March 10 Kitchener announced that the XXIXth Division might go, and that the force against Constantinople would amount, if the Russian corps was up to strength, to little less than 130,000 men with nearly 300 guns. Ebergard’s instructions confined the Black Sea fleet to naval demonstration until Carden appeared before the Princes Islands, when the Russians would attack the Bosporus forts. Up to this point the objective of both Anglo-French and Russian troops was Constantinople; there was still no intention of forcing the Dardanelles by combined operation, though Admiral Jackson pointed out that unless the Gallipoli peninsula was occupied the fleet could not keep the Straits open. From the point of view of Imperial policy the decision meant a radical departure: by 1903 the Committee of Imperial Defence had already recorded that exclusion of Russia from the Straits was not a primary naval or military interest, and Germany was already engaged in an insidious scheme to induce Russia to make separate peace. Orders for the attack on the Narrows were completed; the Smyrna operations were brought to an end as the fleet prepared for the decisive naval effort of March 18.

Chapter XIII — The Dardanelles — Failure of the Attack on the Narrows and the Change of Plan

By the operation orders completed before Carden had to resign the command, the fleet had been reorganised in three divisions: first, the four modern ships under de Robeck with flag in Queen Elizabeth; second, eight older British battleships under Captain Hayes-Sadler of Ocean; third, the four French battleships and two British under Guépratte. The General Idea was to silence the defences of the Narrows and of the minefields simultaneously — not expecting destruction of forts at 8,000 yards, but hoping to dominate them sufficiently to prevent interference with sweeping. Line A, the First Division in line-abreast 14,000 yards from the Narrows opposite Eren Keui, would engage the principal forts at long range; Line B for closer action was accorded first to Guépratte, advancing to the limit of the swept area, 8,000 yards from Rumili, while Triumph and Prince George covered against barrage guns. After four hours the French would be relieved by Hayes-Sadler’s sub-divisions. Sweeping was hoped to begin two hours after bombardment commenced — trawlers to clear a 900-yard passage past Kephez into Sari Sighlar Bay — while the Royal Naval Division demonstrated a landing on the western side of the peninsula. Observation was left to airmen from Ark Royal; armed picket boats attended each battleship against floating mines.

On the evening of the 17th, weather promising well, operation orders issued; dawn of the 18th came with warm southerly breeze and cloudless sky. British minesweepers reported all clear during the night between White Cliff and Kephez Bay to within 8,000 yards of the Narrows. At 8.15 the signal to carry on flew from the flagship. Shortly before 10.0 the fleet approached the entrance; at 10.30 Agamemnon led the First Division in with destroyers sweeping ahead and Prince George and Triumph on either beam. Within half an hour they came under howitzer fire from the back of Kum Kale; by 11.30 they had reached firing positions and opened. The first half-hour gave good promise: Queen Elizabeth on Hamidieh I, Agamemnon on Rumili, Lord Nelson on Namazieh, Inflexible on Hamidieh II — little or no reply from the forts, though barrage fire increased. The well-thought-out plan was about to meet the undetected minefield in Eren Keui Bay that would sink Bouvet, Irresistible and Ocean, cripple Inflexible and Gaulois, and force the abandonment of the purely naval attack — the change of plan that committed the army to the peninsula.

Chapter XIV — Progress of the Oversea Expeditions and Commerce Protection

To appreciate justly all that was involved in the decision to change the Dardanelles plan, the conflicting anxieties must be seen in the light of the general naval situation. Although the Falklands had broken the back of the enemy’s cruiser effort against commerce, the trouble had not been finally eradicated without prolonged operations and a considerable proportion of cruiser force. Nowhere except in the Pacific had the combined expeditions against German oversea possessions attained their end. In Africa three remained in hand — the Cameroons, German South-West and German East — all causing a drain upon the navy. In the Cameroons, since the capture of Baré and the northern railway railhead, no substantial advantage had been obtained; vast distances made concerted general offensive extremely difficult. Captain Fuller’s slender blockading force — Challenger, Dwarf, and about a dozen Nigerian Marine vessels — bore heavy strain preventing supplies from Spanish Fernando Po; when Admiralty wished to replace Challenger by Astraea from the Cape, Dobell protested, and the force was eventually increased for a regular blockade. In German South-West Africa operations had opened as early as September 1914 with occupation of Luderitz Bay, but development had been arrested first by the rebellion and secondly by the need of keeping King-Hall’s squadron concentrated till von Spee was dealt with.

Chapter XV — Home Waters in February and March, 1915 — The British Blockade and the German War Zone

February 18, the day before the Dardanelles attack opened, was the day on which the German new departure in commerce warfare was to begin. The decision had not been reached without misgiving. Tirpitz regarded the declaration of a war zone round the British Isles as premature: Germany had not enough submarines to blockade the whole British coasts effectively, and unless a blockade was effective under the Declaration of Paris it was not lawful. His view was that the submarine blockade should for the present be confined to the Thames. Von Pohl as Chief of the Admiralty Staff took the opposite view and had the ear of the highest quarters. The Dogger Bank result had completed in the Kaiser’s mind the impression Heligoland had begun; he was resolved more than ever to keep in being the fleet he had created, and his impatience to see submarines at work increased with his stiffening determination to deny the fleet all offensive action. Hitherto, apart from sowing mines in the open sea, the Germans had fairly well observed accepted limitations; offences of destruction without warning had been sporadic. Now they threw off all disguise. On February 4 the German Admiralty declared all waters surrounding Great Britain and Ireland, including the whole English Channel, a war zone: from February 18 every enemy merchant vessel found within it would be destroyed without its being always possible to avoid danger to crews and passengers; neutral ships would also be exposed to danger. Up to February 18 losses already included eleven British merchant vessels sunk by submarines (four without warning), fifteen British merchant vessels and eighteen fishing craft sunk by mines, and thirty-eight neutral merchant vessels sunk by mines — the opening of a campaign that would transform the character of the war at sea.

Chapter XVI — The Dardanelles — Organisation of the Combined Attack

In all wars the spring tends to exhibit new developments, and never more conspicuously than in the spring of 1915. The Maritime Powers had secured, according to old standards, complete control of the sea, but unless they could use it to strike in a new direction the prospect of decision was as remote for them as for their enemy. Secondary theatres had before been decisive when war was sufficiently maritime — the Peninsula against Napoleon, the Crimea against Russia, Havana ending the Seven Years’ War — by concentration of naval and military force where the enemy was weakest. Since the army had been sent out merely to make good what the navy had won, it had to concentrate in Egypt for reorganisation. The fleet also had much to do: civilian-manned trawlers had proved unfit for minesweeping under fire, so the whole flotilla had to be reorganised with naval ratings; beach-gear and a landing flotilla had to be improvised, transport anchorages thoroughly protected. Postponement was regarded on the spot as inevitable; Keith and Abercrombie against Alexandria in 1801, nearly six weeks in Marmarice Bay, offered precedent. Air reconnaissances confirmed that damage to the Narrows forts was slight and proved new works covering the vital Kephez minefields. When the time came Peirse was to send Goliath, Euryalus and anything else he could spare. Indications were not lacking that the Germans were forcing a demonstration against the canal; by end of March Intelligence indicated some 14,000 at El Arish with 10,000 more from Ramleh, 12,000 with fifty guns at El Sirr, 4,000 with twenty guns at Nekhl. Montcalm and Philomel took stations at Ismailia and the Bitter Lakes; the Royal Naval Division sent four half battalions to Kantara. With nearly the whole Mediterranean Expeditionary Force in Egypt it was no time for the enemy to deliver attack; April 3 passed without further sign, and organisation of the combined attack went forward toward the landings of the 25th.

Chapter XVII — The Dardanelles — Landing of the Expeditionary Force

It was not till midday on April 23 — a full five weeks since the attack on the Narrows — that the weather began to give promise of the fine spell essential for the combined operation. De Robeck gave the word for preliminary movements about sundown; five aeroplanes from Tenedos bombarded Maidos, forcing two battalions of the 27th Regiment out onto the Kilia plain nearer Anzac Cove. Twelve lighters for the southern beaches went away towed by three transports carrying advanced battalions for W and V Beaches and Morto Bay, followed by Wemyss in Euryalus with Implacable and Cornwallis, the River Clyde, and tugs towing floating piers. Next day the rest moved out: main rendezvous for the XXIXth Division off the mouth of the Straits, for Anzac off Cape Kephalo in Imbros, for the Royal Naval Division west of Xeros Island. British transports alone numbered over sixty, trawlers over thirty, ships of war well over ninety — at least 200 vessels moving in confined waters without mutual interference.

On the 24th Hamilton transferred headquarters from Arcadian to Queen Elizabeth. The Royal Naval Division left Trebuki at dawn by an indirect course outside Lemnos, Imbros and Samothraki; Sapphire and Amethyst with the King’s Own Scottish Borderers and Plymouth Marines rendezvoused off Tekke Burnu for Y Beach; Thursby took out leading Anzac covering battalions for a rendezvous five miles west of Gaba Tepe. At nightfall the glass fell and wind got up, but shortly after midnight the sea was perfectly smooth. In brilliant moonlight Wemyss transferred the three leading battalions of the main landing to Euryalus, Implacable and Cornwallis without a hitch. By 4.30 a.m. on the 25th Captain Grant with the Royal Naval Division was off Xeros Island; Dartmouth and Doris bombarded the Bulair lines while trawlers swept Bakla Bay as though a landing were intended. The feint’s effect on Liman von Sanders was immediate: he moved the VIIth Division from Gallipoli toward Bulair, ordered Essad Pasha down to Maidos, and for over twenty-four hours had his post of command near the central fort of the Bulair lines — leaving the Gaba Tepe–Helles landings to develop against the IXth Division’s coast cordon and the incomplete XIXth Division in reserve at Boghali, while Australian and New Zealand troops went ashore at Anzac Cove and the XXIXth Division at the Helles beaches under the guns of the covering squadrons.

Chapter XVIII — The Dardanelles — The Initial Advance, April 26–28

During the night the rain ceased and dawn came again in perfect serenity. Up at Anzac the troops had dug themselves in, guns had been landed to the crest, and as day broke an imposing array of battleships and cruisers took station — Thursby’s squadron reinforced by Queen Elizabeth, the bulk of the Bulair force hurrying south. With first light the ships opened on assigned targets still difficult to locate; the rain of shrapnel continued on position and beach. For two or three hours the situation remained critical, but fire from the sea, which prisoners afterwards described as appalling, made organised assault almost impossible. By 9.0 a.m. Queen Elizabeth had knocked out the most annoying battery and the Turkish attack died away exhausted. Hamilton could feel the position no longer in acute danger and that he had not counted on the Australians and New Zealanders in vain.

At the southern beaches progress had been slight. Under cover of darkness the devoted naval party in River Clyde had re-established connection with the beach and the troops still in her had stolen ashore, getting touch with the Worcestershire on their left. Hunter-Weston reported the XXIXth Division not strong enough without further assistance to take Hill 141 dominating Sedd el Bahr, and asked for the French brigade. Another anxiety intervened: a message from Colonel Matthews to the ships off Y Beach said he was holding the ridge till the wounded had been embarked — the disquieting inference that the beach so cleverly seized and so vital to the hoped-for envelopment was being abandoned. Matthews’s force, unmolested during the greater part of the first day, had been more heavily pressed than any other in the night by attacks from Krithia working down the gully. By dawn men were exhausted and ammunition short; as Goliath with Dublin, Talbot, Sapphire and Amethyst closed in, the Turks broke off. A serious misunderstanding followed: the right believed an order to re-embark had been given; successive mistaken retirements were rallied by counter-attack, but when the flagship arrived a little after 9.30 evacuation was clearly in hand. The abandonment of Y Beach meant the failure of envelopment; the initial advance was already hardening into the trench warfare that would dominate the peninsula.

Chapter XIX — The Dardanelles — The First Reinforcements and the Battle of Krithia

When news reached home of how great the first effort had been, how much it had cost, and how far short it had fallen of the coup de main planned against Achi Baba, it became clear the enterprise could not be carried on without greater military force. Nothing could be moved from France or Home — Egypt was the only source. The 29th Indian Brigade had left for Lemnos on April 26; in garrison was the East Lancashire Territorial Division, and Peyton’s Mounted Division arrived by end of month. Turks showed renewed activity on the frontier and intrigue with the Senussi, but it was generally admitted that the best defence for Egypt was attack on Constantinople. On April 27 Kitchener told Hamilton that Maxwell would send anything asked for; embarkation of the East Lancashire division and over 3,000 Anzac reinforcements commenced on May 1. D’Amade informed Hamilton that the French were holding another division in reserve. A more serious objection arose when on the evening of April 28 the Bikanir Camel Corps engaged Turkish patrols near Hod el Bada. Departure of Gallipoli troops left only three reliable Indian brigades for the canal; the only ship of force remaining was St. Louis, and Peirse asked for Euryalus and Bacchante back at once. De Robeck, with Albion out of action after being badly hit supporting the French off Kereves Dere on the 28th, could only detach Goliath and submitted that to withdraw another ship would dangerously imperil the situation — not merely by loss of the ship but of officers and men who could not be spared from communication and beach work. The strain on fleet personnel from the multitudinous requirements of an army thrown ashore from the sea was already almost beyond endurance. Thanks to French readiness, Dartige du Fournet was ordered to send Jeanne d’Arc, D’Estrees and D’Entrecasteaux to Port Said; de Robeck could recall Goliath. On April 30 the first French cruisers arrived; the attack, if ever intended, had not developed. Reinforcements reached the peninsula; the attacks on Krithia and Achi Baba, supported by naval bombardments, met Turkish defence that made swift victory impossible and confirmed that the campaign had become a siege.

Chapter XX — Progress of the Submarine Campaign and Loss of the Lusitania

The breakdown of the second attempt to force the Turkish defences in Gallipoli constitutes a definite landmark in the progress of the war. The hope of rapidly completing the investment of the Central Powers and opening direct communications with Russia was at an end. Further progress by combined operations was out of the question without many more troops. By dramatic coincidence there had occurred simultaneously one of the outstanding tragedies of the submarine war against commerce which for the time overshadowed the really more vital check at the Dardanelles, and made suddenly patent how imperfect was the control of the sea already won. Future operations could only take the form of a regular siege like Port Arthur.

As yet neither at home nor at the Dardanelles had the possibilities of the submarine as a means of disputing command made themselves felt except as annoying irritation. After mid-April reports of German submarines making for the Mediterranean increased; on May 6 torpedo-boat 92 of the Gibraltar Patrol fell in with one steering east of Alboran — almost certainly U 21 under Hersing, first to attempt the Mediterranean, which reached Cattaro on May 13 with fuel all but spent. Ten of the smaller UB class were being sent by rail to Pola in sections. Austrian submarines had become so active in the Adriatic that they forced relaxation of the French blockade: on the night of April 26–27 Gambetta, flag of Admiral Sénés, was torpedoed off the Italian coast — every officer perished at his post, only 136 saved of 714. At home during April eleven British merchant ships measuring 22,000 tons were lost, four without warning with thirty-eight lives, besides fishing craft and the Belgian relief ship Harpalyce. The campaign that had opened with the February war-zone declaration was about to produce, in the sinking of the Lusitania on May 7, the outstanding tragedy that overshadowed the Dardanelles check, shook American opinion, and made patent how far short of true command the old standards of sea control now fell — while the resignations of Fisher and Churchill with the formation of a Coalition Government closed the first phase of the naval war of 1915.
