Contents

Chapter XIV — Educational and Philanthropic Movements

The early spring of 1899 and the introduction of Mr. W.S. Robson’s Education of Children Bill, which proposed to raise the minimum age for children leaving school and regulate the employment of children in factories. McCarthy examines the debate: Sir John Gorst’s independent support, the opposition from agricultural interests (who defended field work for children as healthy), the Berlin Conference of 1890 on factory labour, and the eventual passage of the bill. The chapter also covers the growing movement for old-age pensions — the parliamentary committee under Lord Rothschild, the scheme for contributory pensions, the debates on the Poor Law, and the public agitation led by Canon Blackley and Joseph Chamberlain.

Chapter XV — New Ireland

The transformation of Irish Nationalist politics after Parnell. McCarthy traces the reunion of the Irish Parliamentary Party, the emergence of John Redmond as leader of the reunited Nationalists, the replacement of the old landlord-dominated local government by a system of elected county councils (the Local Government Act for Ireland, 1898), the Wyndham Land Act and the beginnings of land purchase, the revival of the Irish language movement, the work of the Gaelic League under Douglas Hyde, and the improvement in economic conditions. The chapter presents an optimistic picture of “New Ireland” emerging from agrarian conflict and political division.

Chapter XVI — Another Death-Roll

The death-roll of 1899. Leading entries include the Duke of Northumberland (Algernon George Percy, 6th Duke), the artist George Frederic Watts, the scientist Edward Frankland, the African explorer Henry Morton Stanley, and the novelist George Meredith — among others. McCarthy uses the chapter to reflect on the changing of generations and the cultural loss represented by these passings. Each figure receives a substantial biographical notice.

Chapter XVII — The Coming South African War

The drift toward war with the Boer Republics. McCarthy describes the re-election of Paul Kruger as President of the Transvaal in May 1898, the refusal of the Volksraad to grant the franchise to Uitlanders, the despatch of Sir Alfred Milner as High Commissioner, the Bloemfontein Conference between Milner and Kruger (May-June 1899), the failure of negotiations, the ultimatum from the South African Republic, and the British decision to send troops. The chapter examines the competing narratives: the British claim to protect the rights of British subjects in the Transvaal versus the Boer fear of being swamped by foreigners and losing their independence.

Chapter XVIII — The War in South Africa

The narrative of the Boer War. McCarthy describes the outbreak in October 1899, the early Boer successes — the sieges of Ladysmith, Kimberley, and Mafeking, the “Black Week” of December 1899 (the battles of Stormberg, Magersfontein, and Colenso) — and the British recovery under Lord Roberts and Lord Kitchener. The relief of Kimberley, the battle of Paardeberg and the surrender of Cronjé, the relief of Ladysmith, the advance on Pretoria, the annexation of the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, and the subsequent phase of guerilla warfare. The chapter also covers the role of colonial contingents, the volunteer movement in Britain, and the financial cost of the war.

Chapter XIX — The General Election of 1900

The “Khaki Election” of 1900. McCarthy analyses the political calculation behind Lord Salisbury’s decision to dissolve Parliament while the war was still in progress. He discusses the campaign: the Liberal split between “Liberal Imperialists” (Rosebery, Asquith, Grey) and the “pro-Boer” faction (Lloyd George, Morley, Labouchere), the patriotic wave that favoured the Government, the Conservative-Unionist victory, the defeat of prominent Radicals, and the results — a reduced but still commanding majority for the coalition. The chapter also covers the new Parliament and the parliamentary debates on the conduct of the war.

Chapter XX — Yet Another Death-Roll

The death-roll of 1900. The chapter opens with Dr. James Martineau, the eminent Unitarian minister (brother of Harriet Martineau). Other figures include Sir John Ruskin (the art critic and social thinker — McCarthy notes his long retirement at Brantwood), Professor Max Müller (the philologist), Sir Henry Irving (the actor), and the Duke of Cambridge (the Commander-in-Chief). McCarthy uses these biographies to reflect on the intellectual and artistic ferment of the Victorian age now passing.

Chapter XXI — Last Years of the Reign

An extraordinary outbreak of regicidal passion in Europe — the assassinations of the Empress of Austria (1898), King Humbert of Italy (1900), and President McKinley of the United States (1901). McCarthy discusses the anarchist movement and its doctrines. He then turns to domestic affairs: the political uncertainty in Britain as the war dragged on, the last speeches and public appearances of the Queen, and the gradual decline in her health. The chapter also covers the social and economic condition of the country — the continuing growth of cities, the expansion of the railway system, the progress of technical education, and the condition of the agricultural labourer.

Chapter XXII — The Death of the Queen

The death of Queen Victoria at Osborne House on 22 January 1901. McCarthy opens with the significance of the new century and then describes the Queen’s final illness, the gathering of the family (including the German Emperor William II at her bedside), the moment of death, and the immediate aftermath. The chapter covers the lying-in-state, the funeral procession through London, and the burial at Frogmore beside Prince Albert. McCarthy reflects on the length and importance of the reign — the longest in English history to that date — and the transformation of Britain through the sixty-three years of Victoria’s rule.

Chapter XXIII — A Retrospect — Political

A comprehensive review of the political history of Victoria’s reign. McCarthy surveys the changes in the constitution and the working of Parliament: the extension of the franchise in 1867 and 1884, the Ballot Act, the reform of the Civil Service, the gradual democratisation of the House of Commons, the decline of the House of Lords, the growth of the party system, and the evolution of cabinet government. He examines the great political movements — Chartism, Free Trade, the Reform Bills, Home Rule — and the leading statesmen from Melbourne to Salisbury. The chapter concludes with McCarthy’s assessment of the political temper of the age: a movement from aristocratic to democratic government, accomplished by gradual reform rather than revolution.

Chapter XXIV — A Retrospect — The Arts of Peace

The material and intellectual progress of the Victorian age. McCarthy surveys the revolution in transport (steamboats and railways), the electric telegraph and submarine cables, the internal combustion engine and the beginnings of the automobile, the great exhibitions (1851 and later), the extension of education, the growth of the press, the development of municipal services (gas, water, sanitation), the advances in medicine and public health, and the improvement in the material conditions of life for all classes. The chapter also covers literature (Tennyson, Browning, Dickens, Thackeray, George Eliot, Carlyle, Ruskin, Arnold), science (Darwin, Huxley, Kelvin, Faraday, Lister, Pasteur), painting (the Pre-Raphaelites, Landseer, Millais, Watts, Leighton), architecture, and music. McCarthy concludes the entire work with a tribute to the peaceful achievements of the reign and the sense of progress that characterised the age.
