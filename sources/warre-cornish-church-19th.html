The English Church in the Nineteenth Century, Part II

Francis Warre Cornish, M.A. (Vice-Provost of Eton College, late Fellow of King’s College, Cambridge) wrote this volume as Part II of the nineteenth-century Church history within the multi-volume series A History of the English Church, edited by Dean Stephens and William Hunt. Where Part I (presumably covering the earlier decades of the century) left off, Warre Cornish takes the story from the aftermath of the Oxford Movement through the great ecclesiastical controversies of the Victorian era down to the end of the nineteenth century.

The work is thematic in organisation but broadly chronological in progression. It focuses on the great questions that agitated the Church in this period: ritual and doctrine, the revival of Convocation, the Church’s engagement with social problems, the legal battles over ritualism, the revision of the Bible, the education question, the disestablishment of the Irish Church, the development of Church organisation at home and abroad, and the missionary expansion of the Anglican Communion.

Sources

The work includes a table of contents, errata list, appendices (dates, accession tables, Indian/Colonial/Missionary bishoprics), and an index.

Contents

Chapter I — Ritual and Doctrine

The theory and practice of ritual as the expression of doctrine. The ceremonial of the Roman Church and the Protestant reaction against sacramentalism. The revival of high eucharistic and sacramental doctrine by the Oxford Movement of 1833. The reintroduction of doctrines and practices long absent from Anglican teaching: the Real Presence, eucharistic vestments, elevation and reservation of the sacrament, extreme unction. The careers of Bishop Blomfield and the early controversies over ritual in London. The work of W. J. Bennett at St. Barnabas’, Pimlico, and St. Paul’s, Knightsbridge. The opposition of the Evangelical and Low Church parties. The development of the ritualist movement through the mid-century.

Chapter II — Revival of Convocation

The revival of the Convocation of the Province of Canterbury after its long suspension. The campaign led by Henry Hoare and others for the restoration of the Church’s ancient synodical body. The opposition of evangelical and erastian opinion. The re-establishment of Convocation in 1852 under Archbishop Sumner and the subsequent development of its role. The establishment of the Houses of Laymen and the growth of Church self-government. The decennial Lambeth Conferences.

Chapter III — Episcopate and Cathedrals

The expansion and reorganisation of the episcopate. The creation of new bishoprics: Manchester, Ripon, St. Albans, Truro, Liverpool, Newcastle, Southwell, Wakefield, Bristol and Gloucester separated. The reform of cathedral establishments. The work of cathedral chapters in education, music, and the promotion of worship. The condition of the parochial clergy and the growth of clerical incomes.

Chapter IV — Sisterhoods: Auricular Confession

The revival of religious communities for women in the Church of England. The foundation of Anglican sisterhoods: Park Village, Devonport, Clewer, Wantage, All Saints’, East Grinstead, St. Mary the Virgin, and others. The work of sisterhoods in nursing, education, and social service. The opposition to and slow acceptance of the religious life. The revival of auricular confession as a regular practice in Anglican devotion. The controversies surrounding the confessional.

Chapter V — The Church and Social Problems

The engagement of the Church with the social questions of the age. The work of the Christian Socialists: F. D. Maurice, Charles Kingsley, J. M. Ludlow, and Thomas Hughes. The foundation of the Working Men’s College. The London and other diocesan societies for moral and social improvement. Church work among the poor in the cities. Temperance and other reform movements.

Chapter VI — Progress of Ritualism

The spread of ritualist practices through the parishes of England. The introduction of eucharistic vestments, candles, incense, and the mixed chalice. The growth of a more elaborate ceremonial in worship. The opposition of bishops and the varied response of the episcopate. Organisations for the defence of ritualist clergy. The impact of ritualism on the ordinary life of the Church.

Chapter VII — Ritualism and the Law Courts (1853-1882)

The legal battles over ritual. The founding of the Church Association to prosecute ritualist clergy. The series of ecclesiastical trials and appeals. The Privy Council judgments in the cases of Westerton v. Liddell, the Denison case, the Hebbert v. Purchas case, and the Clifton and St. Albans cases. The Public Worship Regulation Act (1874) and Archbishop Tait’s effort to suppress ritualism. The imprisonment of five clergy for contempt of the new court. The growing strain between the legal decisions and the conscience of the High Church party.

Chapter VIII — Ritual Commission and Revision of Bible (1867-1870)

The Royal Commission on Ritual appointed in 1867 under Archbishop Longley. Its composition and first report on vestments. More detailed examination of ornaments, the eastward position, the mixed chalice, incense, and altar lights. The second report. The question of the revision of the Authorised Version of the Bible. The appointment of the New Testament Revision Company (1870) and the Old Testament Revision Company. The principles and procedures of the revision.

Chapter IX — Ecclesiastical Legislation (1867-1874)

The legislative work of Parliament affecting the Church. The debates and measures in the years following the ritual controversies. The passage of the Public Worship Regulation Bill in 1874 through the efforts of Archbishop Tait. Lord Shaftesbury’s role. The effects of the Act on the ritualist movement and the imprisonment of clergy under its provisions. The reaction of public opinion.

Chapter X — Liberals and Evangelicals

The two great non-Tractarian parties. The Liberal or Broad Church party: the influence of Coleridge, Thomas Arnold, and the Oriel Noetics. The work of Arthur Penrhyn Stanley, Benjamin Jowett, F. D. Maurice. The Evangelical party: its leading figures after the first generation, its influence on philanthropy and missions, its theological positions and its relation to the rising ritualism. The gradual realignment of party affiliations.

Chapter XI — Essays and Reviews (1860-1866)

The publication of the controversial volume Essays and Reviews (1860). The contributors: Jowett, Mark Pattison, Baden Powell, H. B. Wilson, C. W. Goodwin, Rowland Williams, Frederick Temple. The topics: the interpretation of Scripture, the relation of science and religion, the development of doctrine. The storm of criticism and the ecclesiastical trials of Wilson and Williams. The Privy Council judgment (1864). The publication of Aids to Faith as a reply. The eventual liberalising effect on English theology.

Chapter XII — The Colenso Controversy (1861-1866)

The controversy surrounding John William Colenso, Bishop of Natal. His critical work on the Pentateuch, its denial of Mosaic authorship and strict inspiration. The condemnation of his teaching by the Bishops of the Church of England. His trial and deposition by Bishop Gray of Cape Town. The appeals to the Privy Council and the conflicting jurisdictions of the colonial Church and the Crown. The legal and constitutional crisis over the bishopric of Natal. The long-lasting consequences for the colonial episcopate.

Chapter XIII — National Education (1843-1876)

The Church’s role in the development of national education. The establishment of the Committee of the Privy Council on Education (1839) and the controversy over state inspection of schools. The Factory Education Bill of 1843 and the Nonconformist reaction. The growth of the National Society’s schools. The work of Sir James Kay-Shuttleworth and the training colleges. The rivalry between Church schools and British and Foreign School Society schools. The Education Act of 1870 under W. E. Forster and the creation of school boards. The Church’s response and the continued expansion of voluntary schools.

Chapter XIV — Disestablishment of the Irish Church (1868-1869)

The political campaign for the disestablishment of the Church of Ireland. Gladstone’s conversion to the cause. The general election of 1868. The passage of the Irish Church Act (1869), disestablishing and partially disendowing the Church of Ireland as a state church from January 1, 1871. The commutation of tithes and the compensation arrangements. The reorganisation of the disestablished Church of Ireland under its synodical government.

Chapter XV — Church Organisation

The internal reorganisation and development of the Church of England. The development of diocesan organisation, ruridecanal chapters, and diocesan conferences. The growth of the Church Congress and its role in public debate. The establishment of the Church House and the central committees. The Church’s financial organisation: Queen Anne’s Bounty, the Ecclesiastical Commissioners, and the augmentation of poor livings. The revision of the lectionary and other liturgical reforms.

Chapter XVI — Primacy of Archbishop Benson

The primacy of Edward White Benson as Archbishop of Canterbury (1883–1896). His character and ecclesiastical policy. The Lincoln Judgment (1892) which reconsidered the legality of ritual practices and partially overruled earlier decisions. The end of ritual prosecutions. The growth of the religious life and the development of the Anglican Communion under Benson’s leadership. His death in 1896 at Hawarden.

Chapter XVII — Missions — Part I

The missionary expansion of the Church of England overseas. The Society for the Propagation of the Gospel (S.P.G.) and the Church Missionary Society (C.M.S.) in the later nineteenth century. The growth of native churches. The development of the missionary episcopate. The expansion of missions in Africa, India, the Middle East, and the Far East. Missionary work in the colonies and dependencies.

Chapter XVIII — Missions — Part II

The continuation of the missionary story, with particular attention to specialised and regional work. The Universities’ Mission to Central Africa. The missionary work of Anglican sisterhoods overseas. The development of the native clergy. The growth of the Anglican Communion as a worldwide fellowship of Churches. The expansion of colonial and missionary bishoprics. The rise of the autonomous Churches in the dominions.
