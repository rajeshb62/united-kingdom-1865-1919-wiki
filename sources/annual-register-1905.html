The Annual Register 1905

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Port Arthur, Defence, and the Fiscal Campaign

In England, as throughout the civilised world, the thoughts of men in the first week of 1905 were occupied less by any domestic event than by the fall of Port Arthur. Profound satisfaction at this signal triumph of Britain’s Japanese allies was mixed with respectful sympathy for the gallant defenders of the fortress. Lord Selborne, the first Cabinet Minister to speak after the news (Wolverhampton and Handsworth, 5 January), tried to turn admiration for patriotic devotion into support for Mr Chamberlain’s injunction to “think Imperially,” arguing that the future would lie with nations and Empires counted by hundreds of millions and that Britons must work for better Imperial organisation—commercial, naval, and military—at the approaching Colonial Conference.

Anxiety about defence was uneven. Lord Selborne’s naval administration was generally held vigorous and enlightened; his letter of 3 January confirming that Rosyth on the Firth of Forth would be continuously developed as a naval base (subject to financial as well as naval considerations) was accepted with little demur. Military administration was another matter. Lord Roberts’s January article in the Nineteenth Century and After, “The Army as It Was and as It Is,” insisted that national safety required a large scientifically trained reserve of officers, larger forces or reserves, and better-educated privates—conditions that could not be improvised and which, in his view, had not yet been provided. Rumours of dissensions in the Army Council cast doubt on whether the War Secretary could carry through coherent reform.

There was satisfaction at a special Army Order of 5 January giving general effect to the Esher Committee’s reorganisation of military commands: London became an independent district; the rest of the United Kingdom was divided into seven commands (Aldershot, Southern at Tidworth, Eastern with headquarters in London, Irish at Dublin, Scottish at Edinburgh, Northern at York, and Welsh and Midland at Chester), each under a general officer commanding-in-chief with a major-general or brigadier in charge of administration authorised to correspond with the War Office on routine matters.

The terrible grievance of Mr Adolf Beck, wrongly convicted and imprisoned, was finally met by a Treasury offer of £5,000 compensation—only once equalled in similar circumstances—which he accepted though claiming business losses of £40,000 and chronic bronchitis contracted at Portland.

Opinion continued to set against both the Unionist Government and Mr Chamberlain’s fiscal propaganda. On 7 January at Stalybridge, Mr J. F. Cheetham (Liberal) defeated Mr J. Travis-Clegg by 4,029 to 3,078 (majority 951), reversing a Conservative majority of 81 in 1900. Cotton-district Free Trade feeling and accumulated discontents were the simplest explanation. Mr Chamberlain’s Preston speech of 11 January to some 5,000 hearers made no reference to the reverse, but argued that the cotton trade’s “record” 1904 export values (up more than £10,300,000 on 1903) masked relative decline: Britain’s share of the world’s cotton consumption had fallen since 1896 from 41 to 25 per cent., while Germany, Austria, France and the United States had increased their trade more than 50 per cent. over 1892–1903. His remedy remained tariff revision and the power to “pay [foreign countries] in your own coin”; he carefully declared he would under no circumstances tax raw cotton. A carefully worded resolution designed to allow Balfourite “retaliation” and a colonial conference left him, he said, in an “embarrassing position,” for such a conference must attend favourably to colonial schemes of mutual protection.

Chapter II — Opening of Parliament and the Wyndham Crisis

Unionist fiscal dissensions sharpened before the session. The Greenwich Conservative Association, by 46 to 32, asked Lord Hugh Cecil—the most brilliant of the younger Free-Trade Conservatives—not to stand again; he refused (17 February), though Protectionist local opposition had been discouraged from Conservative headquarters. At the same time Lord Lansdowne’s foreign policy increasingly commanded confidence. A Blue-book on the day Parliament opened showed that Russia’s preposterous 1904 claim to decide at pleasure what was absolute contraband had been virtually abandoned for rice and provisions; coal and raw cotton remained contested in principle, but practical interference with British ships had ceased except for undoubted blockade-runners seized by Japan. Parallel firmness by the United States had helped.

Parliament was opened by the King and Queen in person on 14 February. The Speech recorded satisfaction at the visit of the King and Queen of Portugal; strict neutrality in the Russo-Japanese war; continued Balkan anxiety and Austro-Russian reform proposals to the Porte; ratification of the Anglo-French Convention; arbitration agreements with Sweden and Norway, Portugal and Switzerland; and the agreement with Russia for inquiry into the North Sea (Dogger Bank) disaster. Steps toward a representative constitution in the Transvaal were under earnest consideration; the Tibetan Agreement was noted with praise for the civil and military authorities; negotiations with the Amir of Afghanistan were impending. Domestically the Speech put first the Scottish ecclesiastical difficulty; promised economy in the Estimates; electoral redistribution; Alien Immigration legislation; permanent machinery for the unemployed (with the King’s “profound regret and sympathy” for abnormal winter distress); Scottish education; Workmen’s Compensation amendment; improved status for the Local Government Board and Board of Trade and a Minister of Commerce; valuation reform; naval prize consolidation; industrial-accident notification; renewal of Agricultural Rates and kindred temporary Acts; butter adulteration; and Crown Cases Reserved procedure.

In the Lords, Earl Spencer opened for the Opposition, complimenting Lansdowne’s prudence on war questions, pressing Macedonia, seeking more light on South African self-government and Chinese labour, supporting the India Secretary’s Tibet line while defending Sir Frank Younghusband, and criticising gigantic peace expenditure and successive Army schemes. Lord Lansdowne replied that intervention to end the Far Eastern war might bring rebuffs or worse; contraband had been reduced; a wider Russo-Austrian Macedonian reform scheme would be freely criticised; Transvaal proposals were deliberately limited at first; Chinese-labour prophecies had not been realised; Younghusband had admitted exceeding instructions, but his feat would live after the censure was forgotten. Redistribution, he added, was “physically impossible” this session—sharply reducing the Speech’s announcement. The Address was agreed. In the Commons a privilege motion against the new Lord Alington for canvassing North Dorset tenants was met by Balfour’s previous question (287–191), though the Sessional Order against Peers’ interference was confirmed 345–65.

The session’s Irish crisis centred on the MacDonnell–Wyndham affair: the confidence reposed by Chief Secretary George Wyndham in Under-Secretary Sir Antony MacDonnell, and MacDonnell’s association with Lord Dunraven and the Lord-Lieutenant, Lord Dudley, in conversations that had issued in the Devolution proposals of September 1904. Wyndham’s resignation followed; Sir Antony remained Under-Secretary and Dudley Viceroy. Lord Milner retired from South Africa and was succeeded by Lord Selborne. Walter Long became Irish Secretary and, after pressure, affirmed the Under-Secretary’s definite subordination.

The International Commission’s Dogger Bank report, Education Act difficulties, Navy Estimates, Churchill’s fiscal motion, Trade Unions and Trade Disputes, Coal Mines Employment and Workmen’s Compensation Bills, South African war supplies, the Transvaal Loan, Physical Deterioration, and a series of fiscal debates which Ministers boycotted—Opposition resolutions being carried nem. con. and ignored—filled the early months. Brighton’s bye-election was another Ministerial reverse. Lord Lansdowne spoke on Macedonia and on Germany in the Pacific. The Budget, Home Rule, Irish university education, Land Values, Agricultural Rates continuance, and the introduction of Unemployed Workmen and Aliens Bills carried the House to the Easter adjournment.

Chapter III — Aliens, Education Revolt, and Delcassé

Politicians treated the short Easter recess of 1905 as almost a complete holiday; the notable exception was Mr Brodrick at Godalming (27 April), who emphasised the non-Protectionist, self-sufficing character of Mr Balfour’s fiscal views and believed that if Balfour’s policy alone had stood against sheer Free Trade the Government would have had not a minority but a majority at Brighton. A Local Government Board order published 29 April regulated relief to underfed school children under sixteen (not blind, deaf or dumb, living with a father not on relief): applications must come from school managers, authorised teachers, or education officers; guardians were to inquire and grant relief ordinarily or as a loan, never in money, for not more than a month on a single application, preferably through charitable meal tickets—an effort to treat a painful problem on “sound lines.”

The Aliens Bill’s second reading (2 May) revealed that East London Liberals now largely accepted its main purport. A National Liberal Club meeting of East London Members and candidates (27 April), through Mr Sydney Buxton and eight others who wrote to Sir H. Campbell-Bannerman, hoped Liberals would not oppose second reading. Sir Charles Dilke moved that low-priced alien labour was best met by anti-sweating legislation and that asylum for persecution victims must be retained; Major Evans-Gordon denied anti-Semitic motives and called Britain a “refuse-heap for Southern and Eastern Europe.” Mr Asquith admitted excessive alien crime and approved exclusion of criminals, held the new Bill improved on 1904’s, but would not oppose second reading. Home Secretary Akers-Douglas promised boards of appeal including a magistrate (and hoped a poor-law guardian) and denied intent to exclude bona-fide political refugees. Mr Buxton from the front Opposition bench supported keeping out “uneconomic and undesirable” Russians and Poles who lowered wages and worsened East London overcrowding. Mr Chamberlain welcomed the Bill as protecting workers against underpaid immigrant labour and ironically congratulated Liberals on fiscal progress. Dilke’s amendment fell 211–59 (some ten Liberals with the Government); the Bill was then read a second time without division amid Ministerial cheers.

Education administration under the Act of 1902 was in mounting difficulty. The West Riding County Council resolved to dock denominational teachers’ salaries for time spent on denominational religious instruction—contrary to the Act; the Board of Education advised managers not to comply. Merionethshire was declared in default under the Defaulting Authorities Act of 1904 for failing to maintain voluntary schools. Most urgent was East Ham Borough Council’s April resolution refusing to administer the Education Act after May because of rates said to have reached 2s. 9d. in the pound (with another 9d. needed for new schools), threatening some 25,000 children. On 4 May Mr Gray (West Ham, N.) moved the adjournment; Mr Balfour refused to recognise abdication of local duty, noted the State already paid 60–70 per cent. of education costs, and said East Ham had spent £18 per child on buildings where elsewhere £12 10s. sufficed. The motion failed 159–116. East Ham contested his figures (claiming only 48 per cent. State grant) but on 23 May rescinded its “revolt” 12–7; a deputation of overburdened urban districts saw Balfour privately on 8 June.

The Trade Unions and Trade Disputes Bill was altered in Grand Committee and ultimately withdrawn by its promoters; the Unemployed Workmen and Workmen’s Compensation Bills advanced amid criticism. Balfour spoke at the Albert Hall, Chamberlain in Birmingham; a farewell banquet honoured Mr Choate. Scottish Education, Local Veto (Scotland) (defeated), and Land Values Taxation (Scotland) (second reading) occupied the House. Wyndham explained his resignation; Balfour made a major Imperial Defence statement (with a letter from Lord Esher); Lansdowne spoke on German Pacific islands, the German tariff, and the Anglo-Japanese Alliance. The resignation of M. Delcassé was received with regret in Britain. Fiscal debates continued in both Houses; the Scottish Churches Bill progressed; and the Speaker resigned, thanks were voted, and his successor elected.

Chapter IV — Morocco, War Stores, and the Decline of Balfour

The Whitsuntide recess opened with Lord Roberts’s impressive appeal of 12 June that rifle shooting become “a national pursuit” as archery once had been. He held that better shooting would have prevented most of the unfortunate incidents of the South African War and of 1880–81; failing conscription, security depended on national ability as well as willingness to take arms. Miniature ammunition and short ranges had overcome the obstacles of ranges and expense; under an association amalgamating the National Rifle Association and the Society of Miniature Rifle Clubs he sought at least £100,000 to put rifle clubs within every man’s reach. He avowed that conscription in some form could not permanently be avoided unless obligatory physical training and rifle instruction were enforced in schools up to eighteen—but that was for legislation. The Press was sympathetic; the money came in slowly.

The Co-operative Congress (14 June) claimed over 2,200,000 members, loan and share capital of £36,500,000, and annual trade of nearly £92,000,000, and resolved to exercise direct legislative influence; but a proposal to join forces with the Labour Representation Committee was crushed 801–135 as “handing over … bag and baggage, to a body financed by trade unions and controlled by Socialists.” Mr Haldane at Tranent (13 June) said the Government’s fiscal fog had unified Liberals behind Free Trade, and that Liberals would maintain the Japanese alliance as surely as Conservatives.

Foreign anxiety centred on German pressure upon France over Morocco. Delcassé’s retirement was generally believed due, directly or indirectly, to German hostility; the Sultan’s proposal of an International Conference, instead of accepting French reforms, was regarded as German-inspired at Fez. Britain’s obligation under the Anglo-French Agreement of April 1904 to give France diplomatic support seemed plainly engaged. On 15 June it became known that Britain had distinctly refused the Sultan’s invitation—yet not as a final reply, so that if France later accepted a conference after understanding with Germany on its scope, British support would follow. Paris acknowledged British loyalty and dismissed German suggestions that England wished to drive France into collision with Germany.

The same day brought the Report of Sir W. F. Butler’s Committee on sales and refunds to contractors in South Africa. Mr Arnold-Forster’s preface stressed incomplete evidence and that charges remained sub judice; subject to that, the story was startling. After peace a Pretoria sales department under Colonel Morgan (Army Service Corps) sold surplus stores cheaply to contractors who resold the same goods to the Army at high profit. On oats alone a contractor named Meyer was said to have gained £1 on every 300 lb bought and sold back—a gross daily profit of £2,000. The Committee condemned Morgan (and severely noted his brother Frank’s connection with contracting firms) and held successor Colonel Hipwell unfit and culpable; they abandoned mere negligence for an “impression of cleverly arranged contrivance.” The rhetorical style of the report invited discount, but public demand for a full inquiry was intense. Balfour, after first suggesting a Commons committee, then a non-statutory Royal Commission, was driven (22 June) to a statutory Royal Commission from the outset—losing time and reputation for business management. Commissioners named 26 June were Mr Justice Farwell (chairman), Sir George Taubman-Goldie, Field-Marshal Sir George White, Sir Francis Mowatt, and Mr S. Hope Morley, late Governor of the Bank of England. Sir Robert Reid’s vote of censure on stores mismanagement was debated and defeated by the Government’s full majority.

The Unemployed Workmen Bill was read a second time; the Indian Budget brought into the open the Curzon–Kitchener dispute over Indian military administration, the Home Government’s decision, and denials of the Viceroy’s resignation (premature denials, as Chapter V would show). The Aliens Bill passed committee under closure by compartments and was read a third time. East Finsbury inflicted another Ministerial defeat. Lord Roberts in the Lords warned of military unpreparedness; the War Secretary replied. The Churches (Scotland) Bill was carried through the Commons. Ministerial Redistribution resolutions, after an unfavourable reception and the Speaker’s ruling, were withdrawn. A Unionist party meeting failed to heal fiscal wounds. The Government was defeated on the Irish Land Commission vote and decided to ignore the defeat. The Unemployed Bill passed on a reduced scale after episcopal and labour appeals; the London County Council Tramways Bill was thrown out by the Lords. The Duke of Devonshire’s fiscal motion in the Lords was met by the previous question. As the session ended, the Register recorded the failure of the parliamentary machine and a marked decline in the Prime Minister’s position.

Chapter V — Entente Displays, Curzon’s Fall, Peace in the East, and the Change of Ministry

Though prorogation fell on Friday 11 August, Parliament’s corporate life continued one day longer for a crowning courtesy to the French naval visitors. On Saturday 12 August over 220 Peers and MPs entertained Admiral Caillard and about 130 officers of his squadron at luncheon in Westminster Hall—the Lord Chancellor and the Speaker presiding, Balfour toasting the French Navy and John Morley replying for the Houses. The occasion answered the British Atlantic Fleet’s reception at Brest under Admiral May. Parallel with Anglo-French cordiality ran Anglo-German irritation: after French assent to a Morocco conference early in July, Britain agreed (12 July) to attend, but German circles understood British sympathy lay wholly with France. Suspicious German comment on the Channel Fleet’s Baltic cruise was not matched by official coldness; by the Emperor’s order German squadrons interrupted manoeuvres to greet the British ships (28 August), with banquets at Swinemünde and warm municipal hospitality.

In the prorogation week appeared the Royal Commission report on Food Supply and Raw Material in time of war (chairman Lord Balfour of Burleigh; the Prince of Wales a member). Normally the country held about seven months’ raw cotton, two months’ manganese, and seven weeks’ wheat and flour. The main report saw no reasonable probability of serious interference with supplies but feared panic price rises and recommended a guarded national indemnity against maritime capture; only the chairman, the Prince of Wales, and Admiral Bosanquet signed without substantial reservation. Other sections urged free grain storage experiments, or held that a strong fleet made such experiments needless.

Doubt about the Curzon–Kitchener compromise was confirmed on 21 August by Lord Curzon’s definite resignation of the Viceroyalty. The immediate cause was the Home Government’s refusal to appoint General Barrow—whom Curzon particularly desired—as Military Supply Member of the Viceroy’s Council (they preferred him as Chief of Staff, fearing he would not inaugurate the new system “with an open mind”). Curzon read this as want of confidence and as divergence from the modifications he thought had been secured; Kitchener’s latest proposals seemed to him “almost indistinguishable” from the original scheme the Government had rejected. Brodrick at Godalming (23 August) stressed that in such questions the Secretary of State spoke for Cabinet and India Council, not personal whim, while paying tribute to Curzon’s past services. Publication (26 and 28 August) of lengthy controversial minutes by Kitchener and Curzon was widely judged a serious State blunder.

The announcement of 29 August that peace had been concluded between Japan and Russia was received with universal British satisfaction. Japan’s self-restraint in forgoing a large indemnity was admired; the terms were held to embody her success by sea and land and to secure her against recurrence of the dangers that had forced war. Gratification was deepened by the knowledge that the renewed and extended Anglo-Japanese alliance—by then understood to have been for some little time in existence—had facilitated Japan’s moderate temper.

The Trade Union Congress at Hanley (4–9 September), some 450 delegates representing about a million and a half organised workers, noted the mutilation and withdrawal of the Trade Unions Bill and the failure of Workmen’s Compensation, and urged great trade-union efforts. Volunteer discontents, Balfour’s reassurances, autumn fiscal campaigning (Asquith, Sir E. Grey on foreign policy and Chinese labour, Morley on the Japanese treaty, Chamberlain, Lord Hugh Cecil, the Duke of Devonshire, Lord Londonderry, Austen Chamberlain), the Queen’s and the Premier’s Guildhall appeals for the unemployed, and Lord Roberts’s propaganda for universal military training filled the months. The Conservative Conference at Newcastle saw a Chamberlainite triumph despite Balfour’s appeal for unity; Chamberlain’s contrary Bristol appeal and articles in the Times and Daily Telegraph pointed to the Premier’s resignation. After a transient Liberal crisis on Home Rule, a Macedonian Blue-book, and an announcement that the Volunteers were not to be reduced, Mr Balfour resigned. Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman became Prime Minister and formed an administration favourably received by public opinion. Balfour defended his resignation; Lord Rosebery commented on the new ministry; the new Premier set out Liberal policy; a despatch on Chinese labour issued; and an electioneering Christmastide posed the issues before the country.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. The Royal Commission on the great Churches Cause (Lord Elgin, Lord Kinnear, Sir Ralph Anstruther) reported on 19 April. Expressing regret at the “extreme bitterness” between the Free Church and the United Free Church, it held the Free Church, from paucity of numbers and poverty of resources, incapable of carrying on the work of the Church it claimed to represent and thus of putting its enormous endowments to their proper purpose—yet unjust that those endowments should pass absolutely and unconditionally to the United Free Church. It recommended a statutory Commission in which the whole Free Church funds and property as at 31 October 1900 should vest, charged to allocate them primarily to secure “adequate provision for the due performance of the purposes for which the funds were raised,” preferring the United Free Church where the Free Church could not execute the trusts, on equitable conditions and with liberal equipment for the Free Church according to Presbyterian standards. The “Wee Frees” received the report angrily; the United Free Church with satisfaction; Principal Rainy told its General Assembly (26 May) that the Government Bill would follow Commission lines.

The same day the Church of Scotland Assembly, on Lord Balfour’s motion, unanimously sought parliamentary liberty to ease the 1693 subscription formula—leading to clause 5 of the Ministerial Churches Bill, opposed by some English Low Churchmen and Nonconformists as a precedent for Anglican emancipation, and by Campbell-Bannerman and others as out of place, but carried with much Scottish Liberal support. The Act set up a strong executive Commission; the Free Church required unconditional subscription to the Confession’s literal meaning, the United Free a relation determined by its judicatories. Bye-elections, especially the Ministerial defeat in Buteshire, and Liberal triumphs in Edinburgh and Glasgow University elections, showed Scottish aversion to Protection and Unionist unpopularity. Campbell-Bannerman’s Premiership excited the liveliest Scottish interest; Imperialist Liberals felt represented by Haldane and Asquith for Scottish seats as well as by Grey and Fowler. The King reviewed over 38,000 Volunteers in the King’s Park, Edinburgh, on 18 September (estimated 250,000 spectators), including 700 Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve and a motor-car column, and hoped the spectacle would encourage recruitment. Clyde shipbuilding reached a record 540,080 tons; iron, steel, cotton and woollen trades prospered; jute and linen were hampered by raw-material prices.

Ireland. The year’s most striking political fact was the driving from the Chief Secretaryship—within two years of his acclaimed Land Purchase Act—of George Wyndham, a politician of high ability and partly distinguished Irish lineage, by the more vociferous Unionists angered at his confidence in Sir Antony MacDonnell and at MacDonnell’s part in the Dunraven–Dudley Devolution conversations of September 1904. Yet MacDonnell remained Under-Secretary and Dudley Viceroy: no further “human sacrifices” could be obtained without risk of breaking the Unionist Government. The new Chief Secretary, Walter Long, after pressure, affirmed the Under-Secretary’s definite subordination and a firm intention to enforce the law against agrarian intimidation; at Belfast (20 April) he pledged redress of legitimate grievances and “ascendency to no party, but to everybody justice and fair play.” In the summer and autumn the Executive forbade and suppressed meetings summoned to overawe graziers (aimed at breaking up grass lands for small farmers), tried intimidators before special juries under the Crimes Act, and restored Unionist confidence in the South and West. Land Purchase Act regulations of 9 July required Estates Commissioners to inquire into intimidation and postpone tainted applications—right as a penalty, doubtful as a presumption. Operation of the Act as a whole was blocked because agreed transfers already involved purchase money far exceeding available funds.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1905

By F. Harcourt Kitchin

Few years had been so satisfactory as 1905. A world-wide revival followed the post-war dulness: abundant American cotton of 1904 and bountiful harvests in Canada, the United States and Argentina carried prosperity to Europe. Britain, France, Germany, Austria and Italy had much to congratulate themselves upon; Canada and the United States enjoyed their finest industrial years. Russia remained a black spot from war drain and internal disturbance; Japan must suffer years of financial strain but was coming to be regarded as a “four per cent.” country.

British revival began in foreign trade. Total imports were valued at £565,279,402 (increase £14,240,774, or 2.5 per cent.). Exports of British and Irish produce advanced by £29,312,427 (about 9.7 per cent.) to £330,023,467—passing all records—and re-exports of colonial and foreign merchandise rose by £7,494,240 to £77,798,521. Manufactured exports increased by £26,133,863 (10.7 per cent.), cotton yarn and cloth to India and China far surpassing records; woollens and iron and steel also rose substantially. Imports of manufactures rose only £7,409,339 (of which £1,769,847 re-exported). By the partial test of manufactured export expansion versus import increase, 1905 was one of Britain’s best foreign-trade years.

Internal demand for credit was strong, especially later in the year. The Bank rate did not exceed 3 per cent. for the first nine months, rose to 4 in September on a foreign gold drain, and averaged only 3 per cent. for the year. Rand mines yielded £20,800,000 gold against £16,000,000 in 1904. London Bankers’ Clearing House clearings expanded 16.3 per cent., country clearings 5.2 per cent. Consols closed at 89⅛ against 88⅛ at the start. Japanese Four per cents. rose from 76 to 92½ while Russian Fours fell from 91½ to 82½. Japan borrowed abroad during the war £82,000,000 (£22,000,000 at 6 per cent., the rest at 4), floated a £50,000,000 foreign conversion loan at 4 per cent. (half issued, half retained to convert Sixes in 1907), and raised £48,000,000 internally; completed conversion left war debt about £138,000,000 (foreign £110,000,000, domestic £28,000,000) with annual interest £6,100,000 against normal peacetime revenue of some £23,500,000—yet monetary centres retained confidence.

Lancashire cotton had by far its most prosperous year: American crop over 13,500,000 bales (some 2,000,000 above world requirements); yarn exports up 25 per cent., cloth 11 per cent.; ninety spinning companies averaged 19 per cent. profit on share capital; sixty-six new mills were equipped at about £7,000,000—prosperity that stiffened Lancashire’s opposition to Chamberlain’s fiscal proposals. Woollen and worsted recovered more modestly; exports of yarns and fabrics rose nearly £2,500,000, nearly half from Japanese and Chinese military demand. Shipbuilding set a British record of 1,811,000 tons launched against 1,376,000 in 1904, though rising iron prices later checked new mercantile orders (new steamers ordered in September cost fully 20 per cent. more than a year before). Engineering, machinery (exports up nearly £2,250,000), waggon, motor and cycle trades (output estimated over £10,000,000, nearly all home) were better occupied; coal remained dull and cheap, aiding the revival. British works, Kitchin judged, had been overhauled and made more adaptable to modern requirements.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. The capital domestic event of 1905 was the Chambers’ vote for the separation of Church and State—long a Radical article always postponed until the Pope’s Nuncio’s absence from the New Year diplomatic reception at the Élysée signalled an impending religious crisis. A campaign against freemasonic delation in the army, channelled through General Février and the Legion of Honour, filled Conservative papers with confidential reports on officers’ politics. The session opened 10 January; M. Fallières was re-elected President of the Senate without opposition, while M. Doumer defeated M. Henri Brisson for the Chamber presidency 265–240 and used the chair against a Ministry he called a scandal. After a two-day confidence debate won only 287–281 (a virtual defeat once ministerial votes and Doumer’s abstention were counted), M. Combes resigned on 18 January. M. Rouvier formed a Government (commission 21 January; list 24 January): Rouvier as Premier and Finance; Berteaux remaining at War; Delcassé at Foreign Affairs; Chaumié to Justice; Etienne (Interior) and Thomson (Marine) among new men. The programme included the military law, repeal of the Falloux Law, Income Tax, Separation of Church and State, and an old-age pension fund.

The Military Law, carried in the Senate 239–37 (17 January–15 February) and later by the Chamber without modification, shortened active service to two years while retaining reservist and territorial training periods and requiring Polytechnique and Saint-Cyr students a year in the ranks. The Separation Bill’s general discussion occupied nine sittings ending 10 April; thirty-six further sittings to 3 July settled thirty-seven clauses regulating French religious life—Aristide Briand, as Reporter, making his name by tolerance and concessions to ministers of religion. The 1905 Budget totalled some 3,603,000,000 francs with a nominal estimated surplus of only 21,000 francs, later improved by harvests and colonial trade; Parliament actually increased expenditure by nearly 20,000,000 francs. Labour violence marked the year: at Limoges (April) a porcelain lock-out of nearly 20,000 followed a strike against Haviland; troops fired, killing two workmen (17 April); the strike ended 22 April. Toulon arsenal disturbances on May Day and a Lyons police strike (ended by Prefect Alapetite’s energetic requisition of troops) further strained order.

Colonial embarrassments—Congo scandals leading to Savorgnan de Brazza’s inquiry, the recall of General Gallieni from Madagascar, Indo-Chinese disorders—coincided with the Moroccan crisis. A German press campaign against the French mission under M. St. René Taillandier led up to William II’s theatrical visit to Tangier. Delcassé, feebly supported, offered resignation about 19 April after Nationalist and Socialist attack; it was not accepted, but he was soon forced from office under German pressure—an event deeply regretted in Britain and central to the English chapters’ account of the summer’s diplomacy. French public opinion, reviewing the International Commission on the Dogger Bank firing by Admiral Rozhdestvensky, held that England’s forbearance and France’s conciliatory rôle testified to the reality of the 1904 Franco-English agreement even as Morocco tested its most important colonial clause.

Italy. (The extract’s Italian section is not preserved in the working file; the Register pairs Italy with France in Chapter I of Part II.)

Chapter II — Germany, Morocco, and the Anglo-Japanese Settlement

German policy in 1905, as seen from the English narrative, was dominated by the challenge to France in Morocco and by friction with Britain. The Emperor’s Tangier demonstration, the forced retreat of Delcassé, and the Sultan’s conference proposal were read in London as a deliberate test of the Anglo-French Agreement. British refusal of the conference invitation (mid-June), later qualified so as not to embarrass a French acceptance on agreed bases, and Britain’s subsequent agreement to attend once France assented (announced 12 July), kept faith with Paris without closing the diplomatic door. Baltic Fleet courtesies in August and municipal hospitality at German ports showed that official Germany did not sustain the full bitterness of a section of its press. Lord Lansdowne’s statements on German Pacific islands and the German tariff, and the continued intimacy of the Entente, formed the British side of a relationship the Register describes as one of irritation without rupture.

The renewal and extension of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance—understood in Britain before its formal publicity—framed the Far Eastern settlement. Port Arthur’s fall in January, Japan’s military and naval triumphs, and the peace of late August were received in England with allied pride tempered by respect for Russian defenders. The alliance was held to have encouraged Japanese moderation on indemnity and to open trade prospects in the Far East that Liberals as well as Unionists meant to uphold.

Chapter III — Russia and the Far Eastern Peace

Russia’s year, in the British record, ran from the humiliation of Port Arthur and the Dogger Bank inquiry through catastrophic defeat to a peace that reversed her financial credit relative to Japan’s. The International Commission in Paris on Rozhdestvensky’s firing upon the Hull fishing fleet, aided by French diplomacy, averted worse consequences; England’s forbearance was noted on both sides of the Channel. Internal disturbance and war drain made Russia the “black spot” of the commercial year; Russian Fours fell heavily while Japanese credit rose. The peace terms, without a large indemnity, nonetheless embodied Japan’s completeness of success and were welcomed in Britain as securing the ally against a recurrence of the struggle.

Chapter IV — Colonies and Dependencies (from the English narrative)

South African questions remained politically explosive at Westminster: Chinese labour, the Transvaal Loan, steps toward representative institutions in the Transvaal (the Orange River Colony deferred), and above all the Butler Committee’s exposure of post-war stores sales and contractor refunds, which forced a statutory Royal Commission. Lord Milner’s retirement and Lord Selborne’s appointment marked a change of imperial proconsul. In India the Curzon–Kitchener controversy over military administration ended in the Viceroy’s resignation after the Home Government refused his nominee for Military Supply Member and seemed to him to reopen the substance of Kitchener’s original scheme. Tibet and Afghanistan appeared in the King’s Speech as diplomatic successes or negotiations in progress. Scottish and Irish chapters apart, the colonial note of the English history is one of administrative scandal, constitutional half-steps in South Africa, and a Viceroyalty broken on Army reform.
