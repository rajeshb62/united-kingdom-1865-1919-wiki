The Annual Register 1904

Part I — English History

Chapter I — Fiscal Confusion and the Opening of the Year

The year opened amid the political confusion caused by Mr. Chamberlain’s campaign for fiscal reform and the consequent division in the Unionist ranks. That controversy was only the most prominent of many troubles besetting the Government. Among difficulties already present or plainly imminent were the administration of the Education Act, licensing legislation, and the introduction of Chinese labour into South Africa. The report on the South African war had produced a profound impression of uneasiness among Ministerialists as well as among the Opposition, and it was felt that Lord Esher’s Committee, already devising reform, would encounter the hostility of existing Service interests. Abroad, the impending Russo-Japanese war threatened the peace of Europe because of the Franco-Russian and Anglo-Japanese alliances, while the distress in Macedonia and the inadequacy of the reform scheme raised fears of a spring insurrection that might lead to a European war.

The fiscal question occupied the largest share of public attention. Many eminent Liberal Unionists took the view expressed by Sir Frederick Pollock in a letter to the Times on 7 January, and shared by the Liberal Imperialist Mr. Haldane on 2 January, that Home Rule was in abeyance and that Protection or Free Trade was the supreme issue. Others held that, in view of Mr. Balfour’s Sheffield attitude of 1903, fiscal reform might be left an open question and that the Liberal Unionist organisation remained necessary to prevent a Home Rule revival. Mr. Chamberlain continued his campaign actively. On 1 January it was announced that the Federal Ministry of Australia had invited him to visit the Commonwealth; he replied that he could best serve the cause by remaining where the Motherland must answer the Colonies’ advances—though Lord Rosebery at Edinburgh on 6 January declared that Mr. Deakin’s telegram showed the preferential proposals attributed to the Colonies came from Mr. Chamberlain himself.

Board of Trade returns early in January afforded Free Trade arguments: the total volume of import and export trade was £903,000,000, or £25,000,000 above the highest figure previously recorded, and manufactured exports in 1903 amounted to £235,000,000—higher than Mr. Chamberlain’s “record year” of 1872 even after allowing for the fall of values. Sir John Gorst at Preston (4 January) stressed dangers to the cotton trade; Lord George Hamilton and Mr. Winston Churchill were repudiated by Unionist organisations in their constituencies for Free Trade views; Lord Robert Cecil stated that Lord Salisbury had opposed Mr. Chamberlain’s policy as far as developed before his death. The correspondence between the Duke of Devonshire and Mr. Chamberlain, published on 11 January, prepared the way for the later change in the Liberal Unionist organisation. The Duke held that the Central Association could not subsidise local bodies committed to tariff reform while claiming neutrality, and that the association’s existence was almost impossible to maintain; Mr. Chamberlain denied any need to break it up and proposed a general meeting of delegates, which was fixed for 3 February. Speculations about a Liberal and Free Food coalition followed; a dinner and reception at Wimborne House on 5 February did not produce one.

Mr. Balfour addressed his East Manchester constituents on 11 January—his first such speech as Prime Minister—assuring them that in a Russo-Japanese war Britain would “to the full carry out all her treaty obligations,” then defending a fiscal position aimed at better foreign relations and closer Colonial union while refusing to go beyond Sheffield: a tax on food was only justifiable as a means to Imperial union, and acceptance must be slow. Next day at Manchester he hinted at the Committee of Imperial Defence as another bond of Empire. Sir Henry Fowler and Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman commented severely on the uncertainty of his attitude; the latter, at Maidstone on 13 January, stressed the danger of political corruption in Mr. Chamberlain’s policy and said Liberals would accept Free Trade Unionist co-operation but must be free to amend the Education Act and preserve magistrates’ licensing discretion—without mentioning Home Rule. Mr. Chamberlain at Birmingham on 11 January defended his war record and repeated his fiscal arguments. Bye-elections, passive resistance to the Education Act, the prospect of a Licensing Bill, Chinese labour, Somaliland, Mr. Arnold-Forster on Army reform, the first report of Lord Esher’s Committee, and the conviction and suicide of Whitaker Wright filled the remaining weeks before the session.

Chapter II — Opening of Parliament and the Early Session

In dreary weather the King and Queen opened Parliament in state on Tuesday, 2 February. The King’s Speech referred in most cordial terms to friendly feelings between Great Britain and France as exhibited by the Anglo-French agreement and otherwise, announced similar agreements in negotiation with Italy and the Netherlands, and recorded an agreement with Portugal for arbitration by the King of Italy of the frontier between Portuguese South-West Africa and the Barotse Kingdom. It expressed satisfaction that the Alaskan boundary misunderstanding had been finally removed, reported General Egerton’s successes against the Mullah in Somaliland with Italian and Abyssinian co-operation, and closed a brief reference to Russo-Japanese negotiations with an assurance that Britain would gladly assist a pacific solution. On Macedonia it hoped for practical measures including gendarmerie reorganisation. Domestic paragraphs noted Australian and New Zealand laws giving effect to the 1902 Colonial Conference naval agreements, the New Zealand preferential tariff, and deep concern at the insufficiency of the cotton supply. A Political Mission had entered Tibetan territory, with Chinese concurrence, to secure observance of the 1890 Convention relating to Sikkim and Tibet. The Estimates, though framed with economy, imposed a serious naval and military burden; Army and War Office reform was under consideration. The legislative list was scanty: an Aliens Bill against criminal and destitute immigrants; licensing, valuation, and Scottish education measures; Irish Labourers and Housing amendment; Workmen’s Compensation and Public Health amendments; shop hours; naval prize consolidation; removal after the present Parliament of the need for re-election on acceptance of office; Scottish Congested Districts powers; and sea fisheries.

In the Lords the Address was moved by Lord Fitzwilliam and seconded by Lord Hylton. Lord Spencer, as Opposition leader, referred feelingly to the loss of Lord Salisbury, noted the change of leadership after the Duke of Devonshire’s resignation, and avowed confidence that the Marquess of Lansdowne would prove a worthy successor. He hoped for British mediation in the Far East, questioned Macedonia, Somaliland and Tibet (intimating that the Tibet expedition infringed the Government of India Act, 1858), deprecated any Licensing Bill that deprived magistrates of full discretion, complained of the Speech’s silence on South Africa, and demanded a clear fiscal declaration. Lord Lansdowne replied that the Government supported the Austro-Russian Macedonian scheme while reserving the right to propose more far-reaching measures if it failed; mediation between Japan and Russia had not been invited; the Tibet mission sought only a neighbourly frontier and trade agreement and, as not a military operation, did not contravene the India Government Act; Asiatic labour would not proceed without full Parliamentary discussion; and Ministerial fiscal policy was that of “negotiation or retaliation” defined in Mr. Balfour’s pamphlet and at Sheffield and Manchester.

In the Commons the debate was deprived of much interest by Mr. Balfour’s sudden severe influenza. Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman, speaking with unusual animation, pressed for an electoral appeal on the revolutionary fiscal proposals, congratulated the country on friendly relations with France (crediting the King with a worthy part), and criticised the meagre programme and the absence of Education Act amendment. Mr. Austen Chamberlain, answering for the Government, declined Far Eastern comment, promised Macedonian and South African papers, expressed confidence in the Transvaal war contribution, and asked the House to accept the principle of the need of fiscal reform. The Attorney-General, Sir R. B. Finlay, explained at length his reasons for not prosecuting Whitaker Wright under the law as it stood, taking full responsibility, though the Treasury would bear the costs of the prosecution that had been instituted by others. On 3 February Mr. John Redmond strongly reasserted the Nationalist demand for Home Rule, attacked educational grievances and the shelving of Lord Dunraven’s university scheme, and criticised the working of the Land Act. The outbreak of war between Russia and Japan, Chinese labour, fiscal amendments to the Address, the Venezuela settlement, Army and Navy Supplementary Estimates, the Musical Copyright and Mines (Eight Hours) Bills, Navy Estimates, the London County Council election, the second report of Lord Esher’s Committee, Army Estimates, Mr. Pirie’s fiscal resolution and the Prime Minister’s “other document,” Lord Rosebery at Newcastle, education compromise proposals, the Aliens Bill, the Education (Wales) Procedure Bill, and the Scottish Education Bill occupied the rest of the early session. The impending retirement of Sir William Harcourt was announced, and that of Sir Michael Hicks-Beach was also in prospect.

Chapter III — Easter Recess, the Anglo-French Agreement, and the Budget

The Easter recess produced few speeches of first importance; the coming Licensing Bill and Chinese labour were the chief topics. On the Education Act, preparations for resistance in Wales accompanied tentative compromise suggestions. Liberals demanded full popular control of all schools and abolition of tests for teachers; some would allow undenominational teaching in provided schools to be supplemented, out of school hours and by voluntary effort, with doctrinal teaching for children whose parents desired it. Mr. Lloyd-George at Llandrindod Wells on 5 April declared readiness to concede clergy a right of entry for that purpose. An Isle of Wight arrangement of 16 April leased Cowes and Gurnard voluntary schools to the education authority at a nominal rent with a half-hour weekly right of entry for clergy (and similarly for Roman Catholics and Nonconformists). Major Seely’s unopposed return for the Isle of Wight on 6 April, after resigning to test feeling on fiscal policy and Chinese labour, illustrated the precarious Unionist position; he had made concessions to Liberal opinion on licensing and education while still declaring himself a Unionist. The Oldham Conservatives on 13 April declined Mr. Winston Churchill’s offer to resign and stand again; a few days later he accepted an invitation to contest North-west Manchester in the Liberal interest. Sir William Walrond, chief Unionist Whip, told Tiverton on 12 April that a general election was unlikely in 1904 or even 1905.

The Ministerial position was strengthened by the successful conclusion of the Anglo-French Agreement, popularly ascribed to the King’s initiative. Summaries appeared in Paris on 8 April and London on 9 April; the full documents with Lord Lansdowne’s explanatory despatch were issued on 12 April. Under the Egypt-Morocco arrangement, Egyptian administration was freed from the Caisse de la Dette’s control over revenue and expenditure (its functions limited to receipt of assigned revenues and payment of debt interest); the Caisse’s accumulated surplus of about £5,500,000 would pass to the Egyptian Government; conversion of the Guaranteed and Privileged Debt was postponed till 1910 and of the Unified Debt till 1912. England’s predominant position in Egypt was practically recognised, France undertaking not to raise again the temporary character of the British occupation. In return France was recognised as having special interests in Morocco; Britain would place no obstacles in the way of French action to maintain order and assist reforms, while France recorded no intention of disturbing the political status quo, undertook absolute equality for British commerce for thirty years, and agreed not to fortify the coast near the Straits. On Newfoundland and West Africa, France finally abandoned treaty-shore landing and drying rights under the Treaty of Utrecht, with compensation for injured French subjects; in exchange she obtained access to the Gambia at Yarbatenda, the Los Islands opposite Konakry, and a Nigeria frontier rectification giving a more direct route from the Niger to Lake Chad. On Siam, Madagascar and the New Hebrides, French influence was recognised as predominant in the Mekong valley and British in the Menam, each Power disclaiming annexation of Siamese territory; Britain abandoned protests against the French Customs régime in Madagascar; a Joint Commission was to deal with jurisdiction in the New Hebrides. The Agreement was generally welcomed in both countries; Sir Michael Hicks-Beach at Bristol on 13 April gratefully acknowledged the King’s part and deprecated hasty fiscal change.

The Commons reassembled on 12 April. The conflict at Guru on 31 March had brought the Tibet mission under the Government of India Act, 1858, requiring Parliamentary authorisation for military operations by the Indian army outside India’s frontiers. Mr. Brodrick on 13 April moved a resolution authorising payment from Indian revenues of expenses of military operations protecting the mission, assuring the House that the political status quo would be maintained. The resolution was carried by 270 to 61 and agreed to in the Lords on 19 April. Final revenue returns of 31 March fell short of the estimate (£141,546,000) by £2,724,000—less bad than the £4,000,000 deficit the Press had feared. The Budget of Tuesday, 19 April, presented by Mr. Austen Chamberlain, was awaited with special interest as a possible foretaste of “scientific taxation,” but those expectations were not realised. Subsequent business included introduction of the Licensing Bill, the Trade Unions and Trade Disputes Bill, the second reading of the Aliens Bill, the Education (Local Authorities Default) Bill, cotton-growing debate, the Scottish Education Bill, the Whale Fisheries Bill, the Registration of Clubs (Ireland) Bill, the Street Betting Bill, the Bishoprics of Southwark and Birmingham Bill, a motion for repeal of the Irish Crimes Act of 1887, the Aged Pensioners Bill, and the Licensing Bill’s second reading, together with Mr. Chamberlain’s Birmingham speech on his relations with the Government.

Chapter IV — Whitsuntide to the Close of the Session

The Whitsuntide recess saw little development of the political situation. The Co-operative Congress at West Ham condemned interference with the existing fiscal system. A demonstration of 12,000 against the Licensing Bill filled the Albert Hall on 28 May, with Lord Peel in the chair advocating a time-limit of seven years at most, and speeches by Mr. John Morley, the Bishop of Kensington, Mr. Arthur Chamberlain and Dr. Clifford. The Carmarthenshire County Council on 21 May rescinded resolutions refusing to take over non-provided schools—explained as a tactical change of front rather than decreased hostility. The Report of the Royal Commission on the Militia and Volunteer Forces (28 May), signed by the Dukes of Norfolk and Richmond, Lords Derby and Grenfell and others, declared both forces unfit to take the field and, after rejecting ballot or the Swiss system, recommended Continental conscription—promptly repudiated by the War Minister in the Commons on 2 June.

Parliament reassembled on 31 May. The Anglo-French Convention Bill’s second reading was carried without a division after criticism by Mr. Gibson Bowles, Sir Charles Dilke and others and warm commendation by Sir Edward Grey and Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman; Mr. Balfour congratulated the House on removing Morocco from the number of non-Christian Oriental States whose relations with the Great Powers endangered world peace. The Shop Hours Bill passed second reading after rejection (130 to 42) of Sir C. Dilke’s amendment condemning its failure to regulate assistants’ hours; it empowered local authorities to close shops at or after 7 P.M. and provide a weekly half-holiday, subject to Home Secretary and provisional-order checks, excluding public-houses, druggists and refreshment rooms. The Penal Servitude Bill, explained by Home Secretary Mr. Akers-Douglas as enabling long sentences for habitual criminals with a lighter “habitual offender division” for part of the term—the outcome of a departmental committee appointed by Mr. Asquith in 1894—was read a second time but eventually withdrawn. The Cobden centenary of 3 June produced some seventy large Free Trade demonstrations; Sir H. Campbell-Bannerman spoke at the Alexandra Palace with Mr. Winston Churchill, Dr. Clifford and Mr. Lloyd-George; Mr. John Morley spoke at Birmingham. The Valuation Bill, transferring valuation to County Councils with quinquennial assessments, met strong City and Boards of Guardians opposition and never reached second reading.

The Licensing Bill’s committee stage began on 6 June. Clause 1 transferred to quarter sessions the power of refusing renewal of on-licences on grounds other than misconduct, structural deficiency, or fitness of the holder, exercisable only on reference from district justices and on payment of compensation. Mr. Ellis Griffith’s seven-year time-limit amendment was resisted by Mr. Balfour, who held that mere lapse of time could not take away licence-holders’ equitable right to consideration, that a time-limit would reduce compensation funds and the reduction of licences, and that ante-1869 beerhouses would revert to their original status. Several Ministerialists nevertheless favoured some time-limit. Independent hostility to the Bill continued; the Yorkshire Brewers’ Association even resolved to assist disestablishment in view of clerical opposition. Chinese labour, reference of the Aliens Bill to Grand Committee (later withdrawn), Congo State debate, Lord Rosebery at Queen’s Hall, bye-elections at Market Harborough, Devonport, Sowerby (Liberal victory), Chertsey (Liberal defeat), Oswestry (Liberal victory), Reading and North-East Lanark, Army reform debates and Mr. Arnold-Forster’s statement, the Education (Local Authority Default) Bill, Mr. Chamberlain at the Hotel Cecil and Welbeck, a Vote of Censure, the Finance Bill’s final stages, the case of Lord Dundonald, Lord Curzon at the Guildhall, a Colonial Office debate promising representative institutions for the Transvaal, Russian “Volunteer Fleet” seizures of British merchant ships, the Physical Deterioration Committee report, the Indian Budget, and the King’s Speech closing the session filled the crowded summer months. “Closure by compartments” was applied to the Licensing Bill; the session ended with the familiar “massacre of the innocents.”

Chapter V — The Autumn: Russian Cruisers, Education Struggle, and Political Campaign

For five weeks after the prorogation party strife was virtually suspended. Mr. Balfour, as President of the British Association, delivered at Cambridge on 17 August a characteristic address on the conception of the material world and the tendency of natural science toward an idealistic interpretation of the universe; fiscal discussions at the meeting tended on the whole against Tariff Reform. The first important news of the Recess was the renewed activity of the Russian Volunteer cruisers Smolensk and Peterburg off the Cape of Good Hope, the steamer Comedian being boarded eighty miles from Port Elizabeth on 21 August. Liverpool steamship owners and the Chambers of Shipping and Commerce pressed Lord Lansdowne and Mr. Balfour to settle the contraband question and protect British ships; a deputation from the East India and China section of the London Chamber of Commerce on 25 August cited differential treatment of British as against German ships and higher Lloyd’s premiums. Mr. Balfour announced that, at Russia’s request, two British cruisers would search for the vessels and transmit orders to stop their activity; he adhered to the view that a belligerent could not settle contraband for itself, doubted proof of differential treatment, and implied a firm intention to maintain neutral rights. The cruisers were found at Zanzibar on 6 September; H.M.S. Forte delivered the message, and they were to return to Europe.

The Trade Union Congress met at Leeds on 5–7 September. President Mr. R. Bell, M.P., condemned Chinese labour, fiscal policy, and neglect of social reform, and urged support only for candidates backing the Trade Disputes Bill. Resolutions called for a Minister for Industry of Cabinet rank, protested against conscription and against any departure from Free Trade, and a reference by Mr. John Ward to the King as “almost our only statesman” was received with enthusiasm. Army manœuvres (7–14 September) on a new plan involving naval co-operation tested an invasion hypothesis on the Essex coast under Generals French and Wynne and were later praised by Mr. Arnold-Forster as fruitful.

The Education (Defaulting Authorities) Act opened a new stage of Welsh resistance. At a National Convention at Cardiff on 6 October, attended by five or six hundred delegates, Mr. Lloyd-George declared the fight was “for the status of Wales as well as for religious equality”; rate aid was not to be given to denominational schools, and if the Defaulting Authorities Act were applied parents were to withdraw children from non-provided schools to voluntary schools in Nonconformist chapels, emptying Church schools of grant-earning pupils. Merionethshire hesitated; Cardiff municipal elections on 1 November returned a majority against Mr. Lloyd-George’s policy; the Welsh Bishops appealed for funds to repair Church schools. Passive resistance continued in England: for 1904 there were 29,974 summonses and 1,180 sales under distraint; since the movement began on 31 May 1903 the totals were 37,296 summonses, 1,504 sales, and 80 imprisonments. The King’s Bench on 18 November (Ash v. Nicholl; Cox v. Merriman) held that non-payment of the education rate disqualified for the franchise as part of the poor rate.

Lord Rosebery reopened the political campaign at Lincoln on 20 September, declaring the Government a danger to the country, denying any real Colonial offer at the 1902 Conference, and criticising the Ministerial record on Army reform, foreign affairs, and education while proposing free admission of recognised ministers of religion to teach children of their own creed. Mr. Chamberlain replied in the Times of 26 September, citing Australian and Canadian support for preferential trade. Mr. Brodrick at Bramley on 19 September referred with satisfaction to the treaty with Tibet signed on 7 September. The death of Sir William Harcourt, Mr. Chamberlain at Luton, the Thanet election, Mr. Long on the unemployed, Irish “devolution” proposals of the Irish Reform Committee (23 September), the Russian attack on English fishing boats on the Dogger Bank and the ensuing commission of inquiry, Lord Lansdowne at the Guildhall, arbitration treaties, the Beck case, a new scheme of naval organisation, Army reform speeches by Mr. Arnold-Forster, redistribution controversies, Mr. Chamberlain at Limehouse, a new fiscal Blue-Book, unemployment and pauperism, and the London County Council education scheme occupied the closing months as both parties prepared for a general election.

Chapter VI — Scotland and Ireland

Scotland. The history of 1904 was dominated by the conflict between the Free Church of Scotland and the United Free Church. The small minority who in 1900 had opposed the union of the Free Church with the United Presbyterians—nicknamed the “Wee Frees”—had brought actions restraining transfer of trust funds to United Free trustees. Twice defeated in the Court of Session, they appealed to the House of Lords in Bannatyne v. Overtoun and Macalister v. Young. Compromise proposals sanctioned by the United Free Assembly on 24 May—each body retaining churches and manses possessed on 15 May 1904, Free Church admission to United Free pension funds, £50,000 in cash, and costs on the disputed funds—were rejected by the Free Church. On 1 August the House of Lords reversed the Court of Session: Lord Halsbury (Lord Chancellor) with Lords Davey, James of Hereford, Robertson and Alverstone formed the majority; Lords Macnaghten and Lindley dissented. The majority held that the identity of a Church consisted in the identity of its doctrines; the United Free Church had abandoned the Establishment principle and, by the Declaratory Act of 1892, modified the Calvinistic doctrine of the Westminster Confession; trust property could not follow a change of doctrine. The decision was received in Scotland with general dismay. The “Wee Frees” claimed thirty-seven ministers, 105 congregations and 50,000 adherents; opponents put active ministers at about twenty-eight, mostly in the Highlands and Islands. They could find neither men nor funds for the three Divinity Colleges and the missions now legally theirs. After fruitless settlement proposals, the Court of Session on 24 October gave effect to the Lords’ judgment; the first church surrendered (Muir of Ord) was occupied on 15 November without violence, though later surrenders were less peaceful. Legislation was widely demanded; the Archbishop of Canterbury offered good offices; on 26 November the Secretary for Scotland proposed a Royal Commission, announced on 13 December as the Earl of Elgin, Lord Kinnear and Sir Ralph Anstruther, Bart., to meet in January 1905, with Sir John Cheyne as a “commission of one” for interim possession. By 14 December the Free Church had taken over eleven Highland and Island churches.

Scottish Unionism was cooler toward Mr. Chamberlain than English: a Fiscal reformer was badly defeated in North-East Lanark; the Ayr Burghs Unionist declined Tariff Reform League aid; the basis of Scottish Unionism was understood to be Mr. Balfour’s policy. The Education Bill failed, attributed mainly to local authorities’ dislike of financial provisions earmarking Imperial grants exclusively for education. Sir Henry Craik retired as head of the Education Department, succeeded by Mr. John Struthers. Sir Donald Currie gave £15,000 to Edinburgh University. Clyde shipbuilding output was 417,876 tons (decrease of 37,000 on 1903); Scotland’s total was 448,235 tons. Tweeds for the Far Eastern armies brought overtime in some textile factories; unemployment was far less serious than in England.

Ireland. The chief features of the year were the progressive operation of land purchase under Mr. Wyndham’s Act and a tendency among a section of Unionists toward compromise on the university question and even on Irish government. Nationalist agitation against the high prices demanded by landlords selling under the Act—ranging in some cases to twenty-three years’ purchase of first-term rents and twenty-six of second-term rents—continued vehemently early in 1904. Its tendency to obstruct sales induced Mr. William O’Brien, M.P. for Cork City, to resign on 15 January rather than join the obstruction; a new writ was not moved till 12 August, when he was returned unopposed. Mr. Justice Ross’s judgment that limited owners were not entitled under the Act (though he believed such had been Parliament’s intention) to appropriate the sale bonus to their individual uses necessitated an amending Act.

Supplementary Chapter — Finance and Trade in 1904

By F. Harcourt Kitchin

From the business point of view 1904 witnessed a gradual return to confidence. The early months seemed hopeless, but by December much depression had given place to cheerfulness. Business as tested by figures was better than expected; unlike 1903, which grew steadily worse, 1904 grew steadily better. Foreign trade, especially manufactured exports, and bankers’ clearing-house returns were strongest in the last quarter. Well-informed opinion held that 1904 marked the lowest point of the trade cycle’s downward half. Imports of colonial and foreign merchandise were valued at £551,362,000 (increase £8,762,000, or 1.6 per cent.); exports of British and Irish produce £300,818,000 (increase £10,018,000, or 3.4 per cent.); re-exports £70,322,000 (increase £748,000). The Economist computed that of the import increase £5,400,000 was due to greater quantities and £3,362,000 to higher prices; of the export increase £8,150,000 to quantities and only £1,868,000 to prices.

The Bank of England minimum rate stood at 3 per cent. from 21 April to the end of the year. Transvaal gold output overtopped 1899 and kept London well supplied. Japan placed two loans—half in London and half in New York—totalling only £22,000,000, readily subscribed on favourable terms; most proceeds remained in London, partly in British Treasury bills. France and Germany financed Russia. Consols touched a low of 85 early in March and a high of 91¼ on 1 June, closing at 88⅛; Irish Land stock, Transvaal Guaranteed Three per Cents. and Local Loans stock were preferred by investors seeking over 3 per cent. Japanese Fours recovered as much as 14 points from their lowest and closed near their opening price; Russian Fours were 6 lower on the year but remained well supported on the Continent at 91½ against Japanese Fours at 76 on 31 December.

Cotton experienced conditions of “famine” unmatched since the American Civil War. American raw cotton reached 8.96d. per lb. in February 1904; the 1903–4 United States crop was only 10,124,000 bales against world requirements of about 11,000,000; Lancashire mills went on short time. When the 1904–5 crop—officially estimated at 12,162,000 bales and believed near 13,000,000—came forward, prices fell to 3.77d. by year-end and mills worked full at profitable margins. The British Cotton Growing Association was formed to encourage Empire cotton and reduce dependence on America. Shipping suffered severely from low freights; nearly 1,400,000 tons of new vessels were nevertheless launched in the United Kingdom. Two 30,000-ton turbine Cunarders and nine steamers of 15,000 tons and over were under construction. The “Atlantic Rate War” between Cunard and the International Mercantile Marine Company (Mr. Morgan’s combination) and its allies, arising from Cunard’s Mediterranean–New York service and Liverpool passenger competition with White Star, ended on terms understood to give Cunard nearly all it fought for; Mr. Bruce Ismay of the White Star Line became president of the combination, whose extravagant capitalisation remained a standing object lesson in the worst kind of “American finance.” Iron and steel were depressed; Cleveland warrant iron advanced from 41s. 8d. to 51s. 2d. a ton in three autumn months under American warrant speculation. The finer Bradford woollen trade suffered from high raw-material prices after Australian drought, but Huddersfield, Dewsbury and similar districts turned out over £1,500,000 worth of military blankets and winter clothing for Japan. There had been better years than 1904 and many worse ones.

Part II — Foreign and Colonial History

Chapter I — France and Italy

France. “The situation has become intolerable,” exclaimed the President of the Council at a banquet on the eve of the Parliamentary session—a remark that gave the keynote for a year of harsh religious, military, naval and judicial contests under M. Combes’s Ministry. Early in January the Prefect of Meurthe-et-Moselle expelled the Abbé Delsor, an Alsatian deputy in the Reichstag invited to speak at Lunéville, as a German subject (7 January)—an incident the Nationalists later used against the Government. The session opened on 12 January; M. Brisson, after M. Bourgeois declined, was elected President of the Chamber by the narrowest margin (257 of 508, absolute majority 255). The Ministry survived a Socialist interpellation on police action against strikers only by a reconstituted order of the day of confidence. Dissensions between Radicals and Socialists threatened the Bloc; M. Jaurès’s effort, after the Far Eastern war began, to promote a Chamber declaration against the Franco-Russian alliance failed when the Radical Left and the Union Démocratique voted to maintain it (18 February), though Three per Cents. fell sharply to 93.35 francs on Bourse alarm. A proposal to redistribute Paris municipal seats was rejected against the Government (25 February, 293–226), but the Bloc was rallied by anti-clerical appeals after five officers at Vannes refused to assist expulsion of Congregations.

The Government’s Bill for the Suppression of Congregationist Education aimed to extinguish Congregationist teaching after a delay of some years and liquidate authorised Congregations’ property like that of the non-authorised. After tenacious debate the Chamber gave urgency by 310 to 262 (7 March); amendments confined the prohibition to France, extended the delay from five to ten years, and allowed teaching Congregations to maintain novitiates for colonial and protectorate schools. The modified measure passed on 25 March. Attacks by M. Millerand on neglect of workmen’s pensions yielded the Government only 281 to 271; M. de Lanessan’s accusations against Marine Minister M. Pelletan of compromising national defence forced an extra-Parliamentary inquiry. Parliament voted 450,000 francs for President Loubet’s visit to Italy despite Catholic protests that it insulted the Papacy, adjourned for Easter after a new amnesty (31 March), and during the holidays saw religious symbols ordered removed from courts of justice.

The Anglo-French Agreement signed on 8 April was received cordially by French opinion and Press, Nationalist and some maritime deputies dissenting on Newfoundland; M. Étienne in the Dépêche Coloniale gave the best-informed view of approval for positive advantages and general foreign-policy effect. President Loubet’s visit to Italy (24–30 April) was an event of first-rate importance: despite Catholic efforts to prevent the representative of France saluting the King of lay Italy at the Quirinal, M. Loubet was most cordially acclaimed, and the reconciliation between France and Italy was considered definitely sealed. Municipal elections (1 and 8 May) defeated the Nationalists in Paris, where Socialists allied to Radicals reconquered the majority; over the country Socialists and especially Radicals gained at Nationalist expense. Colonel Marchand, refused permission to join Kuropatkin’s staff in Manchuria, resigned (accepted 13 May) and became free for open politics. On the Chamber’s reassembly the Budget Commission returned eighteen Opposition to fifteen Ministerial members—a secret-ballot humiliation. Debate on the Papal note of 4 May and the recall of the French Ambassador from the Vatican produced an order of the day approving rupture of political relations—the first step toward separation of Church and State. A subsequent scandal over alleged offers to the Chartreux monks of permission to remain in France for one or two million francs led to a commission of inquiry under M. Flandin, predominantly Opposition, which failed to establish how far the “intermediaries’” assertions could be trusted.

Italy. The year sealed Franco-Italian reconciliation through the exchange of royal and presidential visits culminating in Loubet’s reception at Rome. French Catholics protested that the presence of the President of the Republic as guest of the House of Savoy was an affront to the Papacy; French Republicans held that the Italian Sovereigns’ courtesy visit to France made a return visit to Rome imperative. The political effect was to confirm Italy’s improved relations with France while the Triple Alliance framework remained in the background of European diplomacy treated elsewhere in the Register.

Chapter II — The Far East and Related Foreign Questions (as reflected in English affairs)

The Register’s English narrative makes the Russo-Japanese War a constant pressure on British politics throughout 1904. From January the world awaited the outbreak; Mr. Balfour’s Manchester assurance of full treaty obligations to allies set the tone of British neutrality allied to Japan by the 1902 agreement and constrained by the Franco-Russian connection. Once war began, Russian Volunteer Fleet cruisers interfered with British commerce; the Smolensk and Peterburg episodes off South Africa and their interception at Zanzibar, disputes over contraband, and differential insurance at Lloyd’s forced repeated Ministerial declarations on neutral rights. The Dogger Bank attack on English fishing boats later in the year produced a grave crisis resolved by a commission of inquiry. Concurrently the British mission to Tibet, authorised after the Guru conflict under the Government of India Act procedure, advanced to a treaty signed on 7 September; Lord Curzon’s Indian policy and fears of Russian influence in Lhasa framed the Parliamentary debates of April. Chinese labour for the Rand mines, debated through the session and attacked by Liberals, Labour and the Trade Union Congress, linked Far Eastern labour supply to South African reconstruction and to the promise, on the Colonial Office vote, of representative institutions for the Transvaal. The Anglo-French Agreement of April, by settling Egypt, Morocco, Newfoundland and Siam, removed several of the “non-Christian and Oriental” friction points Mr. Balfour named as dangers to world peace, and formed the diplomatic counterpart at home to Loubet’s Italian journey and the wider realignment of the Western Powers.
