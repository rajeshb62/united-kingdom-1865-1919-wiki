The Politics of Grand Strategy: Britain and France Prepare for War, 1904-1914 (1969)

Samuel R. Williamson, Jr.’s foundational study of the Anglo-French Entente as a working alliance — the first detailed analysis, based on British, French, and Belgian archives, of the secret military and naval conversations that committed Britain to continental intervention in 1914.

Source

Williamson, Samuel R., Jr. The Politics of Grand Strategy: Britain and France Prepare for War, 1904-1914. Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1969.

Digital scan via the Internet Archive (China-America Digital Academic Library), captured from a Harvard University Press first edition.

Williamson’s book was a pioneering work in the study of alliance politics and entente diplomacy. Drawing on newly opened French military and naval archives (including the deliberations of the Conseil Supérieur de la Guerre and the Conseil Supérieur de la Marine), the British Cabinet and Committee of Imperial Defence papers, the Belgian Foreign Ministry archives, and private manuscript collections including the diaries of General Henry Wilson, Williamson reconstructed the full story of the secret staff conversations and their impact on the strategic options of both powers. The book remains a definitive treatment of how the Entente Cordiale evolved from a colonial settlement into a de facto military alliance.

Contents

Chapter 1 — The Formation of the Entente Cordiale, 1898-1904 (p. 1)

The Anglo-French colonial agreements of April 8, 1904: the settlement of disputes over Egypt, Morocco, Newfoundland, West Africa, Siam, and Madagascar. The background of Anglo-French rivalry from Fashoda (1898) through the Boer War. The diplomatic negotiations under Lord Lansdowne and Paul Cambon. The nature and limits of the Entente Cordiale as a diplomatic understanding rather than a military alliance.

Chapter 2 — The Entente Challenged: The First Moroccan Crisis, 1905 (p. 30)

Germany’s challenge to the Entente through the Kaiser’s landing at Tangier (March 1905) and the subsequent diplomatic crisis. The fall of Théophile Delcassé. British support for France at the Algeciras Conference. The first test of the Entente’s political cohesion and the emergence of a pattern of Anglo-French diplomatic cooperation against German pressure.

Chapter 3 — The Military Conversations Begin, 1905-1906 (p. 59)

The initiation of unofficial Anglo-French military staff conversations during the Moroccan crisis of 1905-1906. The role of King Edward VII, Sir Edward Grey, and the French attaché Victor Huguet. The informal talks between General Grierson and Colonel Laguiche. The Cabinet’s limited knowledge and the precedent of secrecy. The first tentative discussions about possible British military cooperation with France in the event of a German attack.

Chapter 4 — Preparing for War: British Defense Policy, 1906-1910 (p. 89)

The evolution of British defence policy under the Liberal government. The reforms of the Committee of Imperial Defence and the War Office (the Haldane reforms). The creation of the British Expeditionary Force. The differences between Admiralty and War Office strategic thinking — the “blue water” school versus the continental commitment. The Army’s growing orientation toward intervention on the Continent.

Chapter 5 — French Strategy in Transition, 1905-1911 (p. 115)

The evolution of French strategic planning from Plan XV to Plan XVI. The debates within the French General Staff and the Conseil Supérieur de la Guerre. The influence of General Michel’s proposals for a defensive reserve army and their rejection. French planning for a concentration on the Belgian frontier. The developing French need for British military support to counter German numerical superiority.

Chapter 6 — The Entente Under Stress, 1908-1911 (p. 131)

The strains within the Entente between the Algeciras settlement and the Agadir crisis. The Bosnian crisis of 1908-1909 and its impact on Anglo-French relations. German naval building and the British naval scare of 1909. The growing divergence between British and French strategic priorities. The role of General Henry Wilson as Director of Military Operations and his efforts to deepen the military commitment.

Chapter 7 — Agadir Confirms Britain’s Continental Strategy (p. 167)

The second Moroccan crisis (Agadir, July-November 1911) as a turning point in Anglo-French strategic relations. The German gunboat Panther at Agadir. The Mansion House speech by David Lloyd George. The critical CID meeting of August 23, 1911, at which the Army’s plans for a continental commitment (six divisions to France by M+15) were formally adopted over the Admiralty’s amphibious strategy. The ousting of Admiral Wilson and the triumph of General Wilson’s continental strategy.

Chapter 8 — Joffre Reshapes French Strategy, 1911-1913 (p. 205)

General Joffre’s assumption of command and the reformulation of French war planning: the adoption of Plan XVII. The concentration of French armies on the German and Belgian frontiers. The debates over the use of Belgian territory. The coordination of French railway movement schedules. The Schlieffen Plan and the French response. Joffre’s demands for a British contribution and the integration of the BEF into French defensive plans.

Chapter 9 — The Naval Conversations Begin, 1911-1912 (p. 227)

The beginning of Anglo-French naval staff conversations. The Mediterranean naval balance and the German threat. Churchill’s arrival at the Admiralty and his reorientation of British naval policy. The Anglo-French naval agreement: the concentration of the French fleet in the Mediterranean and the British commitment to guard the Channel and the North Sea. The strategic implications of this division of naval responsibility.

Chapter 10 — The Haldane Mission, 1912 (p. 249)

Lord Haldane’s mission to Berlin (February 1912) as a last attempt at Anglo-German détente. The proposed naval and colonial agreements. The failure of the mission: Germany’s insistence on British neutrality in a continental war as the price of a naval ratio agreement. The diplomatic aftermath and the hardening of British policy toward Germany. The strengthening of Anglo-French ties as a consequence of the failure of Anglo-German understanding.

Chapter 11 — The Mediterranean and the Entente, 1912 (p. 264)

The Mediterranean dimension of the Anglo-French relationship. The Italo-Turkish War and the Balkan Wars. The British decision to withdraw battleships from the Mediterranean to concentrate against Germany in the North Sea. The French assumption of the primary naval role in the Mediterranean. The exchange of letters between Grey and Cambon (November 1912) and the question of “moral obligation.” The political battles within the Cabinet over Mediterranean policy and the extent of commitment to France.

Chapter 12 — The Political Consolidation of the Entente, 1912 (p. 284)

The consolidation of the Entente at the political level through 1912. The role of the radicals and the discovery of the secret military conversations by the radical wing of the Liberal Party. The exchanges of letters between Grey and Cambon and the definition of the Entente as a diplomatic understanding without formal alliance obligations. The politics of secrecy and the limits of parliamentary knowledge.

Chapter 13 — Prepared for War: The Entente Military and Naval Arrangements (p. 300)

The detailed military and naval arrangements completed by 1913-1914. The BEF’s concentration zones in France (Maubeuge-Le Cateau-Hirson). The railway timetables and supply arrangements. The Anglo-French naval conventions for the Channel and the Mediterranean. The role of General Henry Wilson as the principal architect of the military arrangements. The degree of British commitment demonstrated by the specificity of the operational plans.

Chapter 14 — The Entente on the Eve of War, 1913-1914 (p. 328)

The state of the Entente on the eve of the Sarajevo assassination. The CID’s invasion inquiry of 1913-1914 and the debate over compulsory military service. The political vulnerability of the Asquith government. The Curragh mutiny (March 1914) and its effect on the Army’s readiness. The state of Anglo-German relations. The public uncertainty about British intentions and the private certainty of the military planners.

Chapter 15 — The Anglo-French Entente Goes to War: August 1914 (p. 343)

The July crisis and the Anglo-French response. The role of the Entente in shaping British decision-making. The Cabinet crisis of August 2-4. Sir Edward Grey’s speech to the House of Commons. The German ultimatum to Belgium and the invasion of Luxembourg. The British declaration of war. The mobilization and dispatch of the BEF to France. The fulfilment of the secret staff conversations. Williamson’s analysis of the Entente’s role in converting a continental war into a world war.

Select Bibliography (p. 373)

Extensive bibliography organized by archival sources (British, French, Belgian), documentary collections (published diplomatic documents), and secondary works (books and articles in English, French, and German). Key archival collections used: Adm. (Admiralty Archives), Cab. (Cabinet and CID Archives), F.O. (Foreign Office Archives), W.O. (War Office Archives), Archives Guerre (French Ministry of War Archives), Archives Marine (French Ministry of Marine Archives), M.A.E. (French Foreign Ministry Archives), Belg. Arch. (Belgian Foreign Ministry Archives), and RA (Royal Archives, Windsor).
