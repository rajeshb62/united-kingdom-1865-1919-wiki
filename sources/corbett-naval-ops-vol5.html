Naval Operations, Vol. V (Newbolt, 1931)

Source

Official History of the Great War: Naval Operations, Volume V. London: Longmans, Green and Co., 1931. Continuation of Corbett’s series by Henry Newbolt. Full text: archive.org.

Text

Volume V covers the climax and defeat of unrestricted submarine warfare from April 1917 to the Armistice: beginnings and extension of ocean convoy; Mediterranean reorganisation under Gough-Calthorpe; Dover Barrage reform under Keyes; the Zeebrugge–Ostend blocking operations of April 1918; Northern Barrage mining and U-boat operations on the American coast; Russian and Black Sea naval questions; and the Mediterranean and Flanders endings of hostilities. Digests below are grounded only in the available chapter extracts.

Contents

Chapter I — The Submarine Campaign, April to August 1917 (Beginnings of the Convoy System)

The German submarine offensive reached its furthest point in April 1917 and continued to cause serious loss until October, when Newbolt marks a period of “slack water”; by the following March replacement of shipping began to exceed losses and the danger was visibly past. At the crisis the Admiralty’s choice for saving tonnage still lay between controlled sailings and protected traffic lanes on the one hand and a comprehensive convoy system on the other—a decision of the heaviest responsibility, for upon it hung the safety or destruction of the Allied Powers. No reliable system of hunting and destroying submarines had yet been developed. First Sea Lord Jellicoe was held back by the reflection that destroyers for convoy would have to be collected from commands already inadequately supplied; Admiral Duff, as Director of the Anti-Submarine Division, held that unless escorting ships bore a very large proportion to the number of ships in each convoy the system would be an additional danger rather than a protection, and shared misgivings about differences of speed, loss of safety from zigzagging, and the tendency of merchant ships to straggle at night.

The War Cabinet, increasingly conscious of ultimate responsibility as losses mounted, pressed to satisfy themselves that trade-route defence was the best possible and that no better method was being neglected. Jellicoe, as Commander-in-Chief Grand Fleet, had written to the Prime Minister on 30 October 1916 anticipating increased submarine danger in the spring; at a War Committee meeting on 2 November 1916 (Asquith, Jackson, Oliver present) he did not approve of convoys as offering too big a target, and doubted that merchant ships could be kept sufficiently together for a few destroyers to screen them. Runciman added that convoy was wasteful of tonnage because speed was regulated by the slowest ship. When Jellicoe became First Sea Lord, Admiralty opinion had if anything hardened: an official pamphlet of January 1917 stated emphatically that vessels should sail singly whenever possible and that convoy was not recommended in any area where submarine attack was a possibility. The Director of the Trade Division had minuted that experience had not justified convoy outside the Mediterranean. Yet Jellicoe remained clear that if the existing system needed reinforcing it could only be by some kind of convoy, and one of his earliest minutes warned the Staff that they might be obliged to place some of the Atlantic trade under convoy.

Chapter II — The Mediterranean, August 1917 to April 1918

When Vice-Admiral Sir S. A. Gough-Calthorpe took up the Mediterranean command in August 1917 the outlook was sombre: daily sinkings still varied almost in direct proportion to the number of U-boats on cruise, no German submarine had been sunk since May, and the complex of defensive measures devised by three navies never seemed to reduce average daily destruction per operating boat. Shipping was relatively safe along the eastern Spanish coast and inside the Italian western coastal defence system, but these were only strips in a great inland sea. Calthorpe believed losses could be reduced only by concentrating offensive force where U-boats were most likely to be found—above all the Straits of Otranto, through which so large a proportion of German boats passed twice every cruise—yet Italian policy had repeatedly refused a permanent destroyer patrol over the mobile barrage of drift nets and trawlers, and barrage forces had practically been withdrawn (at sea only one day from June to 24 July; not at all between 24 July and 19 August).

The Allied naval conference in London in the first week of September unanimously decided that Mediterranean commercial traffic should be placed in convoy as far as possible. Staff analysis fixed nine routes requiring escort and estimated about 300 escorting units; when the decision was reached, convoys ran only between Malta and Egypt and between Gibraltar and Oran. The inter-Allied Commission de Malte (Calthorpe chairman; colleagues Fergusson, Ratyé, Salazar, Sato) was the only body constitutionally able to reorganise control and routing. Routes planned included Gibraltar–Genoa, Gibraltar–Bizerta, Bizerta–Port Said, Marseilles–Bizerta, Marseilles–Algiers, Malta–Suda Bay (base to transfer to Milo), Naples–Bizerta, Malta–Taranto, and Oran–Gibraltar. Meanwhile Whitehall’s Convoy Section ran separate “through” England–Port Said convoys at ten-day intervals; Fergusson urged that their arrivals synchronise with home-bound Gibraltar departures so local escorts could be used economically—largely possible only because of American reinforcements at Gibraltar (light cruisers Birmingham and Chester; gunboats Sacramento, Nashville, Machias, Castine; revenue cutters Ossipee, Seneca, Manning, Yamacraw, Marietta; yachts Yankton and Nahma). The first through convoy left England on 3 October. Caporetto (Austrian attack 24 October) then threatened new northern Adriatic reinforcement demands as the Italians fell back toward the Piave. On the Palestine coast, coastal bombardment supported Allenby: on 30 October monitors M 31 and M 32 and the Grafton (Jackson’s flag) opened fire near Gaza, while the large monitor Raglan and seaplane carrier City of Oxford (destroyers Comet and Staunch) bombarded Deir Sineid; the simulated landing and bombardment continued through the successful Beersheba attack (31 October) and the assault on Gaza (1 November).

Chapter III — The Submarine Campaign in Home Waters and Extension of the Convoy System (August–September 1917)

As the summer of 1917 closed, German public anxiety about results at sea was matched by Allied misgivings: Russian armies demoralised by revolution; French armies still suffering from the spring defeat and the catastrophe of Craonne; hope of breaking Central Power resistance by a general Allied offensive practically gone. British Ministers could only take upon Great Britain the utmost of the Allied burden and hope American reinforcements in the spring would turn the tide—yet monthly shipping losses continued far in excess of replacement power. Conferences of 12, 13 and 20 July between War Cabinet, Admiralty, Ministry of Shipping and shipping industry showed voyages requiring protection more manageable than supposed; the Admiralty agreed to consider smaller destroyer escorts and to try trawler escort for slow Mediterranean ore ships. On 2 August ship-owners pressed for extension of convoy to vessels over twelve knots and for grouping by speed. With newer U-boats, speed had ceased to be adequate protection unless it approached twenty knots; North Atlantic sailings were to be rearranged to escort faster ships and reduce speed disparity within each convoy, while preserving as far as possible the old grouping by West- and East-coast destination.

The first regular homeward Gibraltar convoy had sailed on 26 July—ocean escort of “special service vessels” (Q-ships), home danger zone covered by trawlers stiffened by one or two destroyers, and local sloops, torpedo boats and armed yachts clearing Cape Spartel. Early in June Lieutenant-Commander Meusel in U 155 (two 5.9-inch guns) had opened a new phase by sinking the Scottish Hero about 450 miles north-west of Cape Finisterre, then cruising near the 43rd parallel and the Azores–Madeira passage until 11 August (nineteen vessels in rather over a hundred days). South Atlantic convoy followed promptly: faster ships (daily run 270 miles) routed to Sierra Leone, slower to Dakar; first Sierra Leone convoy HL1 (seven vessels) sailed 11 August under Port Convoy Officer Commander H. B. Worsley; Rear-Admiral Sheppard organised the first Dakar convoy HD1, with Rear-Admiral H. J. L. Clarke sent out as Port Convoy Officer. Outward convoy, postponed solely for escort shortage, became imperative as homeward sinkings fell and outward losses rose (April ratios: 18 per cent homeward sinkings to sailings, 7 per cent outward; by August the balance reversed). Outward convoys were kept formed only through the danger zone then dispersed; the pivot of the whole system became the Convoy Committee’s proposal that the outgoing escort meet and bring in a homeward convoy—destroyers zigzagging nearly two days with the outward body, steaming by night to the destroyer rendezvous, and meeting the homeward convoy at daybreak, with no margin and rigid programme adherence required.

Chapter IV — The Campaign in Home Waters and Extension of Convoy, October and November 1917

The autumn of 1917 was a time of transition: operations at sea appear in perspective as a depressing sequence of minor reverses and guerilla warfare between patrol craft and submarines, yet in reality a period of endurance, adaptation and construction. Allied land disasters—Ypres offensive continued only as relief; Caporetto and the Italian retreat to the Piave; collapse of the Russian Provisional Government and armistice with the Central Powers—left Admiralty policy only one form: reduce shipping losses to a bearable figure by extending and completing the convoy system, and prepare for American assistance in the coming year. The High Naval Command’s achievement was administrative extension of special trade defence to every important Atlantic mercantile route and correction of faults wherever they appeared; naval forces brought hundreds of convoys to port almost without loss and made U-boat operations more difficult than ever—results that take few lines to state, while minor reverse actions fill many pages.

October opened with a large intercepting operation against U-boats expected to return to Germany in the first ten days of the month. After entering the North Sea, German boats were accustomed to steer south between the meridians of 0° 30′ and 3° E., passing between minefields east of the Dogger Bank and the Outer Silver Pit—a truncated-funnel zone some 315 miles long, about eighty-five miles wide at its northern end and forty-five at the southern. The Admiralty plan was to watch the entire zone for ten days and set a submarine trap of mine nets in the narrowest part. Forces included four submarines of the 10th and 11th Flotillas at the northern end; two leaders and fourteen destroyers of the 13th Flotilla and yacht Shemara opposite Moray Firth and Firth of Forth; Captain P. H. Warleigh in yacht Goissa with four 13th Flotilla destroyers and sixteen trawlers on the net line; Commodore Tyrwhitt at the southern Dogger end. Totals fluctuated—orders allotted twenty-four trawlers, forty-two net drifters, twenty-one destroyers, one flotilla leader and four submarines, with Commander-in-Chief detachments between fifteen and twenty-nine destroyers and leaders and up to eighteen Harwich destroyers engaged. Submarines G 3, G 4, G 7 and G 11 took station on 27 September; nets were laid from 1 October. Hydrophones on William Tennant, Oyama, Chieftain and Swallow recorded submarine movements and explosions; Swallow dropped a depth charge. Gale conditions repeatedly drove Ithuriel and her destroyers into Aberdeen and Peterhead and toward the Orkneys; on the nets, ships kept head to wind; by 6 October Warleigh’s letters had to be floated over in a sealed cylinder and hydrophones were not even put over the side. The extract ends mid-operation on 7 October as destroyers on the net line were relieved.

Chapter V — The End of the Year 1917 in Home Waters (Dover Barrage; Channel Concentration)

In September German submarine captains began to abandon the outer approaches and operate closer in; the tendency became settled policy only in November. After a lull in the first half of the month, between the 13th and 18th nine submarines were located on the approach routes, principally in St George’s and the English Channels, focusing where convoys dispersed—possibly the preliminary of the general campaign against convoy that Admiral Sims had foreseen in September. U-boats concentrating on the Channel and Liverpool approaches appeared to be passing almost entirely through the Dover Straits; the north-about route seemed temporarily abandoned. On 17 November the Admiralty appointed Rear-Admiral Roger Keyes (Director of Plans) chairman of a Channel Barrage Committee (Captain F. C. Learmonth; Captain Cyril Fuller; Captain F. S. Litchfield-Speer; Colonel Alexander Gibb, R.E.; Mr W. McLellan) to investigate barring the Dover Straits and to inquire whether the existing barrage between the South Calliper and the Flanders coast actually obstructed German submarines. Early in July Bacon had proposed a deep minefield between Cape Gris Nez and the Varne Shoal; Admiralty had approved and first lines were about to be laid when the committee assembled.

The committee’s first report was highly critical: throughout the year German submarines had passed the Straits without difficulty and probably used the large light buoys as navigational marks; as an obstruction to surface craft the barrage was almost equally useless—the committee in Swift had passed over the upper jackstay. They urged the deep Gris Nez–Varne minefield extended toward Folkestone, strongly illuminated by searchlights, lightships and intermittent flares from trawlers so that boats would not learn to cross on the surface. Bacon would sanction only modified lighting and deprecated lightships. The committee also recommended a second barrage of four lines of new acoustic or magnetic mines between South Calliper and the Dyck shoal, with indicator loops (Professor Bragg’s experiments) between the two obstructions. On 29 November, when the report reached the Board, eight German submarines were in the English and St George’s Channels (U 96 off the Smalls; U 101 off north Cornwall; U 57, UB 80, UB 62, UB 35 and an unidentified UC-boat in the Channel); a week later eleven were out. Papers and survivors confirmed the Dover barrage was no real obstacle: the Straits were navigated 334 times during 1917 and only three submarines were sunk. Productive waters off Ushant and the Scillies could be reached in about sixty-five hours via Dover versus much longer north-about. On 14 December the Admiralty ordered Bacon to concentrate patrol craft on the deep minefield from near the French shore to the Varne, if necessary withdrawing them from Flanders and Channel barrages, and to assemble a strong destroyer force against Zeebrugge raids. Bacon’s long reply warned that weakening Dunkirk and the Belgian barrage would free German destroyers’ entrance and exit routes and expose minefield drifters to shattering attack. A Whitehall conference on 18 December only emphasised strategic division: Bacon’s dispersed local defence of many points versus staff insistence on concentrating every available vessel on the deep minefield to compel submarines to dive onto the mines. Bacon left London after undertaking to station a flare and searchlight arrangement; the extract ends as events of the next twenty-four hours strengthened supporters of the committee.

Chapter VI — The Beginning of the Year 1918 in Home Waters

On 2 January the Commander-in-Chief attended a Whitehall naval conference. After discussing the impending attack on Flanders bases and intensive air attack on enemy naval bases, the conference turned to the North Sea situation. Shipping-loss curves were still falling; replacements might exceed losses in the spring and reduce the submarine onslaught from major strategy to sporadic trade attack—but success had cost dispersion of principal naval forces. Battle fleet still based at Scapa and Rosyth, auxiliaries at Harwich, and numerical strength great (reinforced 7 December 1917 by the United States 6th Battle Squadron under Rear-Admiral H. Rodman, flag Wyoming), yet strength was deceptive: mining expeditions, special destroyer operations, and escort of Dutch and Scandinavian trades were continuous duties of first-line forces—special operations often needing about fifty destroyers and auxiliaries; Dutch and Scandinavian escort absorbing battle-fleet detachments and about thirty destroyer units. Beatty informed the conference it was no longer desirable to provoke a fleet action even if opportunity occurred, for he could no longer be certain of meeting the German fleet on terms of equality. In a letter laid before the War Cabinet he stressed the enemy’s interior lines against Scandinavian mercantile traffic and the impossibility of covering convoy with the whole Grand Fleet while the High Seas Fleet could effect surprise attack. German battle cruisers were judged more formidable (believed six units including Mackensen—not completed before the war ended—Seydlitz, Moltke, Derfflinger, Hindenburg, Von der Tann); of nine British battle cruisers only Lion, Princess Royal and Tiger were fit for the line; destroyers would be outnumbered; new post-Jutland shell had not yet been supplied. Correct strategy was no longer to bring the enemy to action at any cost but to contain him in his bases until the situation became more favourable. The Admiralty unanimously endorsed this and determined to continue Bight minelaying as obstacle to High Seas Fleet free movement even though the Heligoland quadrant had not yet closed the Bight to U-boats.

Southern activity continued: Dutch traffic was assembled in the Black Deep with routes communicated only after sailing began; Tyrwhitt arranged minesweepers ahead of the convoy outside local swept areas. German destroyers raided Yarmouth on 14 January (shelling from about 11.15; Tyrwhitt to sea an hour and a half later—enemy already retired). Erebus bombarded Ostend on 19 January; on 23 January enemy destroyers attacked drifter flagship Clover Bank (Lieutenant D. L. Webster, R.N.R.) near Thornton Ridge while Erebus, M 26 and destroyers exercised nearby. When Keyes took Dover command the Channel minefield ran across the Straits; night patrols concentrated upon it—a 12- or 15-inch monitor, four thirty-knot destroyers or P-boats, fourteen trawlers, sixty drifters, four motor launches and two paddle minesweepers; monitor near north-eastern Varne; trawlers with flares for illumination. Results at first disappointing (thirty-five to forty unmolested passages in January), yet four German submarines were lost in the Dover Straits between 26 January and 8 February, plus one on 19 December—five since the new system versus only two accounted for in the Dover area in the previous two years. By the second week of February U-boats on the north-about route were increasing; German commanders reported the Straits exceedingly difficult, and a special flotilla of large destroyers in Germany was ordered against the barrage. After Clover Bank on 23 January Keyes expected a larger raid; the enemy were not then ready, and three weeks passed before the expected attack. The extract ends as Keyes had “not alte[red]” his arrangements.

Chapter VII — The Blocking of Zeebrugge, April 22–23, 1918

Whilst the High Seas Fleet searched for the Scandinavian convoy and the Grand Fleet swept the North Sea, a specially constituted force hurled itself at Belgian-coast defences in a desperate endeavour to block Ostend and Zeebrugge—last survivor of a family of adventurous projects. Late in 1916 Bayly at Queenstown had advocated combined operations against Borkum, Ostend and Zeebrugge; Admiralty and General Staff rejected the plan (supply across submarine-infested water to open enemy coast; military axiom against isolated beach lodgements). Bacon’s 1915 lock-gate smoke-screen attack and Tyrwhitt’s later blocking and mole-capture/Antwerp schemes met similar objections. When Keyes became Director of Plans, the First Sea Lord handed him the coastal-expedition dossier; on 3 December he submitted simultaneous blocking of Zeebrugge and Ostend by old cruisers under cover of darkness between 14–17 March (or morning twilight 18–19 March). Bacon’s alternative (18 December visit) added a mole assault: monitor Sir John Moore bows-on with a brow twelve feet wide and forty-eight feet long landing about 1,000 troops; General Craufurd alongside bombarding locks and forts with reduced 12-inch charges; block-ships under cover of the monitor attack. Admiralty decided the attack should be made with Bacon in charge; Keyes raised volunteers from the Grand Fleet (Beatty promised officers and men; no refusals). Keyes’s minute of 27 December listed six blocking cruisers to be selected from Sirius, Thetis, Brilliant, Vindictive, Intrepid, Hermione, Sappho and Iphigenia. Ordered to succeed Bacon at Dover from New Year’s Day, Keyes was left free to plan and execute as he thought best.

Keyes modified Bacon’s plan: lock gates ran back into concrete shelters at the first sign of danger, so bombardment would not serve; a monitor reduced to four knots by false bows could not be held bows-on in a three-knot current; one large brow risked the whole expedition to a single shell. He retained a diversionary mole assault to capture end-of-mole guns menacing the canal approach, plus damage to mole material and destruction of the shore viaduct. A special marine battalion was formed 8 January 1918. Storm-ship Vindictive was fitted with an 11-inch howitzer and two 7.5-inch howitzers, two large fixed flammenwerfer, foretop pom-poms and Lewis guns, retained 6-inch guns, Stokes mortars, false flush deck and fourteen hinged brows; Mersey ferryboats Iris and Daffodil (practically unsinkable, each able to carry 1,500 men, low freeboard requiring scaling ladders for a parapet nearly thirty feet above high water) were to be towed across the Flanders Bight. Block-ships: Thetis, Intrepid, Iphigenia for Zeebrugge; Sirius and Brilliant for Ostend—cement-loaded, bottom charges, masts removed. Submarines C 1 and C 3 were prepared with tons of explosive for the viaduct. Smoke screen was critical: Keyes abandoned flaming phosphorus pots and, through Wing-Commander F. A. Brock, adopted chlor-sulphonic acid (eighty-two tons required); only one English firm manufactured it, and production required temporary stoppage of saxin (synthetic sugar substitute)—War Cabinet suspended saxin so tea-drinkers using substitute drank unsweetened tea. German Belgian-coast armament comprised fifty-six heavy, medium and anti-aircraft batteries; Ostend and Zeebrugge sub-section guns covered approach routes—western Ostend batteries including Aachen (four 5.9-inch), Antwerpen (four 4.1-inch), Beseler (four 5.9-inch). The extract ends mid-catalogue of coastal batteries before the night of 22–23 April itself.

Chapter VIII — The Mediterranean, April to September 1918

The German assault on the Western Front opened 21 March; within days Mediterranean troop movements became heavy as Allenby was ordered to send home the 52nd Division, artillery of the 7th Indian Division, eight Yeomanry regiments formed into machine-gun units, and another British division once the 3rd Indian Division arrived. Allenby answered that the 52nd would embark early in April and that he would send the 74th at once. First echelon left Alexandria on 4 April in Kingstonian and Manitou; Kingstonian was torpedoed on 11 April and beached with great difficulty near south-western Sardinia (very few lives lost). The same day the bulk of the 52nd sailed under six Japanese destroyers; a waiting submarine’s attack failed and Japanese vigilance prevented another; first reinforcements landed Marseilles 13 and 17 April. On 15 April Commodore W. A. H. Kelly began Otranto barrage operations under Italian agreement that barrage forces be under a British officer (Acton at Taranto; Cusani-Visconti at Brindisi): six submarines as Cattaro outposts; destroyers on a Samana–Monopoli line by day and some twenty miles further south by night; hydrophone trawlers between Otranto and south of Cape Linguetta; main auxiliary patrol of drifters and trawlers south of them. Strength tables for May–September show large British and French destroyer and submarine forces, American submarine chasers, hydrophone trawlers, drifters and motor launches. Encounters remained brief and unsubstantiated. Further west, on 17 April Admiral Heathcoat Grant occupied successive Gibraltar patrol lines; on 21 April UB 71 was sunk by motor launch No. 413 (Lieutenant J. S. Bell) on her way to Pola.

French experts began laying their own net between Fano Island and the Otranto coast a week after barrage forces took station. On the night of 22 April—before new dispositions had operated a week—Austrian destroyers attacked: Jackal and Hornet on the western ten-mile beat sighted five hostile destroyers; Hornet suffered terrible punishment (salvo fires in shell-room and magazine, bridge hit, mast down, tiller jammed, sirens jammed on); Jackal pursued northward; Pridham-Wippell in Comet with Torrens, later joined by Cimeterre and Alarm, continued the stern chase past Cape Pali after midnight. Meanwhile German recognition of Ukrainian independence and rapid occupation of the country, entering the Crimea against Sevastopol on 19 April, raised the spectre of the Russian Black Sea Fleet falling into German hands. The Allied Naval Council in Paris on 26 April assessed force: even with Russian dreadnoughts Volya and Svobodnaya Rossiya (23,700 tons) added to four Austrian “Szent-István” (20,010), the enemy remained inferior to seven French dreadnoughts at Corfu plus four Italian; local weakness was the Anglo-French Ægean four (Agamemnon, Lord Nelson, Vérité, Patrie) versus possible Dardanelles sortie of two Russian dreadnoughts and three “Estafi” class. French readiness to send six battleships to Mudros was coupled with a claim that Corfu should then receive at least four Italian dreadnoughts trained on a uniform system with the French fleet. British and American representatives supported uniform signalling and fire control by the Scapa precedent; Admiral Thaon di Revel agreed the Ægean should be reinforced but saw no reason to redistribute Adriatic battle squadrons—contingencies too distant. The extract ends mid-Italian reply.

Chapter IX — Russia

The onerous task of supplying Russia with coal and other necessities had long strained Allied naval and mercantile resources; by 1918 the naval problem was no longer one of keeping open a friendly ally’s ports but of dealing with revolutionary collapse, German eastward advance, and the fate of Imperial Russian naval forces. German recognition of Ukrainian independence and systematic occupation—ostensibly to protect the new republic against Bolshevik invasion—brought German forces into the Crimea by 19 April and against Sevastopol, raising the immediate prospect that the Black Sea Fleet would fall into German hands and force drastic revision of Mediterranean battle-squadron distribution (treated also in Chapter VIII). In northern waters the same revolutionary disintegration that had demoralised the Russian armies left British policy without the former object of sustaining an eastern front by seaborne supply.

Diplomatic and military-political developments intertwined with naval questions. Extracts record the principles put forward as those with which an acceptable peace must accord: (1) union by violence of territories conquered during the war will not be permitted and occupying troops shall be withdrawn; (2) peoples which have lost their political independence during the war shall have it fully restored; (3) national groups desiring political independence… (the extract breaks mid-enumeration). These formulations framed Allied discussion of Eastern Europe as Bulgaria’s collapse later opened the road to the Danube and Constantinople. Within the limits of the available extract, the chapter thus places Russian supply, Black Sea fleet risk, and peace principles as the naval-political setting for the final year rather than as a narrative of fleet actions in Russian waters.

Chapter X — After Zeebrugge: Mining Operations in the North Sea and U-boat Operations on the American Coast

After the Belgian-coast blocking attempt, British and American effort turned again to systematic mining and to the wider geography of U-boat war, including operations on the American coast. The Heligoland Bight mine quadrant, constantly renewed at the outer ends of German swept channels, remained a principal instrument even when it had not yet closed the Bight to submarines: it forced Germany to maintain a vast sweeper service, complicated High Seas Fleet sorties, and supported the containing strategy endorsed in January. Parallel to Bight work, British and American navies prepared and laid the Northern Barrage across the northern exit of the North Sea—an undertaking in which American material credit was heavily involved. Early weaknesses were discovered almost as soon as fields were laid: American mines proved unreliable in large sections that would have to be relaid or reinforced; even in their faulty state the fields were dangerous, and in September alone five submarines were destroyed in the mines as well as three more by other means in the North Sea.

Review of results from January to May underlined the transformation wrought by Keyes’s intensive patrol on the Dover deep minefields. Despite sea hazards, that patrol had given better continuous results against German submarines than any other operation or complex of operations: ten German submarines destroyed in the Straits since the beginning of the year—an extraordinary contrast with the previous year. Named Home-waters losses in the extract include U 103 (rammed by H.M.S. Olympic in the Western Channel), U 154 (sunk by submarine E 35 in the Atlantic), UB 16 (E 34, southern North Sea), UB 31 (Dover minefield), UB 72 (D 4, Channel), UB 74 (depth charge from yacht Lorna, Lyme Bay), UB 78 (rammed by transport Queen Alexandra, Channel), UC 49 (bombed by aeroplanes, trawlers and destroyers, North Sea), UC 75 (rammed by H.M.S. Fairy, North Sea), UC 78 (Dover minefield), and UB 119 (lost, causes and position doubtful). In consequence all larger German submarines were ordered to use the north-about route, spending about six unproductive days of each cruise; part of the Flanders flotilla was later recalled because Dover dangers were so great, and smaller UB- and UC-boats shifted to a less fruitful east-coast of England zone. No other plan of counter-attack, Newbolt concludes from the extract’s closing argument, had given comparable results: continuous harassing attack had compelled redistribution of flotillas, practical abandonment of an entire zone of operations, and substitution of another not nearly so fruitful—the plan of operations conceived in the winter…

Chapter XI — The End of Hostilities in the Mediterranean

The Bulgarian armistice was compounded and signed at Franchet d’Esperey’s Macedonian headquarters on 30 September; General Milne’s circular fixed cessation at midday, and on 2 October London confirmed Bulgarian demobilisation, opening of ports, and railway availability for Allied transport—freeing Entente armies to move north to the Danube and east to Constantinople. Turkey was practically isolated at her moment of greatest difficulty while Ottoman armies still retired before Allenby and the capital was threatened. British Ministers ordered the Supreme War Council to assemble in Paris and instructed Admiralty and War Office to prepare a general Turkish armistice without delay. The First Sea Lord insisted that a naval armistice’s first condition should be Allied fleet entry into the Black Sea under a British admiral, in recognition of Great Britain’s share in the Dardanelles campaign and final Turkish downfall—complicated because the French Commander-in-Chief still commanded Allied Mediterranean forces and Vice-Admiral Amet, senior officer in the Ægean, had been sent there at British request.

False starts included Turkish envoys at Mitylene who proved mere agents of Rahmey Bey, Governor-General of the Smyrna vilayet, seeking Allied support for a local revolution; Lord Robert Cecil instructed that negotiations would only be with accredited representatives of the Turkish Government. On 7 October Allied Premiers substantially approved British-drafted terms but claimed collective Allied rights to occupy strategic points and control Ottoman railways and telegraphs. A conference decided the British Salonica Army under Milne should march east to the Maritza. Command friction continued: Admiralty asked Admiral de Bon that Calthorpe command the Allied Ægean squadron with Gauchet’s instructions on major operations; France opposed at Versailles; nothing was settled when Calthorpe reached Mudros on 11 October and interviewed Amet. Téméraire and Superb were ordered east and Lord Nelson from Malta to redress force inequality with the French 2nd Battle Squadron. On 20 October General Townshend arrived at Mitylene with a British officer and an aide-de-camp of the new Minister of Marine—set free by Izzet’s Cabinet (Talaat having resigned) to announce readiness for separate peace. Calthorpe was empowered to conclude an armistice. De Bon agreed that if an Allied fleet went up the Dardanelles it should be under British command and promised Gauchet would not go to Mudros if Britain objected. Late on 26 October Liverpool brought accredited envoys Raouf Bey (Minister of Marine), Reshad Hikmet Bey and Colonel Saadullah Bey to Mudros. Negotiations turned on occupation of the forts; Calthorpe obtained Admiralty authority to promise only French and British troops would occupy them, refused Amet’s claim to participate with reference back to Paris (Turks wished to treat only with Britain), modified Clauses 7 and 9 for popular acceptability in the capital, and when further reference to Constantinople threatened long delay set a time limit. The extract ends as he informed Raouf and Hikmet he had no objection to their communicating under that constraint.

Chapter XII — The Evacuation of Flanders and the Armistice

German postwar claims that 1919 U-boat output would have doubled and that the Northern Barrage was no real impediment take no account, Newbolt argues, of Allied plans to strengthen counter-attack: American submarine-chaser mass production; further development of defensive control of coastwise traffic by Lieutenant-Commander H. Rundle’s system of controlled groups moving only from protected anchorages under local escort when the route was clear; and American determination to remedy unreliable mines in the Northern Barrage—which even faulty had destroyed five submarines in September alone. Unbroken army successes then redirected operations. On 18 September British armies stormed outer Hindenburg defences; eight days later the Bulgarian front broke. As Flanders collapsed, Harwich Force under Tyrwhitt put to sea in great gales (29–30 September and again 1 October) to intercept German retirement; twenty-eight destroyers escaped between 29 September and the next few days, most along the Dutch coast in territorial waters; five were blown up as they could not sail; submarines had left earlier by detachments. How the Zeebrugge withdrawal of the night of 1–2 October slipped through was unknown at the time.

On 5 October Prince Max of Baden told the Reichstag he had asked President Wilson for immediate armistice on land, water and in the air. Allied Premiers at the Quai d’Orsay (6–7 October) feared the temper of the armies if fighting stopped and ordered Versailles military and naval representatives to prepare German and Austro-Hungarian armistice conditions on eight directing rules: total enemy evacuation of France, Belgium, Luxembourg and Italy; German retirement behind the Rhine; Alsace-Lorraine evacuated without Allied occupation; same for Trentino and Istria; Serbia and Montenegro evacuated… Wilson’s published reply of 8 October demanded acceptance of his addresses to Congress, withdrawal from Allied soil before armistice talks, and clarity whether the Chancellor spoke for the constituted authorities who had conducted the war. Lloyd George feared Germany was manœuvring to escape field defeat by simulated assent to the Fourteen Points—to which Britain could not agree beforehand as the basis of all peace negotiation, especially the vague “freedom of the seas.” Allies informed Wilson that mere evacuation was not sufficient guarantee and invited an American representative to Europe. Central Empires’ reply of 12 October accepted evacuation via mixed commissions and claimed the Chancellor spoke for government and people. Reporting to the Imperial War Cabinet, Lloyd George again stressed the dubious Fourteen Points; the prevailing view was that armistice conditions should approximate final peace terms. The First Sea Lord warned that a naval armistice so conceived must deal with the German fleet as an instrument of high policy, not merely as a combative force.
